Cotoletta alla Milanese

A thick, bone-in veal cutlet breaded and fried in clarified butter until deeply golden, Milan's proud answer to (and possible ancestor of) Vienna's famous schnitzel.

Milanese tradition insists on a properly thick cut of veal, bone still attached, breaded and fried gently in butter rather than oil, resulting in a cutlet that stays juicy within its crisp golden crust — a point of considerable civic pride against comparisons to Austrian schnitzel.

Ingredients

Equipment: Meat mallet, Shallow dishes for breading, Large heavy skillet

  • 4 bone-in veal chops, about 2.5cm thick
  • 2 eggs, beaten
  • 200 g (2 cups) fine breadcrumbs
  • 60 g (½ cup) grated Parmigiano-Reggiano (optional)
  • 150 g (⅔ cup) clarified butter or a mix of butter and oil
  • Salt, to taste
  • Lemon wedges, for serving

Method

  1. Gently pound the veal chops to an even thickness, keeping the bone intact.
  2. Set up a breading station: beaten eggs in one dish, breadcrumbs mixed with Parmigiano if using in another.
  3. Dip each chop in the egg, letting excess drip off, then press firmly into the breadcrumbs to coat evenly on both sides.
  4. Let the breaded chops rest for 15 minutes, which helps the coating adhere during frying.
  5. Warm the clarified butter in a large heavy skillet over medium heat.
  6. Fry the chops for about 3–4 minutes per side, until deeply golden and cooked through, adjusting heat as needed to avoid burning the butter.
  7. Drain briefly on paper towels, season with a little salt, and serve immediately with lemon wedges.

Chef Tips

  • Letting the breaded cutlets rest before frying genuinely helps the coating stay put rather than falling off in the pan.
  • Clarified butter allows for a higher cooking temperature without burning compared to regular butter.
  • A proper Milanese cutlet should be thick enough to stay juicy inside its crisp crust — avoid pounding it too thin.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Pounding the veal too thin, resulting in a dry cutlet by the time the coating turns golden.
  • Skipping the resting step after breading, causing the coating to flake off during frying.
  • Using plain butter at too high a heat, which burns and turns bitter rather than golden.

Cultural Note

Milan fiercely defends cotoletta alla Milanese as distinct from, and in local lore predating, Vienna's wiener schnitzel, with the bone-in cut and butter-frying method considered essential markers of authenticity.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Best eaten fresh; refrigerate leftovers for up to 2 days.

Reheating: Reheat in a hot oven to help re-crisp; avoid the microwave, which leaves the coating soggy.

Serve with: Lemon wedges, A simple arugula salad, Risotto alla Milanese
