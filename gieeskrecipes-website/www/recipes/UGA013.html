🫓 Chapati

Chapati

Dough rested twice, stretched until you can see your hand through it, rolled into a spiral and flattened again — the lamination is what makes a Ugandan chapati layered and soft rather than a thin Indian flatbread.

Chapati came to Uganda with the Indian labourers brought to build the railway, and it has become so completely Ugandan that it produced two dishes of its own — the rolex and kikomando. It is not the Indian bread any more: it is thicker, much oilier, made from plain white flour with no leavening, and it is laminated. The trick is stretching the dough paper-thin, rolling it into a spiral and then rolling that flat, so the thickness you end up with is made of layers rather than one slab.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large bowl, Rolling pin, Heavy frying pan or flat griddle, Pastry brush, Clean cloth

  • 520 g plain flour, plus more for dusting
  • 13 g coarse or sea salt
  • 30 ml (2 tbsp) neutral oil, for the dough
  • 250 ml water, plus up to 6 tbsp more as needed

For brushing and frying

  • 250 ml neutral oil

Method

  1. Mix the flour and salt in a bowl and make a well in the centre.
  2. Add the 2 tbsp of oil and the 250 ml of water, and bring it together.
  3. Knead for about 8 minutes to a smooth ball, adding up to 6 tbsp more water if it is dry.
  4. Cover and rest for 30 minutes.
  5. Divide into 8 pieces and rest a further 10 minutes.

The lamination

  1. Take one piece and stretch it out paper-thin — thin enough to see your hand through it.
  2. Brush it with oil, then roll it up into a rope and coil the rope into a spiral.
  3. Flatten the spiral and roll it out to a round about 23 cm across.
  4. Repeat with the rest, keeping them covered with a cloth.

Cooking

  1. Heat a heavy pan over medium heat with a spoonful of oil.
  2. Cook each chapati about 4 minutes in total, flipping and brushing with more oil between flips, swirling it round the pan so the oil spreads underneath.
  3. Stack them with kitchen paper between and serve warm.

Chef Tips

  • The two rests are the recipe. Thirty minutes then ten relaxes the gluten enough to stretch it paper-thin without tearing, and nothing else gets you there.
  • Stretch thin, then coil. The coil is what restores the thickness — so the finished bread is about a quarter of an inch thick and made of layers. A Ugandan chapati is not meant to be thin.
  • Plain flour, not self-raising. There is no chemical leavening in it at all; the lift comes from the layers.
  • It is much oilier than an Indian chapati and that is not an accident — 250 ml of oil for eight breads is what produces the street texture.
  • Two Ugandan production kitchens disagree on hydration by roughly 30 per cent, so if your dough feels off, adjust the water and do not assume you have gone wrong.
  • Ghee instead of oil for brushing is common and very good.
  • Keep them under a cloth as you go. A chapati that cools uncovered goes stiff.
  • It keeps 24 hours covered at room temperature and a month frozen.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Skipping the rests and then tearing the dough trying to stretch it.
  • Rolling it thin and cooking it — without the coil you have made a different bread.
  • Using self-raising flour.
  • Being mean with the oil.
  • Cooking it too hot, so it colours before the layers set.
  • Stacking them uncovered.

Health Benefits

  • A filling, cheap source of carbohydrate.
  • No dairy, no egg and no leavening agents.
  • The oil content is high by design — this is street food, not a diet bread.

Cultural Note

Chapati is the clearest case of a borrowed food becoming wholly Ugandan. It arrived with indentured Indian labourers on the Uganda Railway in the late nineteenth century, and rather than staying a foreign bread it became the base of two dishes nobody would call anything but Ugandan. Claim the rolex and kikomando without hesitation; the chapati itself is an inheritance.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Covered at room temperature, 24 hours. Freeze up to 1 month.

Reheating: A minute in a dry hot pan brings it back completely.

Serve with: Rolex, Kikomando, Ugandan tea
