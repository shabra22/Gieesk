🌴 Abak Atama

Abak atama — efere abak atama

Palm fruit boiled twenty-five minutes, pounded with light strikes so the nuts do not break, and a litre and a half of water squeezed through the pulp by hand.

Abak atama is the Akwa Ibom palm nut soup and the atama leaf is what distinguishes it — shredded very finely and added only once the soup has begun to thicken. The extraction is the work: a big derica cup of palm fruit boils about twenty-five minutes, then is pounded with deliberately light strikes so the nuts are separated from the flesh without being cracked, and a litre and a half of water is squeezed through the pulp by hand and sieved into the pot. Half a piece of uyayak — Aidan fruit — goes in with the pepper and crayfish.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large pot, Mortar and pestle, Sieve, Large bowl

  • 1 big derica cup palm fruit (eyop)
  • 1.5 litres water, for the extraction
  • 1 medium bunch atama, shredded very finely
  • 8 pieces beef
  • 8 pieces pomo
  • 1 cup periwinkle, in shell
  • 4 medium pieces crab
  • 1 stockfish head
  • 1 medium smoked fish
  • 1/2 cup smoked prawn
  • 1/2 piece uyayak (Aidan fruit)
  • 1 tbsp dry pepper
  • 2 crayfish seasoning tablets
  • 1 beef seasoning cube
  • Salt to taste

Method

  1. Boil the palm fruit about 25 minutes.
  2. Pound it to separate the flesh from the nut, using light strikes so you do not break the nuts.
  3. Pour 1.5 litres of water (about one and a half bottles) over the pounded palm fruit and squeeze the juice from the pulp by hand.
  4. Sieve the liquid into a pot.
  5. Season the beef and steam it until half cooked.
  6. Add the beef to the palm sauce and bring to the boil.
  7. Add the stockfish head, pomo, periwinkle, smoked prawn and smoked fish.
  8. Add the uyayak, dry pepper, crayfish seasoning, beef seasoning and salt.
  9. Cook until the soup begins to thicken, then add the finely shredded atama.
  10. Continue until the soup thickens to the consistency you want.
  11. Serve with pounded yam, rice or eba.

Chef Tips

  • Light strikes in the mortar. Cracking the nuts puts palm kernel into the soup, which is a different and wrong-tasting oil.
  • Atama goes in only once the soup has begun to thicken — not before. It is a strong leaf and it does not need long.
  • Shred the atama very finely. Whole or roughly torn, it dominates everything.
  • A litre and a half of water through one big derica cup of palm fruit. Squeeze by hand; a blender cannot do this job.
  • Half a piece of uyayak, which is Aidan fruit — the same spice the Delta banga recipes use under the same name.
  • Steam the beef only until half cooked before it goes in. It finishes in the palm sauce.
  • Periwinkle in the shell. Eating them out of the shell is part of the dish, and they keep their texture better.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Pounding hard enough to crack the nuts.
  • Adding the atama early.
  • Shredding the atama coarsely.
  • Cooking the beef through before it meets the palm sauce.
  • Trying to extract the cream in a blender.

Health Benefits

  • Palm fruit pulp is exceptionally rich in carotenoids and tocotrienols.
  • Periwinkle and crab are both very lean, high-iron proteins.
  • Very low in carbohydrate.
  • Atama is traditionally used as a digestive.

Cultural Note

Abak atama belongs to the same palm nut family as banga and ofe akwu, and the thing that makes it Akwa Ibom rather than Delta or Igbo is the atama leaf. Each community in the palm belt has taken the same base and claimed it with a different aromatic — banga spice and the oburunbebe stick in the Delta, scent leaf and ugu in Igboland, atama here.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 4 days refrigerated, 3 months frozen.

Reheating: Gentle heat, stirring.

Serve with: Iyan, Eba or garri, Boiled rice
