🦴 Molokoni

Molokoni — putuku, mulokoni

Cow hoof stewed for hours in water with salt, onions and maybe a little tomato, until the meat is falling off the bones and the gristle has turned the liquid into a rich binding sauce.

Molokoni is cow hoof soup, sold in kafunda joints across Kampala, and it is much-prized food that costs very little. It originates with both the Acholi of the north and the Itesot of the east, and it is known to many as nutritious comfort food. The ingredient list a vendor actually gives is short — water, salt, onions and maybe a bit of tomato — and the whole dish is hours of gentle stewing. The gelatinous gristle is what thickens it, and that is why it binds the way it does.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large heavy pot, or a slow cooker, Long spoon

  • Cow hoof, sawn into pieces
  • Water
  • Salt
  • Onions
  • Tomatoes — maybe a bit, per the vendor

No quantities are documented in any Ugandan source

    Method

    1. Have the cow hoof sawn into pieces.
    2. Wash it thoroughly.
    3. Put it in a large pot with water to cover, salt and the onions.
    4. Add a little chopped tomato if you want it.
    5. Bring it to the boil and then reduce to a gentle stew.
    6. Stew for hours, topping up the water as it reduces.

    The cue

    1. It is done when the meat is very tender and falling off the bones, and the liquid has thickened into a rich binding sauce.
    2. Serve hot.

    Chef Tips

    • Falling off the bones is the cue, and hours is the time. A crock pot or slow cooker overnight is the sensible modern route and the Ugandan sources say so themselves.
    • The gelatinous gristle is the thickener. Do not skim it off — it is what turns the water into a sauce and it is the whole character of the dish.
    • Keep topping the water up. Five hours of stewing will boil a pot dry and burnt hoof is unsalvageable.
    • Four ingredients is genuinely all a vendor uses: water, salt, onions and maybe a bit of tomatoes. Resist improving it.
    • One whole cow hoof yields five to six portions, which is worth knowing when you buy.
    • Some Ugandans believe it treats gout and joint pain. The author of the best Ugandan feature on it attributes the effect instead to the gelatinous gristle giving a rich binding sauce, which is an honest reading.
    • There is no quantified Ugandan molokoni recipe anywhere. The one Ugandan feature that prints quantities prints the Nigerian version, mokoto — a very different dish with dry sherry, Worcestershire sauce, dried ground shrimp and rice vinegar in it. I have not borrowed it and you should not mistake it for this.
    • Related dishes exist across the world and the Ugandan sources name them: Italian zampa buratta, Jewish petcha, Mexican beef soups.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Cooking it for two hours and expecting it to be tender.
    • Skimming off the gelatinous liquid.
    • Letting the pot boil dry.
    • Adding a long ingredient list.
    • Using a Nigerian mokoto recipe and calling it molokoni.

    Health Benefits

    • Long-stewed hoof gives collagen, glycine and proline.
    • High in protein with almost no carbohydrate.
    • Cooked in water with almost no added fat.
    • A cheap cut turned into a substantial meal, which is the point of it.

    Cultural Note

    A kafunda is a small neighbourhood bar-restaurant, and molokoni is what it serves — cheap, slow-cooked, and eaten by people who work. That two communities at opposite ends of the country both claim it, and that it has close relatives in Italy, Mexico and Jewish cooking, says something true about what people everywhere do with a cow's foot.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Refrigerate up to 4 days. It sets to a jelly when cold, which is a good sign.

    Reheating: Warm gently. It improves overnight.

    Serve with: Posho, Matooke, Chapati
