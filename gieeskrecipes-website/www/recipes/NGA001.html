🥣 Amala

Amala — elubo, okele

One cup of yam flour to two cups of water, with half a cup of hot water held back and poured into a well in the middle before it sits on low heat.

Amala is the dark swallow of the Yoruba southwest, eaten under gbegiri and ewedu as abula. Elubo — yam flour — goes into water that is already bubbling, which is the opposite of how starch is made in the Delta, where everything starts cold. The detail that makes this recipe worth having is the reserved half cup: you hold water back, make a well in the centre of the mass, pour it in, and leave it alone. Lumps during the process are expected and are turned out at the end rather than prevented at the start.

Ingredients

Equipment: Heavy pot, Turning stick (omorogun), Carving spoon

  • 2 cups water
  • 1 cup yam flour (elubo)
  • 1/2 cup hot water, held back for the well

Method

  1. Heat the 2 cups of water until bubbling hot.
  2. Remove some of the water and set it aside — this is the half cup you will pour back in later.
  3. Reduce the heat and pour the yam flour in while stirring with the turning stick.
  4. Keep stirring until the mixture comes together into a solid mass. Lumps at this stage are acceptable.
  5. Make a circle in the centre of the mass and pour the reserved hot water into the middle.
  6. Simmer 35 minutes on low heat.
  7. Begin stirring and turning again until it is sticky and smooth.
  8. Scoop onto a plate with a carving spoon, or wrap, and serve hot.

Chef Tips

  • The ratio is the thing: 1 cup of elubo to 2 cups of water. The Tribune prints it and a reader asking 9jafoodie for the ratio got the same answer back — 'one to two'.
  • The flour goes into hot water. That is the reverse of starch, where the starch, water and oil all go into a cold pan together.
  • Hold the half cup back before the flour goes in, not after. Once the mass has formed you cannot measure it out.
  • The 35-minute simmer is long, and it is worth knowing that the same newspaper printed five minutes in a second article. Thirty-five gives a softer, more elastic amala; five gives you something you will have to work much harder with the stick.
  • Do not try to prevent lumps at the start. They go out in the final turning, which is what the long low simmer is buying you.
  • If you are not eating it straight away, wrap it while hot — amala skins over fast.
  • White yam flour and the darker elubo behave the same way here. The colour comes from how the yam was dried, not from the method.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Pouring the flour into cold or warm water.
  • Adding all the water at once with no reserve.
  • Stirring through the simmer instead of leaving it covered and still.
  • Panicking at the lumps and adding more water.
  • Turning it out onto a cold plate and letting it skin.

Health Benefits

  • Yam flour has a lower glycaemic response than cassava-based swallows.
  • Naturally fat-free as cooked — nothing is added but water.
  • A source of potassium and resistant starch.
  • Gluten-free.

Cultural Note

Amala is Ibadan's calling card. The Tribune's second article on it puts the matter plainly: abula 'is common in Yorubaland, especially in Ibadan', and Tola Akerele's cookbook headnote says that if you want to impress someone from Ibadan, gbegiri and ewedu with amala is how. The buka — the roadside eating house — is where most people meet it.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Best eaten at once. It firms and dries within the hour.

Reheating: Wrap in damp cling film and microwave in 30-second bursts, or steam briefly.

Serve with: Gbegiri, Ewedu, Abula
