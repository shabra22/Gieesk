🍲 Classic Bean Stew

Bijanjalo

Onions caramelised and tomatoes cooked until they separate from the oil before the beans go anywhere near them, then simmered until the liquid halves and a creamy oily layer rises.

This is the Ugandan bean stew, and the whole thing rests on one instruction: caramelise the onions and cook the chopped tomatoes down until they separate from the oil. Everything after that is straightforward. The number of tomatoes and onions decides how the stew turns out, and at least one whole onion and two or three tomatoes is the floor. The longer it simmers the better it tastes, and the creamy layer on top is how you know you have got there.

Ingredients

Equipment: Pan with a lid, Wooden spoon

  • 720 ml (3 cups) beans, cooked and drained
  • 3 large tomatoes, chopped
  • 480 to 720 ml (2 to 3 cups) water
  • 1 medium green pepper, chopped
  • 1 large onion, chopped
  • 2 cloves garlic, crushed and chopped
  • 2.5 ml (1/2 tsp) grated ginger
  • 15 ml (1 tbsp) curry powder
  • 2.5 ml (1/2 tsp) cumin powder
  • 2.5 ml (1/2 tsp) ground coriander
  • 2.5 ml (1/2 tsp) black pepper
  • 30 ml (2 tbsp) cooking oil
  • Salt

Method

  1. Put the pan on medium heat, add the oil and let it heat up.
  2. Add the garlic and onions to the hot oil and stir well. Reduce the heat slightly so they can cook until translucent.
  3. Add the chopped tomatoes. Let them cook until soft and tender and separated from the oil.
  4. Add the green pepper, ginger, curry powder, cumin, coriander, black pepper and salt. Mix well.
  5. Add the beans to the tomatoes and mix well.
  6. Pour in about half a cup of water and mix. Raise the heat and let the beans cook until that water is almost gone.
  7. Add the remaining water and cook 15 minutes on high heat.
  8. Reduce the heat and let it simmer until the water has reduced by half and there is a creamy oily layer on top.
  9. Serve with your starch of choice.

Chef Tips

  • Caramelise the onions and cook the tomatoes until they separate from the oil. That is the key, and the same technique applies if you are using tomato paste.
  • The number of tomatoes and onions determines the outcome. Use at least one whole onion and two to three tomatoes, and do not economise here.
  • Tomatoes cooked properly add an umami depth. Half-cooked they just make it watery and sour.
  • The first half cup of water and letting it cook almost away is a separate step from the main liquid. It is how the beans take up the spice.
  • The creamy oily layer on top is the doneness cue. It only appears after a genuine simmer.
  • The longer it simmers, the better it tastes. This is not a dish to rush.
  • Add water depending on the consistency you want — soupy or thick are both legitimate.
  • This is the stew that goes over chopped chapati to make kikomando.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Adding the beans before the tomatoes have separated from the oil.
  • Using one tomato and calling it enough.
  • Skipping the first small addition of water.
  • Taking it off before the creamy layer forms.
  • Using undercooked beans.

Health Benefits

  • Beans are high in plant protein, fibre, folate, iron and magnesium.
  • Two tablespoons of oil across six servings.
  • Tomatoes cooked in oil release more absorbable lycopene than raw ones.
  • Entirely plant-based and inexpensive.

Cultural Note

Beans are what Ugandans actually eat most days, and this is the stew. Its author also records a real piece of agricultural history alongside it: before chemical preservatives there were beans for food and better-quality beans kept for seed, and the seed was preserved by wrapping it in dry banana leaves and nearly drowning it in ash, which kept the weevils out.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Refrigerate up to 4 days. Freezes for 3 months.

Reheating: Warm with a splash of water.

Serve with: Chapati, Posho, Matooke
