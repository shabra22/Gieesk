🥄 Ewedu with Kaun

Ewedu

The version that does use potash — a teaspoon boiled in the water before the leaves go in, then mashed with the ijabe and simmered one to two minutes.

This is the counter-version to the blender-and-no-kaun ewedu, and it is the one the newspapers publish. A teaspoon of potash goes into boiling water first, the rinsed ewedu follows, and once the leaves soften they are mashed with the traditional mashing broom or blended if you have no ijabe. Then back to the pot with a stock cube and salt and a one-to-two-minute simmer. The alkali does two things — it preserves the green and it helps the leaf break down to the slippery consistency ewedu is wanted for — and the named food bloggers largely refuse it.

Ingredients

Equipment: Medium pot, Ijabe (ewedu broom), or a blender, Colander

  • 2 cups ewedu leaves
  • Water, to boil
  • 1 tsp potash (kaun), or baking soda
  • 1 tbsp locust beans (iru)
  • Seasoning and salt, to taste

Method

  1. Rinse the ewedu leaves thoroughly.
  2. Boil water with the potash for a few minutes.
  3. Add the rinsed ewedu to the boiling potash water.
  4. Once the leaves soften, mash them with the traditional mashing broom — or blend them if you have no ijabe.
  5. Return to the pot, add the locust beans, the stock cube and salt.
  6. Simmer 1 to 2 minutes and serve.

Chef Tips

  • The potash goes into the water BEFORE the leaves, and boils for a few minutes first. That is the sequence and it differs from adding alkali at the end.
  • A teaspoon is the quantified figure. The same newspaper's other article says only 'a tiny amount', so a teaspoon is the number to work from.
  • If you are using baking soda rather than potash, add it near the END of cooking rather than the start — the two alkalis are not interchangeable in their timing.
  • One to two minutes at the end and no more. This is a much shorter cook than the no-kaun version, because the alkali has done the softening.
  • Kaun keeps the green vivid. That is its visible contribution and it is why the newspaper recipes use it.
  • For the version that refuses potash entirely and gets its viscosity from blending, see Ewedu. Both are legitimate and the named bloggers are on the other side of this question.
  • Read Kaun before using kaun. It is not chemically potash, and it carries a specific safety warning that matters in pregnancy.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Adding the potash with the leaves rather than boiling it in the water first.
  • Using baking soda at the start, where potash goes.
  • More than a teaspoon, which tastes soapy.
  • Over-boiling after the mash.
  • Using kaun without reading the safety note.

Health Benefits

  • Jute leaf is high in vitamins A, C and E, calcium and iron.
  • Very low in calories and fat.
  • The mucilage is a soluble fibre.
  • Cooked briefly, which preserves more of the vitamin C.

Cultural Note

That the newspapers use kaun and the named food bloggers largely refuse it is a real divide in Yoruba home cooking rather than a matter of accuracy. The alkali is the older practice and it does something a blender cannot quite replicate; the refusal is a modern, health-conscious position. Ibadan buka cooks mostly use it.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 3 days refrigerated.

Reheating: Gentle heat with a splash of water. Do not boil.

Serve with: Amala, Gbegiri, Abula

  • ON WHAT THE ALKALI ACTUALLY DOES: 'Understanding Nigerian Condiments - Kaun (Akanwa)', on Funke Koleosho's blog with the post byline 'tasteofnigeria', lists ewedu under 'vegetable enhancement' but does NOT explain the mechanism. The explanation offered above — that it preserves the green and helps the leaf break down — is the standard account of what an alkali does to a leafy vegetable and is NOT asserted as sourced.
