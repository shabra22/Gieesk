🥣 Motoho wa Ting

Motoho — ting ya mabele — motogo wa ting

Sorghum meal and water left in a sealed bucket for two days until it sours, then stirred into boiling water and simmered twenty minutes — the fermented porridge of the Free State, North West and Lesotho, eaten with brown sugar or with meat.

This is sour porridge, and the sourness is the dish. Sorghum meal and water are left to ferment into motlhodi, which is both the flavour and the starter for the next batch, and the cooked porridge is called motoho in Sesotho and ting in Setswana. Thick, it is a breakfast eaten with brown sugar; thinned right down it is a drink given to children. The fermentation also does something useful — it makes the grain's iron and zinc considerably easier to absorb, which is why this has fed people for a very long time.

Ingredients

Equipment: 5-litre bucket with a lid, 3-litre pot, Whisk or wooden spoon

    The motlhodi (fermented base), 48 hours ahead

    • 4 cups mabele meal (coarse sorghum meal)
    • 6 cups water

    The porridge

    • 2 cups water
    • 5 cups motlhodi

    To serve

    • Brown sugar

    Method

    1. Combine the mabele meal and the 6 cups of water in a large lidded bucket. Seal it and leave it undisturbed in a warm place for 48 hours.

    Cooking

    1. Bring the 2 cups of water to the boil in a 3-litre pot over medium-high heat, then reduce the heat to low.
    2. Gradually add the 5 cups of motlhodi, stirring continuously so it does not lump.
    3. Keep stirring until it thickens.
    4. Cover and simmer for 20 minutes.
    5. Serve in bowls topped with brown sugar for breakfast, or without sugar alongside a main meal.

    Chef Tips

    • Warm place, sealed bucket, 48 hours, undisturbed. A cold kitchen will stall it and you will get a flat porridge with no sourness.
    • For a thicker porridge use 4 cups of motlhodi to the same 2 cups of water and cook 5 minutes longer.
    • Keep a cupful of the motlhodi back as a starter for the next batch. With a starter going, the ferment drops to about 24 hours.
    • Coarse meal, not fine. Ground too fine, the porridge goes pasty and loses the grain in it.
    • Fermentation time is the one thing every source disagrees about — 24 hours with a strong starter, 48 hours from scratch, and up to 5 days if you want it properly sharp. Taste the motlhodi and decide for yourself.
    • Stir it in gradually and never stop stirring. Lumps in sour porridge do not dissolve later.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Fermenting it somewhere cold.
    • Pouring the motlhodi in all at once, which sets lumps instantly.
    • Salting the cooking water, which some sources specifically warn against.
    • Using fine maize meal instead of coarse sorghum, which gives an entirely different and much blander dish.
    • Cooking it for five minutes and expecting the grain to soften.

    Health Benefits

    • Fermentation breaks down phytates, which makes the sorghum's iron and zinc far more available.
    • Naturally gluten-free and high in fibre.
    • The lactic acid bacteria make this a genuinely probiotic food.

    Cultural Note

    Ting ya mabele is on Slow Food's Ark of Taste, which tracks foods at risk of being lost. The distinction between bogobe, cooked thick with little water, and motogo, thinned right down, is real and matters to the people who eat it — the same fermented base becomes a meal or a drink depending on how much water goes in.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: The cooked porridge keeps 3 days refrigerated. The motlhodi keeps going indefinitely if you feed it meal and water.

    Reheating: Warm with extra water — it sets very firm in the fridge.

    Serve with: Brown sugar and butter, Seswaa, Morogo wa Dinawa
