🍚 Posho

Posho — Kawunga — Obundu — Akaalo

Maize flour beaten into boiling water and mingled hard for fifteen to twenty minutes with a wooden stick until it stops tasting of raw flour, then left to firm in its own steam.

Posho is the maize staple, and the word you use for it depends on where you are — posho and kawunga in the centre, obundu and akaalo elsewhere, and none of them is the Swahili ugali, which is a Kenyan and Tanzanian word. It is flour and water and nothing else, and the whole craft is in the mingling: a stiff, continuous beating with a wooden stick that breaks the lumps and cooks the starch. Ugandans eat it softer than Kenyans and harder than Zambians, which is a real regional preference rather than a matter of skill.

Ingredients

Equipment: Heavy-based pan, Wooden mingling stick, or a sturdy wooden spoon, A lid

  • 1 litre water
  • 500 g maize flour, white or yellow

That is all

    Method

    1. Bring the water to a full boil. It must be at boiling point before any flour goes in — this speeds the whole process up.
    2. Add the flour gradually to the boiling water, beating vigorously and continuously so no lumps form.
    3. Keep mingling as it thickens. It will get very stiff and you will need to lean into it.
    4. Mingle for 15 to 20 minutes in total. You can stop occasionally, cover the pan and let the steam inside do some of the work, then carry on.
    5. Taste it. When it no longer tastes raw and flourly, it is ready.
    6. Cover the pan, reduce the heat right down, and let it finish in its own steam for a few minutes.
    7. Turn it out and serve with a rich stew or sauce.

    Chef Tips

    • Boiling water first, always. Flour into water that has not reached the boil is how you get lumps that never come out.
    • Fifteen to twenty minutes of mingling. This is the number people get wrong — five minutes gives you something that looks right and tastes of raw flour.
    • Taste for rawness rather than watching the clock. That is the only real doneness test.
    • The cut-and-fold motion is what breaks the lumps. Stirring in circles just moves them around.
    • If it comes out too hard, add about a cup of water to the pan, cover, let it come to a gentle boil, then uncover and carry on mingling.
    • Yellow and white maize flour behave the same; yellow tastes slightly different.
    • The ratio here is the conventional one rather than a published figure — the best Ugandan source on posho gives the technique in detail but no ingredient list at all, since it was filmed rather than written. Start at two parts water to one of flour by weight and adjust.
    • In Kenya it is preferred harder than in Uganda, and in Zambia and Zimbabwe softer, almost like a thick porridge.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Adding flour before the water boils.
    • Stopping the mingling too early.
    • Stirring rather than cutting and folding.
    • Adding all the flour at once.
    • Calling it ugali, which is the Kenyan and Tanzanian word for it.

    Health Benefits

    • A cheap, filling source of carbohydrate and energy.
    • Naturally gluten-free and fat-free as cooked.
    • Nutritionally plain on its own — what it is eaten with does the work.
    • Whole or less-refined maize flour adds fibre and B vitamins.

    Cultural Note

    Maize is not native to Africa and arrived through the Atlantic trade, so posho is a relatively recent staple sitting on top of much older millet and sorghum foods. Anyone who went through the East African boarding-school system knows it differently again — on the menu seven days a week, all year, which is why some Ugandans have complicated feelings about it.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days. It sets very firm, which is what you want for posho chips.

    Reheating: Steam or microwave briefly. Overnight posho is better fried than reheated plain.

    Serve with: Binyebwa, Malakwang, Eshabwe
