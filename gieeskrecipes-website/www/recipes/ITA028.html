🍝 Fettuccine Alfredo (Italian-Inspired)

Fettuccine al Burro

This Italian-rooted Fettuccine Alfredo stays true to its Roman origins — just butter, Parmigiano-Reggiano, and pasta water emulsified into a silky sauce, without the cream added in most versions abroad.

Invented in Rome by Alfredo di Lelio in the early 1900s, the original dish was simply 'fettuccine al triplo burro' — tripled butter — tossed tableside with Parmigiano until glossy. The heavy cream common in international versions is a later, non-Italian addition.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large pot, Large skillet or wide pan, Tongs

  • 400 g (14 oz) fresh fettuccine (or dried, if unavailable)
  • 115 g (½ cup) unsalted butter, cut into pieces, at room temperature
  • 100 g (1¼ cups) finely grated Parmigiano-Reggiano, plus more for serving
  • Fine sea salt, to taste
  • Freshly ground black pepper, to taste

Method

  1. Bring a large pot of salted water to a boil and cook the fettuccine until al dente (fresh pasta cooks in 1–3 minutes).
  2. Reserve at least 1 cup of pasta water before draining.
  3. In a large, warm skillet, add half the butter and a splash of pasta water.
  4. Add the drained pasta to the skillet and toss over low heat.
  5. Gradually add the remaining butter and Parmigiano in stages, tossing continuously and adding pasta water as needed to build a smooth, glossy emulsion.
  6. Continue tossing until the sauce clings silkily to the pasta — this should take about 2 minutes of vigorous tossing.
  7. Season with salt and pepper, and serve immediately with extra Parmigiano.

Chef Tips

  • Room-temperature butter emulsifies far more smoothly than cold butter straight from the fridge.
  • Toss vigorously and continuously — the technique, not extra ingredients, is what creates the classic silky texture.
  • Fresh pasta gives the most authentically Roman result, but dried fettuccine works well too.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Adding heavy cream, which is not part of the traditional Roman recipe and changes the dish's character.
  • Using cold butter, which can cause the sauce to look greasy instead of emulsified.
  • Not reserving enough pasta water, leaving no way to loosen the sauce to the right consistency.

Cultural Note

Alfredo di Lelio created this dish for his wife after childbirth to help her regain her appetite and strength — its rich simplicity was meant to be comforting and restorative, long before it became an international restaurant staple.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Best eaten immediately — the butter-cheese emulsion does not reheat well.

Reheating: Not recommended; make fresh for the best texture.

Serve with: A simple green salad, Crusty bread, A crisp white wine
