🐟 Mallaay Qumbe

Mallaay Qumbe — Maraq Kalluun

Firm white fish poached in a coconut milk sauce built on red onion, tomato, eight cloves of garlic and four teaspoons of xawaash — the fish curry of the Somali coast, and the xawaash is what makes it Somali.

Coconut and fish is the signature of the whole Indian Ocean littoral, and versions of this dish are claimed by the Swahili coast and by Somalia both — the same plate, two histories. What settles it here is the spicing: four teaspoons of xawaash for a pound of fish is an assertive, unmistakably Somali hand, where the Swahili version leans on freshly ground coriander and cumin instead. The other thing to get right is that the fish poaches in the sauce rather than sitting on top of it.

Ingredients

Equipment: Pot with a lid, Sharp knife

    The fish

    • 450 g (1 lb) barramundi, or another firm white fish, cut into serving pieces
    • 1.25 ml (1/4 tsp) fine sea salt

    The sauce

    • 30 ml (2 tbsp) olive oil
    • 1 medium red onion, diced
    • 2 large Roma tomatoes, finely diced
    • 8 cloves garlic, minced
    • 1 can (400 ml) unsweetened coconut milk
    • 16 g (1 cup) cilantro leaves, finely chopped
    • 20 ml (4 tsp) xawaash
    • 5 ml (1 tsp) fine sea salt

    To serve

    • Steamed white rice
    • Extra cilantro

    Method

    1. Season the fish with the 1/4 tsp of salt and set it aside.
    2. Heat the oil in a pot over medium-high heat and sauté the onion for 8 to 10 minutes, until nearly translucent.
    3. Add the tomatoes, cover, and cook for 7 minutes, stirring and breaking them down as they soften.
    4. Add the garlic and cook for 1 to 2 minutes.
    5. Stir in the coconut milk, cilantro, xawaash and the remaining 1 tsp of salt. Cover and cook for 4 minutes.
    6. Add the fish, making sure the sauce covers it — add a splash of water if it does not. Cover and simmer gently for 8 to 10 minutes, until the fish flakes easily.
    7. Serve over rice with extra cilantro.

    Chef Tips

    • The fish must be submerged. It poaches in the sauce, which is why it comes out silky rather than dry, and a piece standing above the surface will be tough while the rest is perfect.
    • Eight cloves of garlic to a pound of fish looks like a lot and is correct.
    • Four teaspoons of xawaash is a heavy hand and it is what makes this Somali rather than generically coastal. Do not halve it.
    • Use a firm white fish. Barramundi, kingfish, snapper and cod all hold; tilapia and sole fall apart in the sauce.
    • Do not stir once the fish is in. Spoon sauce over it instead, or you will break it up.
    • Full-fat unsweetened coconut milk. Light coconut milk gives a thin sauce and sweetened is a disaster.
    • There is a second, entirely coconut-free lineage of Somali fish stew that uses tamarind with potatoes, carrots and eggplant and simmers much longer. It is a different dish, not a worse version of this one.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Leaving the fish sitting on top of the sauce instead of under it.
    • Stirring after the fish goes in.
    • Cutting the xawaash back.
    • Using a delicate fish that disintegrates.
    • Overcooking — the moment it flakes easily, it is done.

    Health Benefits

    • White fish is high in protein and low in saturated fat.
    • Coconut milk supplies medium-chain fats; it is also where most of the calories are.
    • Turmeric, cumin, coriander and cinnamon all come in with the xawaash.
    • Naturally free of gluten and dairy.

    Cultural Note

    Somalia has the longest coastline in mainland Africa and a fish-eating tradition much older than its reputation suggests. This dish sits in the current of centuries of Indian Ocean trade — the coconut, the spices and the technique all arrived by sea — and it is claimed by both Somali and Swahili kitchens, which is less a dispute than a description of how that coast has always worked.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Refrigerate up to 2 days.

    Reheating: Warm very gently. The fish toughens and the coconut can split over high heat.

    Serve with: Bariis Iskukaris, Steamed white rice, Basbaas
