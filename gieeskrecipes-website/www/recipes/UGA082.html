🫘 Bean and Mukene Sauce

Bijanjalo n'omukene

Beans soaked overnight and skinned, tomatoes blanched and peeled, and a cup of dried silverfish folded in for two minutes before the beans join them — plant protein and animal protein in one pan.

Bean and mukene sauce puts plant and animal protein in the same pot with a wide range of vitamins and minerals, and the source recommends it specifically for building the strength of people recovering from illness. The preparation is more careful than most: beans soaked overnight and then skinned, tomatoes blanched and peeled, and the mukene added for only two minutes before the beans. Both the skinning and the peeling are what make it gentle enough for its intended purpose.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large bowl, for soaking, Large pan, Second bowl, for the tomatoes

  • 500 g dry beans
  • 240 ml (1 cup) dried mukene
  • 5 large red tomatoes
  • 1 large onion, chopped
  • 30 ml (2 tbsp) Royco
  • 1 clove garlic, crushed
  • 15 ml (1 tbsp) cooking oil
  • Salt

Method

  1. Sort, wash and soak the beans overnight in plenty of water.
  2. The next day, remove and discard the bean skins.
  3. Boil the beans in salted water and drain when cooked.
  4. Meanwhile put the tomatoes in very hot water. When the skins have loosened, peel them and chop the tomatoes finely.
  5. Chop the onion, crush the garlic and wash the mukene.
  6. Heat the oil in a large pan and fry the onion until soft.
  7. Add the garlic and keep frying until golden brown.
  8. Add the tomatoes, reduce the heat and cook slowly in a covered pan until done.
  9. Mix the Royco with a little cold water and pour it into the tomato mixture. Cook, stirring, for two minutes.
  10. Add the mukene, reduce the heat and cook, stirring occasionally, for two minutes.
  11. Add the beans, mix well, then add a little water to make a thick sauce.
  12. Bring to the boil, reduce the heat, cover the pan and cook slowly a further five minutes.
  13. Serve hot with cooked or fresh vegetables and any staple.

Chef Tips

  • Skin the beans after soaking. It is fiddly and it is what makes this sauce smooth and easy to digest, which is the whole point of the dish.
  • Blanch and peel the tomatoes. Same reasoning — no skins anywhere in the finished sauce.
  • The mukene gets two minutes only, and it goes in before the beans. Longer and the little fish break up entirely.
  • Wash the mukene well. If you bought it at a local market, rub off the sand, wash it thoroughly and soak it in clean cold water for 30 minutes so the sand settles at the bottom.
  • The original's last paragraph tells you to blend the finished sauce in a blender or pass it through a sieve. There is no soup in this recipe and blending a bean-and-whole-dried-fish sauce contradicts everything above it — it reads as a paragraph transplanted from a different recipe in the same series. It is not reproduced in the steps, and it is flagged here rather than quietly dropped.
  • Mukene is omena in Kenya and dagaa in Tanzania, and it is eaten across central, eastern and northern Uganda.
  • The source recommends this particularly for people recovering from illness, which is why the texture work matters.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Skipping the bean-skinning.
  • Leaving the tomato skins on.
  • Cooking the mukene for as long as the beans.
  • Not washing the sand out of market mukene.
  • Following the transplanted blender instruction.

Health Benefits

  • Beans and dried fish together give complete protein from two sources.
  • Mukene eaten whole is exceptionally high in calcium, plus omega-3 and vitamin B12.
  • Skinning the beans makes them considerably easier to digest.
  • Contains fish, and the bouillon makes it high in sodium.

Cultural Note

Mukene went from being dismissed as poor man's food, and fed to livestock, to being sold in supermarket packs and exported — a genuine reputational turnaround in one generation. That a 2011 newspaper was already recommending it as convalescent food suggests the nutritionists were ahead of the snobbery.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days.

Reheating: Warm gently with a splash of water.
