🥜 Yaji

Yaji — suya spice, suya pepper

The only yaji with gram weights: 110 g of crushed kuli kuli to 75 g of ginger powder, sieved to remove the fibre, yielding 190 g.

Yaji is the spice that makes suya and it is built on kuli kuli — defatted groundnut cake, not peanut powder, and the difference matters because the oil is what would turn the blend into a paste. This version is the only one of several documented that gives gram weights alongside the spoon measures, which makes it the one you can actually reproduce: 110 g of crushed kuli kuli to 75 g of ginger powder, with 6 g of cayenne flakes, ten strands of African negro pepper, a 4 g stock cube and half a teaspoon of salt. It yields 190 g and keeps for years in the freezer.

Ingredients

Equipment: Mortar and pestle, or a plastic bag and a blunt object, Dry mill or spice grinder, Sieve, Flat tray

  • 5 tbsp crushed kuli kuli (110 g)
  • 5 tbsp ginger powder (75 g)
  • 2 tbsp cayenne pepper flakes (6 g)
  • 10 strands of African negro pepper
  • 1 small stock cube (4 g)
  • 1/2 tsp salt

Method

  1. Crush the kuli kuli with a mortar and pestle, or put it in a plastic bag and crush it with a blunt object.
  2. Crush the dry cayenne pepper in a mortar or any other rough container till the skin separates from the seeds, then pick out the flakes on a flat tray.
  3. Break the African negro pepper into pieces, and crush the stock cube.
  4. Combine all the ingredients in a bowl.
  5. Grind in small batches in the dry mill till they turn into powder.
  6. Sift the suya spice with a sieve to remove all the fibre from the African negro pepper.
  7. Store in plastic bags or containers in the freezer.

Chef Tips

  • Kuli kuli, not peanut powder. The groundnuts have had their oil pressed out, and that low oil content is what stops the blend caking — one named source calls it the secret to professional-quality suya spice.
  • Sieve at the end, without fail. African negro pepper is fibrous and the fibre is unpleasant in the mouth.
  • Separate the cayenne flakes from the seeds. The flakes carry the colour and most of the flavour; the seeds are mostly heat and grit.
  • Grind in small batches. A loaded mill heats the blend and the residual oil in the kuli kuli starts to release.
  • The freezer, and it keeps for years. Ginger powder in particular loses its punch at room temperature.
  • This blend has no cloves. That is worth knowing, because the academic description of yaji treats clove and black pepper as canonical — see the note in the sources.
  • Kuli kuli to ginger here is 110 g to 75 g. Other named blends run as high as 1:1 by volume. These are different spice philosophies, not variants of one ratio.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Using peanut or groundnut powder instead of kuli kuli.
  • Skipping the sieve.
  • Grinding everything in one go.
  • Storing it in a cupboard rather than the freezer.
  • Assuming any single blend is the authoritative one.

Health Benefits

  • Defatted groundnut cake is high in protein and low in fat.
  • Ginger has documented anti-inflammatory activity.
  • Capsaicin from the cayenne is associated with raised thermogenesis.
  • No oil added.

Cultural Note

Yaji is a closely held thing. One named source describes how traditional suya spice recipes were guarded secrets among northern vendors, and how her sister had to consult several Mai Suyas to assemble the ingredient list, which the author then refined by experiment. That no two published blends agree is not sloppiness — it is what happens when a trade secret gets written down by several different people.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Years in the freezer in a sealed bag, per the source. 6 months in a cupboard.

Reheating: Not applicable.

Serve with: Suya, Kilishi, Dambu Nama

This one: kuli kuli 110 g to ginger 75 g, no cloves, with a stock cube and African negro pepper. 9jafoodie's: 1/2 cup each kuli kuli and ginger powder — 1:1 by volume — plus chilli, paprika, onion and garlic powder, no cloves, no stock cube. Abi Olayiwola's: 100 g kuli kuli to just 2 tablespoons ginger, with 8 pieces grain of selim, no cloves. Her second version substitutes peanut butter powder for kuli kuli and IS the only blog blend containing cloves — and she flags it herself as a deviation for readers who cannot get kuli kuli. The kuli-kuli-to-ginger ratio therefore ranges from 1:1 by volume to roughly 100 g: 2 tbsp. These are three different spice philosophies.
  • THE ACADEMIC DESCRIPTION, and it disagrees with the blogs. 'Chemical Composition and Biological Effects of Yaji: A Popular Nigerian Composite Suya Sauce', Erhirhie Earnest Oghenesuvwe, Trends in Applied Sciences Research, 2019, 14(4), pp. 215–225, describes yaji as a composite spice of seven ingredients: 'table salt (sodium chloride), Maggi (monosodium glutamate, MSG), groundnut cake powder (Apios Americana), clove (Syzygium aromaticum), black pepper (Piper nigrium), red pepper (Capsicum annuum) and ginger (Zingiber officinale)'. The paper gives NO QUANTITIES — it identifies the seven components and never proportions any of them. But it does establish CLOVE AND BLACK PEPPER as canonical constituents, which three of the four blog blends omit entirely. Ginger is the only ingredient all five agree on.
  • Urban note from the source: Mai Suyas — suya vendors — in Nigerian cities typically source finished spice from northern suppliers rather than blending their own.
