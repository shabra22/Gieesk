🍌 Gonja

Gonja

Ripe sweet plantain peeled and turned over charcoal until it blisters and caramelises — with nothing on it at all, which is the rule.

Gonja is the sweet plantain, and it is one of the simplest things sold on a Ugandan street: a sigiri, a grill, and some gonja. It cannot be eaten raw and has to be cooked, and the one rule everybody agrees on is that it goes on the fire naked. No spices, no sugar, no salt — Ugandan writing on it makes the contrast with West African versions explicitly. Uganda grows more plantain than anywhere in the world, and this is the least complicated thing done with it.

Ingredients

Equipment: Charcoal grill, or a sigiri with a grill, Tongs

  • 4 ripe gonja (sweet plantains)

That is all. No spices, no oil, no salt.

    Method

    1. Let the charcoal burn down to steady embers.
    2. Peel the gonja.
    3. Lay them on the grill over the coals.
    4. Turn them regularly so they colour evenly and do not scorch on one side.
    5. They are ready when the outside has blistered and caramelised and the inside is soft right through.
    6. Eat hot, off the grill.

    Chef Tips

    • Nothing goes on it. Ugandan writing on gonja is explicit that it must be cooked au naturel, and it is the contrast with the heavily spiced West African treatments.
    • Ripeness decides everything. The skin goes green to yellow to very dark, and the darker it is the sweeter and softer the result.
    • Turn it often. Left alone it blackens on the underside while the top is still firm.
    • Charcoal, not a pan. Frying and boiling are both done in Uganda and both are fine, but the roadside version is charcoal and the smoke is part of it.
    • It also gets boiled or steamed, and fried — three standard Ugandan methods, none of them spiced.
    • No source gives a timing or a doneness cue beyond look and feel, so go by the blistering and the give.
    • It is sold at roadside stalls in pairs, and it needs no equipment beyond a stove and a grill — which is why it is such a common small trade.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Seasoning it.
    • Using an underripe plantain.
    • Leaving it on one side too long.
    • Eating it raw, which is not possible with this fruit.
    • Cooking it over flames rather than embers.

    Health Benefits

    • Plantain is high in potassium, vitamin B6 and vitamin C.
    • Roasted with no fat at all.
    • A good source of resistant starch and fibre.
    • Naturally gluten-free and low in sodium.

    Cultural Note

    Gonja is not tied to any particular community — it is eaten across Uganda, and it is the classic roadside trade because the barrier to entry is a stove and a grill. The packaging is worth noting too: it is handed to you wrapped in its own peel, which is one fewer piece of plastic on the street.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Eat it hot. It does not keep well.

    Reheating: Back over the coals briefly.

    Serve with: Muchomo, Pork Joint Pork, Ugandan tea
