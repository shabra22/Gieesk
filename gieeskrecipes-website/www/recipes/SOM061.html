Bun iyo Toor

Whole coffee beans, hull still on, boiled a minute and then fried slowly in oil for most of an hour with ghee added at the end — served floating in their own scented oil, with a sweet and salty mix alongside.

Bun is coffee eaten rather than drunk. The beans go into oil with their hulls on and fry for thirty-five to forty minutes, and what comes out is chewed whole. It is served swimming in the oil in a kurbin, a wooden bowl kept for the purpose, and the custom is to take a spoonful into the palm, eat the beans, and rub the perfumed oil into the face — that part is called foolbaxsi. Bun is offered first to the wadaads at prayer gatherings, made with supplications over it, and brought out at ceremonies asking for a safe birth.

Ingredients

Equipment: Small saucepan, Heavy frying pan, Slotted spoon, Wooden bowl for serving, if you have one

    For the bun

    • 25 g (1/4 cup) whole coffee beans, with the hull still on
    • 475 ml (2 cups) water, for boiling
    • 240 ml (1 cup) canola oil
    • 2 tbsp browned butter or ghee (optional, added near the end)

    For the toor

    • 1 cup corn nuts
    • 1 cup raisins, dark and yellow mixed
    • 40 g (1/4 cup) toasted almonds
    • 4 tbsp small hard sweets
    • 2 tbsp sugar
    • 60 ml (1/4 cup) oil, ideally the oil the bun was fried in

    Method

    1. Wash the coffee beans well.
    2. Boil them in the water for 1 minute, then drain and dry them thoroughly.
    3. Heat the cup of oil in a heavy pan over medium heat and add the beans.
    4. Fry for 35 to 40 minutes. This is slow on purpose — the hull softens and the bean becomes something you can chew.
    5. Add the browned butter or ghee in the last few minutes, for the flavour it gives.
    6. Serve the beans in their oil, in a wooden bowl if you have one.
    7. For the toor, let the frying oil cool, then mix the corn nuts, raisins, almonds and sugar with a quarter cup of it.
    8. Scatter the hard sweets over the top and serve the toor alongside the bun.

    Chef Tips

    • The hulls stay on. They are part of what you are eating, and a hulled bean fried this long is simply burnt.
    • The minute of boiling before frying is what starts softening the hull. Skip it and the beans never become chewable.
    • Forty minutes on medium heat, not twenty on high. Rushing it gives hard, bitter beans.
    • Mix the toor with the bun oil rather than fresh oil. That is where the aroma is, and it is the reason the two are served together.
    • Let the oil cool before it goes near the raisins, or they will cook and toughen.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Using ordinary roasted, hulled coffee beans, which are the wrong ingredient entirely.
    • Frying on high heat to save time, which burns the outside and leaves the bean hard.
    • Draining the oil away. The oil is served with the beans and used for the toor; it is not a cooking medium to discard.
    • Serving it as a drink. Bun is chewed, and the oil is rubbed on the face, not sipped.

    Health Benefits

    • Whole coffee beans contain the same antioxidants as brewed coffee, and rather more caffeine by volume — a small spoonful is the serving.
    • Almonds and raisins in the toor add fibre, iron and vitamin E.
    • Traditionally offered in small quantities, which is the sensible way to eat it.

    Cultural Note

    Bun sits at the centre of Somali religious and ceremonial life: served first to religious scholars at dhikr gatherings, prepared and eaten with supplications, and brought out at women's ceremonies praying for a safe childbirth. There is a Somali saying about it — oh bun, your secret is out, you are best with ghee. The fried-coffee rite is shared across the Horn, with close relatives in the Oromo buna qalaa, so it is regional rather than uniquely Somali.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Keeps a week at room temperature in its own oil, in a sealed jar.

    Reheating: Serve at room temperature, or warm the bowl gently.

    Serve with: Cambuulo, Popcorn, Sisin
