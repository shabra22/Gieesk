Omuramba — omugusha

Brown omugusha sorghum mixed with wood ash and soaked twenty-four hours in the dark until it blackens and sprouts, sun-dried, ground on a rubengo stone, then mixed with warm water and kept seven days in a cool room.

Omuramba is the sorghum drink the Bakiga make in preparation for the cold months of June and July, and it is what enturire is built on. The ash is believed to control the final taste — a different claim from enturire's tannin argument, and both are documented. The sprouted grain is called amamera, and the grinding stone a rubengo. Seven days in a cool room is the whole fermentation, and it is served at every community event, mostly to older men, in wooden cups.

Ingredients

Equipment: Soaking vessel, A dark place, Drying mat, Winnowing basket, Grinding stone — rubengo, Fermentation vessel, A cool room, Wooden cups, to serve

  • Brown sorghum, the omugusha variety, harvested and dried
  • Wood ashes
  • Clean water, for the 24-hour soak
  • Warm water, for the fermentation

Method

  1. Harvest and dry the brown omugusha sorghum.
  2. Mix it with wood ashes, which is believed to control the final taste.
  3. Soak it in clean water for 24 hours, in darkness, so it blackens and sprouts into amamera.
  4. Sun-dry the sprouted grain, then winnow it.
  5. Grind it to powder on a grinding stone, the rubengo.
  6. Mix the powder with warm water.
  7. Keep it seven days in a cool room before drinking.
  8. Serve in wooden cups.

Chef Tips

  • Twenty-four hours in the dark, and the grain blackens and sprouts. Both changes together tell you the soak is done — the sprouted grain has its own name, amamera.
  • Warm water for the fermentation, not hot and not cold. That is what the source specifies.
  • Seven days in a cool room. Only moderately low temperatures are documented, so a cool larder rather than a refrigerator.
  • The ash here is said to control the final taste. In enturire, the same ash is credited with removing tannins and improving flavour, aroma and colour. Both claims are documented and they are not incompatible.
  • Grind it properly to powder. The rubengo stone gives a finer result than a hand mill and the texture of the drink depends on it.
  • The Bakiga prepare it for the cold months of June and July and regard it as an energy drink and an immunity booster. That is their framing and it is worth passing on as theirs.
  • It is omuramba that enturire is built on, so making this first is the best way to understand Enturire.
  • No flour-to-water ratio is documented, and no temperature beyond moderately low.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Soaking it in the light.
  • Using hot water for the fermentation.
  • Cutting the seven days short.
  • Fermenting it somewhere warm.
  • Grinding it coarse.

Health Benefits

  • Sorghum is high in fibre, iron, magnesium and B vitamins.
  • Sprouting raises the available sugars and lowers the tannins.
  • Fermentation adds lactic acid bacteria and improves mineral absorption.
  • Regarded locally as an energy drink and an immunity booster, which is a community claim rather than a measured one.

Cultural Note

Brewing something specifically in preparation for June and July — Kigezi's cold months, high in the south-western hills — is the kind of seasonal logic that gets lost when a cuisine is written up as a list of dishes. That it is served mostly to older men is also worth recording plainly rather than smoothing over.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Drunk after its seven days. It keeps some days beyond that.

Reheating: Served at ambient temperature.

Serve with: Karo, Empengyere, Roasted groundnuts
