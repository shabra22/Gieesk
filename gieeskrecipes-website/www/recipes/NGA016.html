🫑 Ayamase

Ayamase — ofada stew, designer stew

Green peppers roasted whole at 200°C for forty-five minutes before blending, in bleached palm oil, fried until the oil floats to the top.

Ayamase is the green stew eaten with ofada rice, and the thing everyone argues about is the palm oil. It is heated alone in a dry pot until it loses its red colour — bleached — which is what allows the finished stew to read green rather than muddy brown. This version takes a distinctive route to the peppers: they go into a 200°C oven for about forty-five minutes on a foil tray before being blended coarsely, which concentrates them rather than drying them out in the pot. The endpoint for the stew itself is the oil floating back to the surface.

Ingredients

Equipment: Oven and foil tray, Blender, Heavy dry pot with a lid, Extractor fan or open window

  • 6 green bell peppers (green tatashe)
  • 3-4 scotch bonnet (ata rodo)
  • 1 big red onion, chopped
  • Palm oil — see the note on quantity
  • 1 cup chicken or beef stock (optional)
  • 4 cups boiled assorted meats, cut into bite-size chunks
  • 2 heaped tbsp locust beans (iru)
  • 1 cup shredded boiled stockfish (panla)
  • 2 tbsp dried crayfish
  • 2 bouillon cubes
  • Boiled eggs — see the note
  • Salt to taste

Method

  1. Roast the green peppers and scotch bonnets on a foil tray at 200°C for about 45 minutes, then blend coarsely.
  2. In a well-ventilated kitchen, heat the palm oil alone in a dry pot until it bleaches. Keep the lid on.
  3. Fry the boiled meats in the bleached oil until slightly crisp, and set them aside.
  4. Ensure approximately one cup of oil remains in the pot.
  5. Add the onions and fry until fragrant.
  6. Add the locust beans and fry until aromatic.
  7. Add the crayfish and the shredded stockfish and fry 2 to 3 minutes.
  8. Add the blended green peppers. Stir, and add the bouillon cubes and salt.
  9. Cover and fry until the oil floats to the top.
  10. Add the fried meats and the boiled eggs.
  11. Add stock if it is too thick, and cook 8 to 10 minutes further.

Chef Tips

  • Ventilate before you start. Bleaching palm oil produces heavy smoke and the breakdown products in it are irritants, not harmless. Open windows, run the extractor, and do not leave the pot unattended — overheated oil can catch fire.
  • The one cup of oil is what should REMAIN after frying the meat, not what you start with. The author deliberately declines to give a starting quantity because you fry in it first.
  • Roasting the peppers at 200°C for 45 minutes is this recipe's signature. Other versions blend them raw and then either boil the puree dry or drain it in a sieve.
  • Blend coarsely. Ayamase has texture; a smooth puree makes it a sauce.
  • The oil floating to the top is the cue, and every ayamase source consulted agrees on it even where they disagree on everything else.
  • Eggs are in the method but not the ingredient list on the original page. Four to six boiled eggs is the usual number across the other versions.
  • Bleaching times across named sources run from 5 to 20 minutes. Watch the oil, not the clock, and note that the shorter times are paired with a laxer endpoint — 'a lighter colour' rather than 'clear'.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Bleaching with the kitchen closed up.
  • Leaving the oil unattended while it bleaches.
  • Starting with one cup of oil instead of ending with it.
  • Blending the peppers smooth.
  • Adding ingredients to oil that is still fiercely hot, which spits.

Cultural Note

Ayamase is party food and restaurant food — 'such a hit at parties and gatherings', as its author puts it, and widely known as designer stew for the money and effort it takes. It belongs with ofada rice, the unpolished local rice named after a community in Ogun State, which one named source describes bluntly: it has a peculiar umami taste, and if you cook it at home it stinks to high heavens.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 1 week refrigerated, 3 months frozen. The oil content preserves it well.

Reheating: Gentle heat. It is already a fried stew and reheats better than most.

Serve with: Ofada rice, Eko, Boiled white rice

The 200°C/45-minute pepper roast, the fry-the-meat-first sequence, the one-cup residual oil, the 2-to-3 minutes on crayfish and stockfish and the 8-to-10-minute finish are hers.
  • AUTHOR'S OWN GAP: the palm oil quantity is deliberately not given. Her ingredient list reads 'Palm Oil (Not specified cos you'll be frying the meats with it as well)'. The one cup in the method is a residual-after-frying figure, not a starting quantity. That is recorded rather than filled.
  • LIST/METHOD MISMATCH, flagged: the method says 'Add fried meats and eggs' but eggs are not in the ingredient list, and no egg quantity appears anywhere on the page.
  • INTERNAL INCONSISTENCY on the page: the bleaching time is printed once as '15-10 minutes thereabouts' and once as 'I only ever bleach for 15-20 minutes'. Both are on the page; neither is corrected here.
  • DISAGREEMENT on bleaching, not reconciled. 9jafoodie: 5-10 minutes on medium, cue 'just until the oil changes to a lighter color'. All Nigerian Recipes: 'It took me 12 minutes on low to medium heat', cue 'it turns clear. It should look like vegetable oil when done'. Sisi Jemimah: 15-20 minutes. Lola Osinkolu: 10-12 minutes on medium, cue 'darkens slightly', lid closed throughout. Ajoke: 15-20 minutes on low, cue 'transparent but not black', with a white-paper test. Roughly a fourfold spread, and the endpoint descriptions are not describing the same state of the oil.
  • SAFETY, from the sources themselves. All Nigerian Recipes: 'Do so in a well ventilated area. Turn on your kitchen extractor'; 'Do not leave the pot unattended because the oil will catch fire if overheated'. 9jafoodie gives detailed smoke-management instructions. Sisi Jemimah references her own past kitchen fire and explicitly declines to advocate extreme bleaching. Lola Osinkolu: 'Open your windows and doors! Bleaching palm oil can be smoky.' No source consulted gives a temperature in degrees.
  • ON THE NAME: 9jafoodie settles the etymology — 'the Ofada sauce got it name from the local rice it is usually served with (Ofada rice), the sauce itself is called Ayamase.' All three of those sources treat ayamase and ofada stew as the same dish. Ajoke alone calls ayamase the 'fraternal twin' of ofada stew, green pepper against red.
