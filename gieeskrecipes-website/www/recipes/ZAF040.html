🥙 Rotis

Roti

Dough rested, buttered, dusted with cornflour, rolled into a coil and chilled to set the layers, then rolled thin and pan-fried — and clapped between your palms at the end to make it flake.

A Cape Muslim roti is a laminated bread, and the lamination is done with butter and cornflour rather than ghee: each disc is smeared, sprinkled, dusted, rolled into a sausage and coiled, then chilled so the layers hold when it is rolled out again. The last step is the one nobody writes down — you clap the hot roti between your palms, which shocks the layers apart and gives it the flakiness. Durban's roti is a separate tradition with its own technique.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large glass mixing bowl, Rolling pin, Clingfilm, Non-stick frying pan

  • 480 g flour
  • 2 tbsp self-raising flour
  • 1 tsp salt
  • 120 ml (1/2 cup) vegetable oil
  • 360 ml (1 1/2 cups) lukewarm water
  • 2 tbsp cornflour
  • 125 g salted butter, at room temperature

Method

  1. Sift the flour and salt into a large bowl, preferably glass.
  2. Add 1 tbsp of the oil to the lukewarm water, then mix it into the dry ingredients — with a spoon first, then floured hands — to a smooth, non-sticky dough. It is far better to start too sticky and add small amounts of flour than to end up with a dry dense dough, which cannot be fixed.
  3. Smear the top of the dough with a little oil, cover with an upturned bowl and rest it on the counter at least 20 minutes.
  4. Knead for at least 5 minutes, until smooth and elastic. If it is not elastic yet, leave it another 5 minutes and try again.
  5. Divide into 8 to 10 balls, smear each with a drop of oil and rest again for 10 minutes under clingfilm.
  6. Roll each ball into a circle. Smear with about 1 tbsp of the butter, sprinkle with a little oil and dust with cornflour.
  7. Roll each circle up like a Swiss roll, then twist the sausage into a rough coil. Do all of them, cover with clingfilm and chill at least 15 minutes.
  8. Roll each coil out into a thin circle and pan-fry in a non-stick pan over medium to high heat until golden on both sides, brushing sparingly with a mixture of oil and melted butter as you go.
  9. Clap each hot roti between your palms to fluff it up and separate the layers.

Chef Tips

  • Start the dough sticky. A dry dough is the one mistake you cannot correct later, and flour is easy to add.
  • The 15-minute chill after coiling is what sets the butter so the layers survive being rolled out. Skip it and the butter smears through and you get one solid bread.
  • Clapping them is a real technique, not a flourish. It is what makes them flake.
  • Brush sparingly during frying. Too much fat in the pan fries rather than toasts them.
  • The dough doubles well and the raw balls freeze. For garlic-and-herb rotis, work chopped coriander and finely chopped garlic into the dough.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Making the dough too dry from the start.
  • Skipping the chill, so the layers merge.
  • Rolling them out too thick after coiling.
  • Frying in a wet pan with too much butter, which gives a greasy roti rather than a flaky one.

Health Benefits

  • Provides slow-release carbohydrate.
  • Made with oil and butter rather than ghee, so it is lower in saturated fat than most laminated flatbreads.
  • No egg, so vegetarian throughout.

Cultural Note

Roti rolled around a curry is a salomie, which is the Cape's version of a wrap and predates every chain that sells one. The bread itself is one of the clearest things the Cape Muslim and Durban Indian kitchens share, and each has its own way of getting the layers.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Wrapped, 2 days at room temperature. Raw balls freeze for a month.

Reheating: Warm in a dry pan and clap again.

Serve with: Dhal Curry, Tripe and Trotter Curry, Any curry with gravy
