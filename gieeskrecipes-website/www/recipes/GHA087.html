🥬 Ademe Detsi

Ademe detsi — jute leaf soup

Leaves washed in brine and stirred into boiling water for two minutes to maintain the green colour, then stirred fifteen minutes until the mixture becomes firm.

Ademe is jute leaf — the same plant as the north's ayoyo and Nigeria's ewedu — and the Ewe make it into ademe detsi with crabs, smoked fish and fresh scallops. The two useful instructions are both about handling: wash the leaves in brine, and add them to boiling water stirring for two minutes to maintain the green colour. The sauce cooks about ten minutes, the seafood goes in, and then it is stirred with a wooden spoon for about fifteen minutes and gradually until the mixture becomes firm. No quantities are published, which is stated rather than filled in.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large pot, Wooden spoon, Bowl of brine, for washing the leaves

  • A handful of ademe leaves
  • Smoked fish
  • Crabs
  • Fresh scallops (adode)
  • Onions
  • Tomatoes
  • Garlic
  • Ginger
  • Celery seed (nkitinkiti) — see the note on this identification
  • Kpakpo shito
  • Palm oil (optional)
  • Salt, to taste

No quantities are published in the source. See the note.

    Method

    1. Wash the ademe leaves in brine.
    2. Add the leaves to boiling water and stir for 2 minutes, to maintain the green colour.
    3. Make the sauce with the onions, tomatoes, garlic, ginger, celery seed and kpakpo shito, and cook about 10 minutes.
    4. Add the meat, crabs, smoked fish and scallops.
    5. Stir with a wooden spoon for about 15 minutes.
    6. Stir gradually until the mixture becomes firm.
    7. Serve with hot akple, banku or kokonte.

    Chef Tips

    • Wash the leaves in BRINE, not plain water. It is the only source in this collection that specifies salted water for jute leaf and it helps hold the colour.
    • Two minutes' stirring in boiling water to maintain the green colour. Longer and ademe goes olive-grey.
    • Until the mixture becomes firm is the finishing cue, after about fifteen minutes of stirring. Jute soup thickens as the mucilage develops.
    • Fresh scallops — adode — alongside crabs and smoked fish is a distinctly coastal Ewe combination and not something you see in the northern ayoyo.
    • Palm oil is optional here, which is unusual for a Ghanaian soup and makes this a lighter dish than most.
    • The source names nkitinkiti as celery seed; another Ewe cook names the same word as aniseed. The two are not the same spice and no source reconciles them.
    • No quantities exist in the source at all — not even 'a handful' for anything but the leaves. Build it by eye.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Washing the leaves in plain water.
    • Boiling the leaves longer than two minutes.
    • Serving it thin, before it has firmed.
    • Adding palm oil heavily, which weighs it down.
    • Assuming nkitinkiti is aniseed when this source says celery seed.

    Health Benefits

    • Jute leaf is exceptionally high in vitamins A, C and E, calcium, iron and folate.
    • Crabs, scallops and smoked fish together give a very complete amino acid profile.
    • The mucilage is a soluble fibre.
    • Optional palm oil makes this one of the leanest Ghanaian soups.

    Cultural Note

    That the same jute leaf is ademe to the Ewe, ayoyo in the north and ewedu in Nigeria, and that all three traditions prize exactly the same slipperiness, is one of the clearest threads running across West African cooking. The Ewe version is the most seafood-heavy of the three, which is what you would expect from a coastal people.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: 2 days refrigerated. The mucilage degrades.

    Reheating: Gentle heat. Do not boil.

    Serve with: Akple, Banku, Kokonte
