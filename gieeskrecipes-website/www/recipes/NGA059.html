🫕 Nkwobi with Ngo Sauce

Nkwobi — ngo sauce

The best-documented emulsion in the collection: potash dissolved in a cup of the cow leg stock, added gradually to half a cup of palm oil, residue excluded, stirred until thick and not fluid.

This version adds three controls to the emulsion that no other source states, and together they are the difference between succeeding and splitting. The potash is dissolved in a cup of the cow leg stock rather than plain water. It is added gradually, and you are told explicitly not to add too much — the consistency must be thick, not fluid. And the undissolved particles that settle at the bottom are deliberately left behind. There is a fourth control too, a thermal one: the meat must have cooled before it goes in, not be piping hot. The author names the result Ngo sauce.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large pot or pressure cooker, Second pot for the emulsion, Blender, Wooden spatula, Cup, for the stock

  • 2 big cow feet, cut into small chunks
  • 3-4 pieces of medium stockfish (okporoko, panla), optional
  • 1/2 cup palm oil
  • 1 1/2 cups ugba (oil bean)
  • 1 levelled tbsp ground ehuru (calabash nutmeg seeds)
  • 2 tbsp pepper soup spice
  • 1 medium onion (optional)
  • 3-4 scotch bonnet (atarodo)
  • 1 tsp kaun / akawun / edible potash
  • 3 tbsp ground crayfish
  • 3 bouillon cubes, preferably chicken
  • Salt to taste

Garnish

  • Chopped utazi leaves
  • 2 medium red onions, cut into rings

Method

  1. Thoroughly clean, wash and boil the cow foot on medium to high heat with enough water, the whole onion if using, the bouillon cubes and salt. Boil till very soft, then discard the onion.
  2. Boil the stockfish separately with a bouillon cube and salt till tender — or cover with water in a microwaveable bowl and heat on high till tender. Shred into bite-size chunks and set aside.
  3. Dissolve the potash in a cup of the stock from the boiled cow leg, or in hot water. You will not dissolve all of it; some particles will settle at the bottom.
  4. Blend the scotch bonnet, crayfish and ground ehuru until smooth.
  5. If using ugba, put it in a small pot with water and salt, boil 3 minutes and set aside.
  6. Put another pot on medium heat. Add the palm oil, then add the potash mixture gradually — being careful not to add the particles or residue, and careful not to add too much of the mixture. The consistency needs to be thick and not fluid. Stir till it thickens considerably. This is the Ngo sauce.
  7. Add the boiled cow leg, making sure the meat has cooled down first and is not piping hot.
  8. Add the shredded stockfish.
  9. Add the blended pepper and crayfish mix and the pepper soup spice, and mix thoroughly.
  10. Strain the ugba and add it, then stir to combine thoroughly and cook 3 to 5 minutes.
  11. Serve immediately, garnished with onion rings and thinly sliced utazi leaves.

Chef Tips

  • Dissolve the potash in the cow leg stock, not water. It carries flavour into the emulsion rather than diluting it.
  • Gradually, and not too much. Those are the author's own two warnings and they are the ones that matter — the target is thick, not fluid.
  • Leave the residue at the bottom of the cup. Some potash simply will not dissolve and it should not go in.
  • Let the meat cool before it meets the emulsion. Piping hot meat can break the sauce, and this is the only source that says so.
  • Boil the ugba three minutes with salt first, then strain. It takes the raw edge off the ferment.
  • Blend the scotch bonnet, crayfish and ehuru together rather than adding them separately. It gives a smoother, better integrated sauce.
  • Garnish and serve at once. Ngo sauce is at its best in the first few minutes.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Adding all the potash mixture at once.
  • Letting the residue into the sauce.
  • Adding piping hot meat to the emulsion.
  • A fluid sauce rather than a thick one.
  • Adding raw ugba straight from the bag.

Health Benefits

  • Cow foot is exceptionally rich in collagen and gelatine.
  • Fermented ugba adds plant protein and B vitamins.
  • Very low in carbohydrate.
  • Ehuru and the pepper soup spices are traditionally used as digestives.

Cultural Note

Ngo, ncha, or no name at all — the sauce at the centre of nkwobi is the same in every account, and the fact that three careful cooks give it three treatments tells you it is the part of the dish that rewards attention. Everything else is boiling cow foot until it yields. The emulsion is where nkwobi is won or lost.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 3 days refrigerated. Best eaten immediately.

Reheating: Low heat, stirring gently as the emulsion loosens.

Serve with: Cold palm wine, Chilled stout, Abacha
