🌽 Kasoli nu Saak

Kasoli nu Saak — Kasoli ane Jugu nu Saak

Gram flour cooked in oil to pinkish-brown, ground peanuts stirred in for five minutes more, then yoghurt and spices off the heat and corn cobs returned in six hundred millilitres of their own cooking water.

Kasoli nu saak is the Indian-Ugandan dish in its clearest form, and the name says so: kasoli is Swahili for sweetcorn, saak is Gujarati for curry. A Swahili noun inside a Gujarati grammatical frame. This version is documented from the inside — an old Gujarati preparation of corn in chickpea flour and yoghurt, amended by the author's grandmother to include peanuts after her years living in Uganda. That is hybridity attested by the family it happened to, not inferred.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large pan, Grinder, Frying pan, Bowl

  • 875 g fresh or frozen corn cobs (about 4), halved
  • 100 g unsalted peanuts
  • 50 g gram flour (chickpea or besan flour)
  • 60 ml (4 tbsp) rapeseed oil
  • 250 ml Greek yoghurt
  • 7.5 ml (1 1/2 tsp) salt
  • 2.5 ml (1/2 tsp) turmeric
  • 7.5 ml (1 1/2 tsp) red chilli powder
  • 5 ml (1 tsp) sugar
  • 45 ml (3 tbsp) fresh coriander, finely chopped

Plus about 600 ml of the corn's own cooking water

    Method

    1. Boil salted water and add the corn — 6 to 8 minutes if frozen, 5 if fresh — then let it rest. Reserve the cooking water.
    2. Grind the peanuts finely.

    The roux

    1. Heat the oil, add the gram flour and stir continuously 4 to 5 minutes until pinkish-brown.
    2. Add the ground peanuts and stir another five minutes.

    Off the heat

    1. Remove from the heat and mix in the yoghurt, salt, turmeric, chilli powder and sugar.
    2. Add the sweetcorn and return to medium heat.
    3. Slowly add about 600 ml of the reserved cooking water while stirring.
    4. Cover and heat through 5 minutes, until the sauce is the consistency of double cream.
    5. Garnish with the coriander. Serve with rice, chapatis, or on its own.

    Chef Tips

    • Reserve the corn water. Six hundred millilitres of it goes back into the sauce, and that is where the sweetcorn flavour ends up.
    • Cook the gram flour to pinkish-brown, four to five minutes of continuous stirring. Under-cooked gram flour tastes raw and chalky and it is the commonest fault in this dish.
    • Add the yoghurt off the heat. On the heat it will split, every time.
    • Double cream is the consistency to aim for. Thinner and it slides off the corn; thicker and it claggs.
    • This is a rarity among Indian dishes in having no onions, no ginger and no garlic. Do not add them.
    • The peanuts are the Ugandan addition. The Gujarati original was corn in chickpea flour and a yoghurt sauce, and the peanuts came in after the author's grandmother's years in Uganda.
    • A second quantified version of the same dish builds it on tomato and onion instead of yoghurt — three-quarters of a cup of tomato puree, pureed onion and garlic, and a green chilli-ginger paste, with the peanuts powdered and 500 ml of water. That is a genuine divergence in the tradition rather than one version being wrong, and it is worth knowing both exist.
    • The fuller name is Kasoli ane Jugu nu Saak, jugu being the peanut element, which is useful if you are looking it up.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Throwing away the corn water.
    • Adding the yoghurt over heat.
    • Under-cooking the gram flour.
    • Adding onion, ginger or garlic.
    • Letting the sauce reduce past double cream.

    Health Benefits

    • Gram flour and peanuts together give a substantial amount of plant protein.
    • Yoghurt adds calcium, protein and live cultures.
    • Sweetcorn supplies fibre, folate and the carotenoids lutein and zeaxanthin.
    • Contains peanuts and dairy.

    Cultural Note

    The Gujarati trading community arrived with the Uganda Railway and its dukas were the commercial spine of the colonial economy. Idi Amin announced the expulsion on 4 August 1972 and extended it on 9 August, with a ninety-day ultimatum expiring in early November; the first evacuation flight landed at Stansted on 18 September. Estimates of those affected range from 55,000 to 80,000 and cannot be reconciled from the published sources. This dish went with them, which is why the recipe survives in Lincolnshire and the name survives in two languages.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days.

    Reheating: Warm gently. Do not boil, or the yoghurt will split.

    Serve with: Chapati, Rice, Potato Bhajia
