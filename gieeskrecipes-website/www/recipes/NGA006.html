🍛 Abula

Abula

Not a soup but an assembly: amala with gbegiri and ewedu ladled side by side and stew over the top, mixed on the plate rather than in the pot.

Abula is the Ibadan plate, and the reason it needs its own entry is that nothing about it happens in a pot. The three components are cooked separately and brought together at the moment of eating — amala in the middle, gbegiri and ewedu poured alongside so they meet without being stirred together, and a spoonful of buka stew over everything. The Tribune describes it simply as the combination of ewedu and gbegiri soups. What makes it a dish rather than three dishes is the order and the geometry.

Ingredients

Equipment: Wide shallow bowl, Two ladles, Serving spoon

    Per plate

    • 1 serving amala
    • 1 ladle gbegiri
    • 1 ladle ewedu
    • 2-3 tbsp obe ata or buka stew
    • Assorted meat from the gbegiri — shaki, ponmo, beef

    Equipment on the table

    • A wide shallow bowl, not a deep one

    Method

    1. Have all three components hot and ready at the same time. Nothing here is cooked to order.
    2. Turn the amala out into the middle of a wide shallow bowl.
    3. Ladle the gbegiri onto one side of it.
    4. Ladle the ewedu onto the other side, so the two soups meet at the edge of the amala without being stirred into each other.
    5. Spoon the stew over the top, and add the assorted meat.
    6. Mix on the plate as you eat, pinching off the amala and dragging it through whichever soup you want with each mouthful.

    Chef Tips

    • Do not pre-mix the gbegiri and ewedu in a pot. The whole character of abula is that the two stay distinguishable until the eater combines them.
    • A wide shallow bowl, not a deep one. You need surface area for the two soups to sit apart.
    • Timing is the only hard part. Amala skins over fast, so turn it out last, once both soups are already warm.
    • The stew goes on top, not underneath. It is the salt and heat of the plate; the two soups beneath it are mild on purpose.
    • Gbegiri thickens as it stands, ewedu thickens as it cools. Loosen both with a splash of water just before serving.
    • Eaten with the hand. Pinch, dent, dip, swallow — the amala is the vehicle, not the thing being tasted.
    • If you make only one of the two soups it is not abula. It is amala and gbegiri, or amala and ewedu, and Ibadan will tell you the difference.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Stirring the two soups together before serving.
    • Using a deep bowl.
    • Serving the amala first and letting it skin while the soups warm.
    • Leaving the stew out.
    • Letting either soup set too thick to pour.

    Cultural Note

    Abula belongs to Ibadan more than to Yorubaland generally, and the buka is its home. It is the plate by which an Ibadan cook is judged, which is why Tola Akerele frames her recipe as a way to impress someone from there. The name covers the combination, not any one element — and in a city of bukas the test is not whether the gbegiri is good but whether the three things arrive together at the right temperature.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Assemble to order. The components keep separately — see each.

    Reheating: Reheat the three components separately and plate fresh.

    Serve with: Amala, Gbegiri, Ewedu
