🍃 Ofe Onugbu

Ofe onugbu — bitterleaf soup

Ten to twelve cocoyams as the thickener and a handful and a half of bitterleaf washed without breaking the leaves into pieces — the long strands stay whole.

This is onugbu learned in Awka, and the point the author brings back from there is about the leaves: the long strands of bitter leaf should stay intact rather than being shredded. They are washed without breaking them up, and how much bitterness you leave in is explicitly a matter of taste rather than a target. Ten to twelve cocoyam pieces thicken it — considerably more than the three or four in ofe oha — and the yellow pepper goes in a minute before the end. It is one of the very few Igbo soups in the record with a stated yield.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large pot, Separate pot for the cocoyam, Mortar and pestle or blender, Colander

  • 6-8 pieces beef
  • 2 segments smoked fish
  • 1/2 cup stockfish pieces
  • 10 smoked prawns
  • 6 pieces pomo or kanda
  • 10-12 pieces cocoyam
  • 1 wrap ogiri
  • 1 1/2 handfuls bitterleaf
  • 2-3 tbsp crayfish
  • Yellow pepper, to taste
  • 3 cooking spoons palm oil
  • Seasoning cubes
  • Salt to taste

Method

  1. Wash the bitterleaf without breaking the leaves into tiny pieces. If the leaves are very bitter, some cooks use potash or hot water to cut it; market leaves sold pre-washed still need thorough rinsing. How much bitterness to leave is a matter of taste.
  2. Boil the cocoyam in its skin until soft, then peel and pound or blend smooth.
  3. Cook the beef, pomo or kanda and stockfish with the seasoning cubes and salt until tender.
  4. Add the smoked fish, smoked prawns and crayfish.
  5. Add the palm oil.
  6. Add the cocoyam paste and let it dissolve, then cook 5 minutes more.
  7. Add the ogiri.
  8. Add the washed bitterleaf and cook 3 to 5 minutes.
  9. Add the yellow pepper 1 minute before the end.
  10. Taste, adjust the salt and serve.

Chef Tips

  • Keep the leaves whole. Washing without breaking them is the Awka instruction and it changes how the soup eats — you get long strands rather than flecks.
  • Ten to twelve cocoyams is three times what ofe oha uses. Onugbu is meant to be thick.
  • Bitterness is a preference, not a target. Wash until it tastes right to you and stop there.
  • Pre-washed market bitterleaf still needs rinsing thoroughly. It is rarely as clean as it looks.
  • Five minutes after the cocoyam has dissolved, not five minutes from when it goes in. Wait for it to disappear first.
  • Yellow pepper at the very end, one minute. It is for aroma.
  • If the cocoyam is not enough to thicken it, ofor or achi can be added — but add them sparingly and off a hard boil.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Shredding the bitterleaf into small pieces.
  • Over-washing until there is no bitterness left at all.
  • Under-washing, which leaves it unpleasantly harsh.
  • Skimping on the cocoyam, which leaves it thin.
  • Adding the yellow pepper early.

Health Benefits

  • Bitter leaf is traditionally used for digestion, blood sugar and fever, and carries iron and vitamin C.
  • Cocoyam is a low-allergen starch high in potassium.
  • Very low in carbohydrate.
  • High in protein and collagen from pomo, kanda and stockfish.

Cultural Note

Onugbu is the Anambra soup, and the fact that a Nigerian food writer travelled to Awka for a wedding and came back with a note about not breaking the leaves tells you how specific the knowledge is. Bitterness here is not a flaw to be eliminated but a quantity to be set, and where a cook sets it is part of their signature.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 4 days refrigerated, 3 months frozen.

Reheating: Low heat with water to loosen.

Serve with: Akpu or fufu, Iyan, Eba or garri
