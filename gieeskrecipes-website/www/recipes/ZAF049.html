🐟 Smoorsnoek

Smoorsnoek

Onions cooked glossy with chilli and garlic, potato softened under a lid, tomato in, and smoked snoek flaked through at the very end with lemon and coriander — half an hour, and the fish does the seasoning.

Smoor means to braise or smother, and smoorsnoek is the West Coast's way with a fish that is too bony and too strong for polite treatment. Snoek is the working fish of that coast, sold fresh, salted as soutvis, or smoked — and smoked is what this dish wants, because the smoke is most of the flavour and the salt means you barely season the pot. It is a Cape dish shared between Afrikaans and Cape Muslim kitchens.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large frying pan with a lid, Tweezers or fingers, for the bones

  • 1 tbsp vegetable oil
  • 2 medium onions, finely sliced
  • 1 clove garlic, crushed
  • 1/4 tsp dried chilli flakes
  • 500 g potatoes, peeled and diced into 1 cm cubes — or sweet potatoes
  • 2 ripe tomatoes, seeded and diced
  • 250 g smoked snoek, skin and bones removed, shredded
  • Juice of 1 small lemon
  • A handful of fresh coriander, finely chopped
  • Salt and pepper, to taste

To serve

  • Extra lemon slices

Method

  1. Heat the oil and sauté the sliced onions until softened and glossy, then add the garlic and chilli flakes and fry for another minute.
  2. Add the diced potato, cover, and cook over low heat for about 15 minutes, until tender.
  3. Add the diced tomato and cook for 5 minutes more.
  4. Add the shredded snoek and heat it through for a few minutes — no longer, or it dries out.
  5. Gently stir in the lemon juice and the chopped coriander, and season to taste. Taste before you salt.
  6. Serve with extra lemon slices.

Chef Tips

  • Pick the bones out carefully and then check again. Snoek is notoriously bony and a smoorsnoek full of bones is a miserable thing.
  • Season only at the end. Smoked snoek is already salty and it is easy to over-salt the pot before the fish goes in.
  • The classic uses ordinary potato. Sweet potato, as some modern versions have it, makes a sweeter and softer dish — both work, and they are noticeably different.
  • The fish goes in last and barely cooks. It is already cooked; you are warming it.
  • Fresh snoek can be used if that is what you have, but you will need to season properly and you lose the smoke.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Salting early, which is easy to do and impossible to fix.
  • Adding the snoek with the potatoes, so it cooks for twenty minutes and goes to threads.
  • Rushing the bone-picking.
  • Cooking the tomato down to nothing — five minutes is enough to soften it, not to break it.

Cultural Note

Snoek is a class marker as much as a fish: it was the cheap, plentiful catch of the West Coast and it was eaten by the people who caught it. The same fish turns up salted as soutvis, wind-dried as bokkoms, smoked into pate, braaied with apricot jam, and smothered here — a fair demonstration of what a community does with the one thing it has plenty of.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Refrigerate up to 2 days.

Reheating: Warm gently in a pan. The fish toughens if you push it.

Serve with: Fresh bread and butter, White rice, A green salad
