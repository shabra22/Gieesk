🍩 Bofrot

Bofrot — togbei, puff-puff

The chewy yeast-risen version, where palm wine is traditional — it should at least double in size, so make sure your bowl is large enough.

The Ghanaian doughnut, called bofrot in Twi and togbei in Ga, which delightfully means goat's balls. The traditional riser is palm wine from the oil palm; this is a diaspora formula substituting yeast and a little dry white wine for it, and the author says so openly. The yeast is softened in warm water and sugar until it begins to bubble, a third of a beaten egg goes in, then bread flour, nutmeg, a pinch of baking powder and salt. It sits somewhere warm for at least two hours and should at least double in size. Fried at 375, the balls are done when they are quite dark — probably darker than you would think.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large bowl — big enough for the dough to double, Cloth, to cover, Deep fryer or large heavy pot, Soup spoon, Paper-towel-lined colander

  • 1/3 cup warm water — or 1/2 cup if you are not using wine
  • 1.5 oz sugar — 3 tbsp plus 1 tsp
  • 1.25 oz dry white wine or water — 3 tbsp if using wine. See the note on this figure
  • 1/3 egg — beat a large egg, pour it into a 1/4 cup measure and pour out a third
  • 1 tsp yeast
  • 4 oz bread flour, higher gluten than regular white flour — about 1/2 cup plus 1/3 cup unsifted
  • 1/4 tsp ground nutmeg
  • 1/8 tsp baking powder
  • Pinch of salt
  • Oil for deep frying

Traditionally: palm wine in place of the yeast and white wine.

    The oldest recipes call for currants; a more recent one calls for raisins.

      Method

      1. Measure the liquid into a bowl and stir in the sugar to dissolve.
      2. Sprinkle the yeast over to soften. Let it sit for a few minutes to allow the mixture to begin to bubble.
      3. Meanwhile measure the flour and have the nutmeg, baking powder and salt ready.
      4. Beat an egg and measure a third of it in a 1/4 cup measuring cup.
      5. Once the yeast begins to foam, add the egg and mix it with the liquids.
      6. Gradually sift and stir in the flour, adding the nutmeg, baking powder and pinch of salt.
      7. Mix all together well, cover the bowl with a cloth and allow it to sit in a warm place for at least 2 hours.
      8. It should at least double in size by then, so make sure your bowl is large enough to accommodate the dough.
      9. Heat the oil to 375 degrees in a deep fryer or large heavy pot, filling it no more than halfway.
      10. Line a colander with paper towels.
      11. Dip a soup spoon in the oil first, then scoop the batter and drop it into the oil as a ball.
      12. If the balls flatten out, there is likely too much liquid in your batter. If they sink to the bottom, your oil is not hot enough. If they brown on the outside but do not cook on the inside, your oil is too hot.
      13. Turn the balls as needed to cook evenly.
      14. When they are quite dark, probably darker than you would think they need to be, remove them to drain in the paper-towel-lined colander.
      15. Enjoy them warm or at room temperature.

      Chef Tips

      • At least double in size is the rise cue, and the two hours is a minimum rather than a target. Judge by the dough, not the clock, and use a bowl with room.
      • The yeast must visibly bubble and foam before the egg goes in. That is the second cue and it tells you the yeast is alive.
      • Three oil diagnostics in one recipe, all observable: flattening means too much liquid in the batter; sinking means the oil is too cool; browning outside while raw inside means too hot.
      • Quite dark, probably darker than you would think. Pale bofrot are raw in the middle — this is the most common failure.
      • Dip the spoon in the oil before the batter. The batter slides off a greased spoon in a ball instead of clinging.
      • Palm wine is the traditional riser and gives it a distinctive taste. Yeast plus a little dry white wine is this author's substitution for a kitchen with no palm wine, and she presents it as such.
      • This is a deliberately small batch and can be doubled, tripled or quadrupled. Make the small one the first time.
      • In Ghana experienced cooks scoop the batter in the hollow of the right hand and drop it in a perfect ball. The spoon is the beginner's method.

      Common Mistakes to Avoid

      • Pulling them out while still golden rather than quite dark.
      • A bowl too small for the dough to double.
      • Adding the egg before the yeast foams.
      • Dropping batter from a dry spoon.
      • Oil too cool, so they sink and soak.

      Health Benefits

      • Bread flour's higher gluten gives more protein than a cake flour.
      • Egg contributes complete protein and choline.

      Cultural Note

      Bofrot is sold from glass cases on Ghanaian street corners, eaten with hausa koko for breakfast, and handed out at every church gathering. That the Ga name means goat's balls is the kind of fact a cook remembers from Nungua in the 1970s and writes down, and the palm-wine original is a reminder that West African baking was fermenting dough long before commercial yeast arrived.

      Storage & Serving

      Storage: Best the day they are made. 2 days in an airtight container.

      Reheating: A hot oven briefly. The microwave makes them tough.

      Serve with: Sobolo, Hausa Koko, Bissap
