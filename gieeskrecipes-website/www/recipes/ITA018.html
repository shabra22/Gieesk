Risotto Bianco

This foundational Risotto Base — Risotto Bianco — is the master technique behind virtually every Italian risotto, built on the essential rhythm of toasting rice, deglazing with wine, and gradually adding warm stock while stirring.

Risotto's creaminess comes not from cream at all, but from the starch released as Arborio or Carnaroli rice is stirred and slowly hydrated, ladle by ladle, with hot stock — a technique that rewards patience and attention far more than any specific ingredient.

Ingredients

Equipment: Wide, heavy-bottomed pan, Ladle, Small saucepan for keeping stock warm, Wooden spoon

  • 1.2 L (5 cups) chicken or vegetable stock, kept at a low simmer
  • 2 tbsp olive oil
  • 40 g (3 tbsp) butter, divided
  • 1 small onion, very finely chopped
  • 320 g (1½ cups) Arborio or Carnaroli rice
  • 120 ml (½ cup) dry white wine
  • 60 g (¾ cup) grated Parmigiano-Reggiano
  • Salt, to taste

Method

  1. Keep the stock warm and gently simmering in a saucepan alongside your risotto pan throughout cooking.
  2. Heat the olive oil and half the butter in a wide, heavy-bottomed pan over medium heat. Add the onion and cook gently until soft and translucent, about 5 minutes, without browning.
  3. Add the rice and toast, stirring constantly, for 2 minutes until the edges turn translucent and the grains are coated in fat.
  4. Pour in the wine and stir until fully absorbed.
  5. Begin adding the warm stock one ladleful at a time, stirring frequently and waiting until each addition is mostly absorbed before adding the next.
  6. Continue this process for 18–20 minutes, adjusting heat to maintain a gentle simmer, until the rice is tender but still has a slight bite (al dente) and the mixture is creamy.
  7. Remove from heat and beat in the remaining butter and Parmigiano vigorously — this final step, called mantecatura, is what makes risotto glossy and creamy.
  8. Season with salt to taste, and let rest, covered, for 2 minutes before serving immediately.

Chef Tips

  • Keep the stock hot throughout — adding cold stock shocks the rice and stalls the cooking process.
  • Stir frequently but not constantly — enough to release starch, without turning the rice to mush.
  • The finished risotto should be "all'onda" — loose enough to slowly spread when the plate is tilted, never stiff or gluey.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Rinsing the rice before cooking — this washes away the starch essential to risotto's creaminess.
  • Adding all the stock at once instead of gradually, which prevents the rice from releasing starch properly.
  • Skipping the mantecatura (final butter and cheese beating) step, losing the signature glossy finish.

Health Benefits

  • A good source of easily digestible carbohydrates.
  • Can be made gluten-free and adapted to be dairy-free by omitting the butter and cheese finish.

Cultural Note

Risotto is the defining dish of Lombardy and the broader Po Valley, a region where rice cultivation has thrived since the Renaissance thanks to the area's extensive irrigation canals.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Best eaten immediately — risotto does not hold or reheat especially well, though leftovers make excellent arancini.

Reheating: Reheat gently with a splash of warm stock, stirring constantly, though texture will not fully match fresh risotto.

Serve with: Saffron for Risotto alla Milanese, Mushrooms, Seafood, Roasted vegetables
