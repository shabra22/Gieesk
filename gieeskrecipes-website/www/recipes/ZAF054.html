🌾 Dikgobe

Dikgobe tsa mabele — lehata

Whole sorghum grain stir-fried with onion, garlic and thyme, cooked in stock for half an hour, then finished with soaked cowpeas over a further hour — the Setswana grain-and-legume dish that belongs to weddings and to funerals.

Dikgobe is a category rather than a single recipe: a grain and a legume cooked together until they are one thing. The version written here uses whole sorghum, mabele, which is the older and more specifically Tswana form; a samp version is equally current and is what many sources mean by the name. At Setswana funerals it is one of only two starches considered proper, alongside sorghum porridge, which tells you where it sits — this is food for the days that matter.

Ingredients

Equipment: Two bowls, for the overnight soaks, Deep saucepan with a lid

    Soak overnight, in separate bowls

    • 500 ml (2 cups) whole sorghum grain (mabele)
    • 250 ml (1 cup) cowpeas (dinawa)

    The pot

    • 15 ml (1 tbsp) oil
    • 1 onion, chopped
    • 1 clove garlic, chopped
    • A handful of fresh thyme
    • 1 litre (4 cups) vegetable stock
    • 250 ml (1 cup) hot water
    • Herb salt, to taste

    To finish

    • 15 ml (1 tbsp) butter

    Method

    1. Soak the sorghum and the cowpeas overnight in separate bowls of water. Drain both and discard the soaking water.
    2. Heat the oil in a deep saucepan and fry the onion, garlic and thyme together until the onion is soft.
    3. Stir the drained sorghum into the onion mixture and cook for 3 minutes.
    4. Add the vegetable stock, put the lid on, and cook covered for 30 minutes.
    5. Stir in the drained cowpeas and add the cup of hot water. Cook covered over medium heat for a further 1 hour, until the cowpeas are soft.
    6. Season with herb salt.
    7. Add the butter and cook for another 10 minutes on low heat. Serve warm.

    Chef Tips

    • Soak the grain and the legume separately. They absorb at different rates and soaking them together gives you one of them undercooked.
    • The grain goes in first and gets a half-hour head start. Sorghum takes considerably longer than cowpeas and adding them together is the commonest way to ruin this.
    • Samp instead of sorghum makes an equally legitimate dikgobe — the dish is the pairing, not the specific grain. Samp will need longer, closer to two hours.
    • The butter at the end is what pulls it together. Ten minutes on low with the butter in changes the texture noticeably.
    • Cowpeas, sugar beans or jugo beans all work. Whoever cooked it in your family used whichever bean they had, and the bean genuinely changes the taste.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Soaking the sorghum and the beans in one bowl.
    • Adding the beans at the start.
    • Skipping the overnight soak, which no amount of extra cooking makes up for.
    • Salting before the legumes are soft, which can keep their skins tough.

    Health Benefits

    • Sorghum and cowpeas together form a complete protein without meat.
    • Very high in dietary fibre and slow-release carbohydrate.
    • Sorghum is naturally gluten-free and drought-tolerant — a grain suited to where it grows.

    Cultural Note

    Sorghum was the staple grain of southern Africa long before maize arrived, and dishes like this one are where it survives. That dikgobe is one of the two acceptable starches at a Setswana funeral is not an incidental detail: the older grain holds the older place.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Refrigerate up to 4 days. Freezes for 3 months.

    Reheating: Warm with a splash of water — it firms up considerably when cold.

    Serve with: Seswaa, Beef stew, Morogo wa Dinawa
