🍲 Ikokore

Ikokore — ifokore, water yam porridge

Grated water yam dropped by hand in small balls into simmering stock, spaced apart and never stirred — done in about fifteen minutes, when the balls go translucent and the sauce thickens.

Ikokore is the Ijebu dish, and it is entirely about the water yam. Peeled, washed and grated raw, salted straight away, it goes into the pot in small balls dropped from the hand with space between them so the stock can boil up around each one. You do not stir. If you need to move them you rock the pot. After about fifteen minutes the balls turn translucent and the sauce thickens, and only once they are set on the outside can a wooden spoon go anywhere near them. This is the only ikokore in the collection with a stated yield.

Ingredients

Equipment: Grater, Medium pot, Wooden spoon

  • 1/4 medium-size water yam
  • 16 pieces of beef, in small chunks
  • 1 cup pieces of smoked fish
  • 1/2 cup smoked prawns, dressed
  • 1 1/2 cooking spoons palm oil
  • 1 tbsp chopped efinrin (scent leaf)
  • Dried pepper, to taste
  • Seasoning, to taste
  • Salt to taste

Used in the method but not listed in the original

  • 1 onion
  • Beef seasoning

Method

  1. Peel, wash and grate the water yam. Add a little salt and mix. Be careful — it is slimy.
  2. Season and steam the meat with onion, pepper, salt and beef seasoning.
  3. When the meat is cooked, add the smoked fish, prawns and more seasoning to taste, with some more water, and cook about 3 minutes.
  4. Add the palm oil and cook 5 minutes.
  5. Using your hand, drop the water yam paste in small balls into the pot. Leave space between the balls so the stock can boil up and cook them.
  6. Reduce the flame to very low to let the balls cook through. Do not stir immediately. If you need to, rock or shake the pot to free them.
  7. When cooked, the balls are translucent and the sauce thickens. This takes about 15 minutes.
  8. Once the balls are set on the outside you can use a wooden spoon to stir the pottage, avoiding squashing them.
  9. Taste for salt, add the efinrin, cook 2 minutes, and it is ready.

Chef Tips

  • Salt the grated yam the moment it is grated. It stops it discolouring and it seasons from the inside.
  • Space the balls. Crowded together they fuse into one mass and you have lost the dish.
  • Rock the pot, do not stir it. That is the intermediate move between leaving it alone and breaking the balls up.
  • Translucent is the cue. Raw grated water yam is opaque and pale; cooked it goes glassy, and the sauce thickens at the same moment.
  • Water yam, not white yam. White yam will not grate to a paste and will not hold a ball — that one goes to iyan.
  • Grated water yam is genuinely slippery. Grate it over a bowl and expect to lose some to your hands.
  • Another named source forbids stirring at any point — 'Don't mix the Ikokore when cooking at all. Just let it cook.' If you are nervous, follow that and skip step 8.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Using white yam instead of water yam.
  • Dropping the balls touching each other.
  • Stirring before the balls have set.
  • Boiling hard instead of dropping to a very low flame.
  • Forgetting to salt the grated yam.

Health Benefits

  • Water yam is high in potassium and dietary fibre.
  • High protein from beef, smoked fish and prawns.
  • Red palm oil supplies vitamin E and carotenoids.
  • Scent leaf carries aromatic oils with antimicrobial activity.

Cultural Note

The Vanguard puts ikokore's standing plainly: it is an Ijebu delicacy, and if it is not on the menu at an Ijebu party the ceremony is not complete. The article goes further and measures its weight by comparison — for the Ijebu, ikokore carries the significance that pounded yam does for Ekiti men. Few Nigerian dishes are so tightly bound to one subgroup.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 2 days refrigerated. The balls soften.

Reheating: Low heat, no stirring, with a splash of water.

Serve with: Eba or garri, Fried fish, Cold palm wine
