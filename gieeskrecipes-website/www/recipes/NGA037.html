🖤 Omoebe

Omoebe — Edo black soup, omebe

Three leaves ground to a paste and simmered just two minutes in palm nut cream — the soup goes black because the vegetables oxidise, not because anything is burnt.

Omoebe is the Edo soup that turns almost black, and the colour is oxidation of the leaves rather than any kind of browning. Bitter leaf, scent leaf and ugwu are washed and ground smooth together — on a grinding stone traditionally, a blender now — and added at the very end to a base of palm nut cream and assorted meats. Two minutes and it is done. This is the only version with meat weights: half a kilo each of beef, shaki and ponmo. The leaves themselves are measured in bunches, because no source anywhere publishes a leaf weight for this dish.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large pot, Blender or grinding stone, Colander

  • 1 bunch bitter leaf
  • 1 bunch scent leaf (efirin) — basil as a substitute
  • 1/2 bunch ugwu (pumpkin leaves)
  • 1/2 kg beef
  • 1/2 kg shaki (tripe)
  • 1/2 kg ponmo (cow skin)
  • 1 cup crayfish pieces
  • 3 pieces stockfish
  • 1 piece dry fish
  • Seasoning cubes
  • 3 cooking spoons palm nut cream
  • 1 tbsp crayfish powder
  • 3 scotch bonnet peppers
  • 2 yellow peppers
  • 1/2 bulb onions

Method

  1. Boil the beef, shaki, ponmo and stockfish with the onions, scotch bonnet and seasoning cubes until softened.
  2. Wash the vegetables and blend until smooth, using a blender or a grinding stone.
  3. Remove the softened meats and return the rest to the pot to finish softening.
  4. Add the palm nut cream, crayfish powder, crayfish pieces and the broken smoked fish. Taste.
  5. Simmer four minutes on low.
  6. Add the ground vegetables with the chopped yellow pepper.
  7. Simmer a further two minutes, and serve hot with a swallow.

Chef Tips

  • Two minutes on the leaves and no more. The colour develops from oxidation as they hit the hot soup; cooking them longer dulls it and turns the flavour flat.
  • Grind the three leaves together, not separately. They are meant to be one paste.
  • Palm nut cream, not palm oil. Every substantive source for this soup uses the pulp of the palm fruit rather than the pressed oil, and the two are completely different materials.
  • Bitter leaf needs real washing. If you have bought it unwashed, par-boil it ten minutes uncovered, then rinse repeatedly in cold water and squeeze dry.
  • Scent leaf is efirin in Yoruba, nnchanwu in Igbo, ntong in Efik and aramogbo in Edo. Basil is the honest substitute if you cannot get it.
  • Yellow pepper goes in with the leaves, chopped rather than blended — it is there for fragrance at the end.
  • The leaf set is contested. This version uses three leaves; others add uziza, and one adds distinctively Edo leaves called ebe-ewewia or ebewewe that appear nowhere else.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Using palm oil instead of palm nut cream.
  • Cooking the leaves more than two minutes.
  • Grinding the leaves separately.
  • Under-washing the bitter leaf, which leaves it harsh.
  • Blending the yellow pepper into the leaf paste.

Health Benefits

  • Bitter leaf is traditionally used for digestion and carries iron and vitamin C.
  • Palm nut cream supplies carotenoids and vitamin E.
  • Very low in carbohydrate.
  • High in protein and collagen from ponmo, shaki and stockfish.

Cultural Note

Omoebe is an Edo soup with a reputation for being medicinal, which is partly the bitter leaf and partly the sheer density of greens in it. Among the Esan it is omebe, and the leaves used shift from community to community — which is exactly why the leaf set is the one thing no two sources agree on.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 4 days refrigerated. The colour darkens further.

Reheating: Low heat, briefly. The leaves are already fully cooked.

Serve with: Iyan, Starch, Eba or garri
