Rigatoni alla Gricia

Often called the forerunner of carbonara and amatriciana, gricia is Rome's simplest pasta — just guanciale, pecorino, and black pepper — sometimes nicknamed 'the white amatriciana.'

Gricia is thought to be the oldest of Rome's four great pastas, predating both carbonara and amatriciana, and its minimal ingredient list makes technique everything — rendering the guanciale properly and emulsifying the pecorino into a silky sauce without eggs or cream.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large pot, Large sauté pan

  • 400 g (14 oz) rigatoni
  • 200 g (7 oz) guanciale, cut into small strips
  • 150 g (1½ cups) grated Pecorino Romano
  • Freshly ground black pepper, generous amount
  • Salt, for the pasta water

Method

  1. Bring a large pot of well-salted water to a boil and cook the rigatoni until al dente.
  2. Meanwhile, cook the guanciale in a cold, dry pan set over medium-low heat, letting the fat render slowly until crisp and golden.
  3. Remove the pan from the heat once the guanciale is crisp, leaving the rendered fat in the pan.
  4. Drain the pasta, reserving a generous cup of the starchy cooking water.
  5. Add the pasta to the pan with the guanciale fat, along with a good splash of pasta water, and toss over low heat.
  6. Off the heat, add the pecorino gradually, tossing continuously and adding more pasta water as needed to form a smooth, creamy sauce that coats the pasta.
  7. Finish with a generous amount of black pepper, toss once more, and serve immediately.

Chef Tips

  • Rendering the guanciale slowly over gentle heat is the key technique — rushing it burns the fat before it fully renders.
  • Adding pecorino off direct heat, gradually, prevents it from clumping or turning stringy.
  • The starchy pasta water is what emulsifies everything into a proper sauce — do not skip reserving it.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Cooking the guanciale over heat that's too high, burning it before the fat properly renders.
  • Adding cheese directly over high heat, which causes it to seize into clumps instead of forming a smooth sauce.
  • Using pancetta or bacon as a substitute, which lack guanciale's distinctive texture and flavor.

Health Benefits

  • A rich source of protein and calcium from the guanciale and pecorino.
  • Best enjoyed as an occasional indulgence given its richness.

Cultural Note

Gricia takes its name from Grisciano, a small hamlet near Amatrice, and is considered by many Roman cooks to be the ancestor from which both amatriciana and carbonara eventually evolved.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Best eaten immediately; the sauce does not reheat well once it has set.

Reheating: Not recommended; gricia is at its best freshly tossed.

Serve with: Nothing more — this is a complete, minimalist dish
