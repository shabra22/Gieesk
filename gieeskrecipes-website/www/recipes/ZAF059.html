🌿 Mokhuse

Mokhuse — mukusule

Sun-dried greens boiled forty minutes to soften, then folded into onion, garlic, chilli, paprika and tomato cooked down to a thick sauce — the Limpopo way with dried morogo, and no groundnut in it at all.

Mokhuse is sun-dried leafy greens, and the point of them is that they keep. Venda migrant workers travelled with bags of it, which is as good a description of what this food is for as any. This is the tomato-and-chilli treatment rather than the groundnut one, and the two are genuinely different dishes made from the same raw material. The forty minutes of boiling is not optional — dried greens are tough and there is no shortcut.

Ingredients

Equipment: Pot, Colander

  • 250 g mokhuse (sun-dried greens)
  • 480 ml (2 cups) hot water, plus more if needed
  • 60 ml (1/4 cup) canola or sunflower oil
  • 1 large onion, finely chopped
  • 2 cloves garlic, finely chopped
  • 1 red chilli, finely chopped
  • 5 ml (1 tsp) ground paprika
  • 2 ripe tomatoes, chopped
  • Salt and ground black pepper, to taste

To serve

  • Mealie meal porridge

Method

  1. Cook the mokhuse in the 2 cups of hot water over medium heat for 40 minutes. Give it another 10 to 20 minutes if it is still tough.
  2. Drain and set aside.
  3. Heat the oil in a pot and add the onion, garlic, chilli and paprika. Cook for 2 to 3 minutes, until the onion softens.
  4. Add the tomatoes and stir until they break down into a thick sauce. Season with salt and pepper, adding a splash of water if it starts to stick.
  5. Return the drained mokhuse to the pot and warm it through for 5 minutes.
  6. Serve with mealie meal porridge.

Chef Tips

  • Forty minutes of real boiling, and taste it before you drain. Dried greens that have not softened properly stay leathery no matter what sauce goes on them.
  • Drain them. Dried greens hold a lot of water and it will thin the tomato sauce to nothing.
  • This has no peanut in it. The groundnut version is a separate dish — see the dobi treatment in morogo wa dinawa.
  • Swiss chard works if you cannot find mokhuse, but skip the 40-minute boil entirely and just wilt it.
  • A quarter cup of oil for 250 g of greens is a lot by modern habit and is correct here — dried greens drink oil and taste thin without it.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Cutting the initial boil short.
  • Adding the greens without draining them.
  • Under-cooking the tomato, so it stays watery rather than becoming a sauce.
  • Cooking the greens in the sauce for another half hour, which turns them to sludge.

Health Benefits

  • Dried indigenous greens are high in iron, calcium and vitamin A.
  • Drying concentrates the minerals, so a small quantity goes a long way.
  • High in fibre and entirely plant-based.

Cultural Note

That Venda migrant workers carried bags of mokhuse to the mines says most of what needs saying about it: a food that keeps for a year without refrigeration, weighs almost nothing dry, and tastes of home. Indigenous greens like these were dismissed for decades as poverty food and are now being reconsidered, which is a familiar pattern.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days. The dried greens keep for a year in a dry place.

Reheating: Warm gently in a pan.

Serve with: Vhuswa or stiff mealie meal porridge, Tshidzimba, Beef stew
