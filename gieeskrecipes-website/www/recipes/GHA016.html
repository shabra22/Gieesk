🥘 Groundnut Stew, Adapted

Nkantenkwan

A declared adaptation with a full cup of peanut butter and both palm oil and a marinade — reduce the liquid by one third, stirring every fifteen minutes.

This is a food historian's version and he says plainly it is his own way, written after eating the dish at a chop bar in Kumasi. The chicken is marinated with quartered red onion, smashed garlic, a ginger knob, a hot pepper, a stock cube, tomato paste and oil, then sautéed twenty minutes. Palm oil, broth, a full cup of unsweetened peanut butter, tomatoes, bay leaves and thyme go in, and the whole thing simmers forty-five minutes on low, stirred every fifteen minutes, until the liquid has reduced by one third. The cup of peanut butter makes this the thickest documented version.

Ingredients

Equipment: Heavy pot or Dutch oven, Mixing bowl, Wooden spoon

    Marinade

    • 2 lb boneless chicken breasts, or bone-in thighs and legs
    • 1 large red onion, quartered
    • 4 garlic cloves, smashed
    • 2 inch ginger knob
    • 1 small fresh hot pepper
    • 1 stock cube, or 2 tsp powdered broth
    • 1 tsp coarse black pepper
    • 2 tbsp tomato paste
    • 2 tbsp vegetable oil

    Stew

    • 2 tbsp palm oil
    • 2 cups vegetable or beef broth
    • 1 cup unsweetened peanut butter
    • 2 large chopped tomatoes, or a 28 oz tin
    • 2-3 bay leaves
    • 2-3 thyme sprigs

    Method

    1. Combine the marinade ingredients with the chicken.
    2. Sauté the chicken 20 minutes.
    3. Add the palm oil, broth, peanut butter, tomatoes, bay leaves and thyme.
    4. Simmer 45 minutes on low, stirring every 15 minutes.
    5. The liquid should reduce by one third.
    6. If using any optional umami additions, cook 10 minutes more.
    7. Serve with fufu or rice.

    Chef Tips

    • A full cup of peanut butter to two pounds of chicken is the richest documented ratio — roughly double the leanest. Expect a stew you can stand a spoon in, not a soup.
    • Reduced by one third is the cue, and it is the only documented groundnut soup that gives a volume endpoint rather than an oil-separation one.
    • Stir every fifteen minutes, which the author specifies. At this peanut concentration it will catch on the base otherwise.
    • Both palm oil and vegetable oil are used, in the stew and the marinade respectively. The palm oil is for colour and flavour rather than cooking.
    • Bay leaves and thyme are this author's additions and are not part of traditional nkatenkwan. Leave them out if you want the Ghanaian version.
    • It is a declared adaptation — 'My Way' is in the title — and the headnote credits a chop bar in Kumasi as the original. Treat it as a documented diaspora interpretation rather than a Ghanaian recipe.
    • His yield note is worth repeating as published: serves four to six, or two Ghanaians.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Treating this as a traditional recipe rather than the declared adaptation it is.
    • Skipping the fifteen-minute stirring intervals.
    • Leaving out the marinade stage.
    • Using sweetened peanut butter.
    • Stopping before the liquid has reduced by a third.

    Cultural Note

    A named food historian publishing his own version and labelling it as such is the honest way to adapt a dish, and it makes the recipe more useful rather than less — you can see exactly where he departed. The yield note, serves four to six or two Ghanaians, is also the best joke in the source material for this country.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: 4 days refrigerated, 3 months frozen.

    Reheating: Low heat with broth or water to loosen. It thickens considerably when cold.

    Serve with: Fufu, Rice, Omo Tuo
