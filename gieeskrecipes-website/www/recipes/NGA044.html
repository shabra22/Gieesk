🍲 Banga Soup

Banga — obe eyin, ofe akwu, obe-ekpo

Palm nut sauce cooked fifteen minutes with the spice mix, then reduced by a quarter to a third with the meat — lid off throughout, so it does not turn bitter.

Banga is the flagship soup of the Niger Delta and the way southerners from that region recognise one another. It is palm nut pulp rather than palm oil, cooked with a spice blend of atariko, rigije and belentientien, and the technique has two load-bearing instructions. First, the soup must reduce — by at least a quarter to a third — which is what concentrates it. Second, the lid stays off the whole time, so it neither turns bitter nor stays liquid. Four kilos of nuts or a tin of concentrate; either way this makes about eight litres.

Ingredients

Equipment: Very large pot, or a clay pot (evwere), Spice grinder, Blender, Sieve

  • 4 litres fresh palm nut sauce, or an 800 g tin of palm nut sauce with 1-2 tins of water
  • 2 red onions, peeled and blended with 4 red or yellow hot fresh chili peppers, to taste
  • 2 tbsp crayfish or dried shrimp, ground
  • 3 tbsp banga soup spice mix — see below
  • 1 tbsp dried belentientien, rinsed
  • 2 bouillon cubes
  • 2-3 bruised lemongrass leaves (optional)
  • Banga stick (optional)
  • About 1.5 kg soup chicken pieces
  • 500 g beef, cut into small chunks
  • 1 cup shrimps and prawns — heads removed, blended, strained, the liquid set aside
  • 1-2 fresh tilapia
  • 1 tsp bitterleaf, washed and shredded
  • Salt to taste

Banga soup spice mix — no quantities given in the source

  • Atariko (pronounced ataiko)
  • Rigije
  • Belentientien

Method

  1. For the spice mix, combine the atariko, rigije and belentientien in a spice grinder and blend till a fine, speckled powder forms.
  2. Put the palm sauce, fresh or tinned, in a large pot with the blended onion and chilli, the ground crayfish, the banga spice, the belentientien, the bouillon cubes and the banga spice stick.
  3. Cook on medium-high about 15 minutes. If you are using tinned concentrate it will need water added; the fresh version needs cooking down to concentrate the flavours and thicken.
  4. Add the chicken and beef, along with the shrimp head liquid.
  5. Cook on medium-low until the meat is tender, checking and adjusting the seasoning. The soup should be reduced by at least a quarter to a third.
  6. Once the meat is cooked, add the fish, the shrimps and prawns, and the fresh bitterleaf.
  7. Do not put the lid on the pot, so the soup does not turn too bitter and also does not stay too liquid.
  8. Gently stir and leave to simmer 7 to 8 minutes, being careful not to stir too vigorously so the fish does not break up.

Chef Tips

  • Lid off. The reason is given: covered, the soup turns too bitter and stays too liquid. Three independent Delta sources give the same instruction for banga and owo.
  • Reduce by a quarter to a third. That is the real doneness measure, not a time.
  • Strain the blended prawn heads. Leaving the shells and tentacle strands in is, in the author's own words, the absolute worst thing.
  • Go easy on the spice mix. The author's own first attempt used double, triple, quadruple what her mother said to, and the result was overpowering and bullish.
  • Stir gently once the fish is in, and not much. Tilapia breaks up at nothing.
  • A clay pot — evwere — is traditional and holds heat beautifully. Stand a saucer of water beneath it to stop it overheating.
  • Leave finished banga to sit for at least an hour and reheat it shortly before serving. That advice comes from another named cook's grandmother and it is good.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Covering the pot.
  • Not reducing the soup.
  • Failing to strain the blended prawn heads.
  • Over-spicing, which makes it bitter.
  • Stirring hard after the fish goes in.

Health Benefits

  • Palm nut pulp is exceptionally rich in carotenoids and tocotrienols.
  • Very high in protein from chicken, beef, fish and prawns.
  • Very low in carbohydrate.
  • Lemongrass and the banga spices carry aromatic compounds with digestive uses.

Cultural Note

Banga is how the Niger Delta identifies itself at the table. One named source puts it directly: she and her husband are both from the south of Nigeria, where banga is the flagship of soups and it is how southerners from the Delta are recognised. The clay pot it is cooked in — the evwere — is described on the same site as a precursor to the römertopf, which is a fair claim for a vessel that has been doing the same job for considerably longer.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 4 days refrigerated, 6 months frozen in meal-sized portions.

Reheating: Simmer gently, lid off. It improves on standing and reheating.

Serve with: Starch, Iyan, Eba or garri

  • THE REGIONAL NAME LIST, as printed, and the most useful cross-regional naming line found in any source: Yoruba (West) Obe Eyin; Igbo (East) Ofe Akwu; Edo/Delta/Urhobo Banga; Itsekiri Obe-Ekpo; Bayelsa Lii Fulou or Luu Fiai; Akwa Ibom/Cross River Efere Abak/Atama. Across West Africa: Sauce Graine in Togo and Benin, Mbanga in Cameroon, Abe nkwan in Ghana.
  • On the spices' function, attributed on the page to the author's mother: belentientien (clerodendrum volubile) and atariko (part black pepper, eucalyptus, very aromatic) serve to 'cut' through the richness, providing acidic balance and 'creating layers and dimensions of flavor'.
  • A FOUR-WAY DISAGREEMENT ON WHAT BELENTIENTIEN IS, not reconciled. This page glosses it 'dried belentientien, rinsed (dried mint – a pinch)' — that is, mint — and separately gives its botanical name as clerodendrum volubile. Nigerian Food TV calls it 'Banga spice leaves (called Obeletientien)' and offers scent leaf or bitter leaf as substitutes. Sisi Jemimah writes 'Crushed Obeletientien leaves or Dried bitter leaves'. The Itsekiri National Congress spells it beletete and places it in the ground spice blend rather than treating it as a leaf. Four sources, four identifications.
  • DISAGREEMENT on the stick: 'Banga stick' is marked optional here, while two other sources treat the oburunbebe stick as essential, one of them stating that without the four spices 'you have a palm nut stew' rather than banga. Also, only this source uses lemongrass.
