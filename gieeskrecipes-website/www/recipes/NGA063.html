🥬 Edikang Ikong

Edikang ikong — edikaikong

Three to four cups of waterleaf to one and a half of ugwu, steamed fifteen to twenty minutes with no water added at all — then two cups go in later.

Edikang ikong is the Efik soup of Calabar and the ratio of its two greens is the most argued-about number in Nigerian cooking. This version is waterleaf-dominant: three to four cups of waterleaf against one and a half cups of ugwu. It opens with the rule that gets quoted everywhere — do not add water — but the rule is scoped to the first fifteen to twenty minutes of steaming the meat in its own juices, and two cups of water do go in after that. The target is a thickish, non-watery consistency, and fresh leaves beat frozen.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large pot with a tight lid, Colander, Sharp knife

  • 500 g assorted meats
  • 3-4 cups waterleaf, washed and thinly sliced
  • 1 1/2 cups ugwu leaves, washed and thinly sliced
  • 3-5 pieces yellow scotch bonnet
  • 2 cooking spoons palm oil
  • 1 medium smoked or dried fish
  • 2 medium pieces stockfish
  • 1/2 cup raw king prawns
  • 3 heaped tbsp ground crayfish
  • 1 bouillon cube
  • 2 cups water, added after the steaming
  • Salt to taste

Method

  1. Steam the assorted meats with the bouillon cube and salt for 15 to 20 minutes, adding no water — let them cook in their own juices.
  2. Continue until the meats are tender, then add the 2 cups of water along with the stockfish and dried fish.
  3. Add the prawns and cook 3 minutes.
  4. Add the palm oil and cook 5 minutes, to dissolve it fully into the soup.
  5. Add the sliced waterleaf and cook 3 to 5 minutes on low heat.
  6. Add the ugwu, then the ground crayfish, and cook 1 minute.
  7. Take the pot off the stove and let it sit 3 minutes on the residual heat before serving.

Chef Tips

  • The no-water rule is about the first stage only. Fifteen to twenty minutes of steaming the meat in its own juices — then water goes in.
  • Waterleaf first, ugwu second. Waterleaf releases liquid as it cooks, which is why it leads and why the soup can afford to start dry.
  • Thinly sliced, both greens. Chunks of waterleaf stay watery in the middle.
  • Five minutes on the palm oil, specifically to dissolve it fully. Undissolved oil floating on top is the commonest fault in this soup.
  • One minute for the ugwu and three off the heat. Fluted pumpkin leaf collapses fast.
  • Fresh leaves over frozen, which the author states directly. Frozen waterleaf in particular releases far more liquid.
  • If your ugwu is very tender you may need less waterleaf, or none — another named source skips it entirely for exactly that reason.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Treating the no-water rule as absolute and then wondering why it burns.
  • Adding ugwu and waterleaf together.
  • Slicing the greens thickly.
  • Under-cooking the palm oil so it sits separate.
  • Boiling it after the greens are in.

Health Benefits

  • Fluted pumpkin leaf supplies iron, calcium and vitamins A and C.
  • Waterleaf is high in vitamin C and very low in calories.
  • Very low in carbohydrate.
  • High in protein from the meats, stockfish and prawns.

Cultural Note

Edikang ikong is the dish Calabar is known for, and its reputation is for density — it is meant to be almost a vegetable stew rather than a soup, which is what the no-water rule is really protecting. The Efik name for fluted pumpkin leaf is ikong ubong, and the dish's name carries it, so the leaf and the soup are named together.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 4 days refrigerated, 3 months frozen.

Reheating: Low heat, briefly. The greens are already fully cooked.

Serve with: Iyan, Eba or garri, Akpu or fufu

  • THE MECHANISM THAT EXPLAINS THE ARGUMENT, and the single most useful line found on the subject, is 1Q Food Platter's: she lists the waterleaf and then notes 'I did not use in this recipe because the ugu was very tender.' Waterleaf's function is moisture and softening — if the ugwu is tender you do not need it. The ratio is therefore a variable dependent on leaf condition rather than a fixed constant, which is precisely why it is argued about.
  • THE 'NO WATER AT ALL' RULE DOES NOT EXIST IN ABSOLUTE FORM in any source consulted, and hardening it would be wrong. This recipe is the only one with an explicit prohibition — 'Do NOT add water' — but it is scoped to the initial 15-to-20-minute steaming phase, after which she adds 2 cups. All Nigerian Recipes gives not a prohibition but a pot-state rule: 'There should be as little water as possible in the pot when the meat and fish are done.' 1Q Food Platter adds 3 cups explicitly. So the rule concerns the state of the pot when the vegetables go in, not withholding water from the dish.
