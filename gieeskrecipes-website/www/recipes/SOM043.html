🥩 Cad iyo Caano

Cad iyo Caano

Boneless lamb shoulder rubbed with garlic crushed in salt and lemon, seared directly on red coals three minutes a side, rested ten, and drunk down with camel milk — as close as Somalia comes to a national dish.

Cad iyo caano means a chunk of meat and camel milk, and if one plate of food stands for Somalia this is it. The phrase has outgrown the food: to have cad iyo caano is to have enough, to be provided for. The cooking is nomadic in origin and unchanged — meat laid on the embers rather than over them, where a charcoal fire runs hot enough to seal the surface before the inside dries out.

Ingredients

Equipment: Mortar and pestle, Natural hardwood charcoal, Barbecue or fire pit, Long tongs

  • 1.1 kg (2 1/2 lb) boneless lamb shoulder, cut into large flat pieces
  • 5 cloves garlic, minced
  • 2 lemons, juiced
  • 2 tsp kosher or coarse salt
  • 1 tsp ground black pepper

To serve

  • Fresh camel milk, or whole cow's milk

Method

  1. Put the garlic and the coarse salt in a mortar and crush them together — the salt does the work of grinding the garlic down to a paste.
  2. Add the lemon juice to the paste and mix.
  3. Rub the marinade over both sides of each piece of lamb and leave them while you get the fire going.
  4. Light natural hardwood charcoal and wait for it to burn down to red embers. Blow the top layer of ash away.
  5. Lay the lamb pieces directly on the coals.
  6. Cook 3 minutes a side for medium-rare to medium, or 4 minutes a side for medium-well and up. Season with the black pepper as you turn.
  7. Take the meat off and rest it for 10 minutes — longer than it spent cooking — before cutting it.
  8. Cut across the grain and serve with a glass of camel milk.

Chef Tips

  • Meat on the coals, not on a grate above them. Fat dripping onto embers through a grate flares up and deposits soot, which tastes quite different from the char that comes from direct contact.
  • Rest it for the full ten minutes. The cooking takes eight; the rest takes ten, and skipping it loses the juices onto the board.
  • Somalis go for the cuts nearest the bone — ribs, shoulder, shanks — and traditionally do not eat meat rare. Medium is where a shoulder is at its best.
  • Deboning gives you pieces of uneven thickness, which is useful when people round the fire want it cooked differently. Keep the bones for fuud ari.
  • The lemon is not only for flavour — the acid tenderises the shoulder while the fire heats up.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Cooking over a grate, which causes flare-ups and sooty meat.
  • Cutting into it straight off the fire, which empties the juices onto the board.
  • Cooking a shoulder to well-done, after which a naturally fatty cut goes dry rather than tender.
  • Putting the meat on before the flames have died to embers, so the outside chars black while the middle is raw.

Health Benefits

  • Lamb provides protein, iron, zinc and vitamin B12.
  • Grilled with no added fat — the marinade is garlic, lemon and salt only.
  • Camel milk is lower in fat than cow's milk and is traditionally valued as a complete food in pastoral diets.

Cultural Note

Camel milk with camel or lamb meat is the traditional meal of Somali pastoralists, and cad iyo caano is the phrase that stands for sufficiency itself. Different regions will name their own representative dish, but this is the one that Somalis anywhere recognise as the country on a plate.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days.

Reheating: Warm briefly in a hot dry pan. Reheating past warm cooks it further.

Serve with: Camel milk, Bariis Maraq, Basbaas
