🌿 Ewedu

Ewedu — jute leaf, ooyo

Jute leaf boiled two minutes, then eight to ten more with locust bean, and pulsed — not blended — with only half a cup of the cooking water, so it stays semi-pureed rather than smooth.

Ewedu is the slippery green half of abula. The traditional tool is the ijabe, a small broom that pounds the leaves in the pot until they break into tiny pieces — and the point of it is draw, not smoothness. That is why a whisk substitutes perfectly well and a blender overdelivers. This version uses the pulse function deliberately, to a semi-puree, and it uses no potash at all: the author says so outright and relies on the blending for viscosity instead.

Ingredients

Equipment: Medium pot, Ijabe (ewedu broom), whisk or blender, Colander

  • 1 big bunch ewedu leaves
  • 2 cups water
  • 1 tbsp locust beans (iru)
  • 1 heaped tbsp ground crayfish
  • 1 bouillon cube (half a crayfish-flavoured cube used in the original)
  • Salt to taste

Method

  1. Pick the leaves off without the stalk or stem. Only the leaf goes in.
  2. Wash thoroughly — ewedu carries grit.
  3. Bring the 2 cups of water to a rolling boil on medium heat.
  4. Add the leaves and boil 2 minutes.
  5. Add the locust beans and continue boiling until tender, roughly 8 to 10 minutes.
  6. Take it off the heat and let it cool briefly.
  7. Transfer to the blender with the iru and only 1/2 cup of the cooking water — not all of it.
  8. Pulse to a semi-pureed consistency. Do not blend smooth.
  9. Return to the pan with the crayfish, bouillon and salt.
  10. Off the heat, let it simmer 3 minutes on the residual heat, stirring.

Chef Tips

  • Only half a cup of the boiling water goes into the blender. Tip all of it in and you get soup instead of ewedu.
  • Pulse, do not blend. The ijabe breaks the leaves into pieces; it does not puree them. A whisk does the same job — the author says it 'functions just as good as an Ijabe'.
  • No potash here, and that is a deliberate position: 'I don't cook with Kaun (Potash), but rest assured, blending does enhance the viscosity.' For the version that does use it, see Ewedu with Kaun.
  • Stems make it stringy. Strip them.
  • The last 3 minutes are on residual heat with the stove off. Boiling it after the crayfish goes in dulls the green.
  • Ewedu is served beside gbegiri rather than mixed into it — the mixing happens on the plate.
  • If it will not draw, you under-boiled the leaves. Tender is the condition, not the clock.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Blending it smooth.
  • Pouring all the cooking water into the blend.
  • Leaving the stalks on.
  • Boiling it hard after the crayfish is in.
  • Adding potash by default when the recipe is built not to need it.

Health Benefits

  • Jute leaf is high in vitamins A, C and E and in calcium.
  • Very low in calories and fat.
  • The mucilage is a soluble fibre.
  • Iron-rich.

Cultural Note

Ewedu is Corchorus — the same jute leaf eaten as molokhia across Egypt and the Levant, and it travels under that name in Middle Eastern shops. Across Nigerian languages it is ewedu in Yoruba, kerenkere in Igbo and rama or ayoyo in Hausa. In Yorubaland it is almost never eaten alone: it goes on the plate with gbegiri and a stew, and the three together are abula.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 3 days refrigerated. It thickens cold.

Reheating: Gentle heat with a splash of water. Do not boil.

Serve with: Amala, Gbegiri, Abula
