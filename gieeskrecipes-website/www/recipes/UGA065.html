🌱 Ttimpa in Groundnut Soup

Ttimpa — cocoyam leaves

Young cocoyam leaves wrapped in banana leaf and steamed soft — often on top of whatever else is cooking — then mashed and stirred into a separately boiled groundnut sauce.

Ttimpa is cocoyam leaves in groundnut sauce, and the method is a good example of how efficient Ugandan kitchens are: the leaves steam in a banana leaf parcel set on top of the bananas or potatoes already in the pot, so the dish costs no extra fuel. The groundnut paste is dissolved in cold water and boiled separately, and only then do the mashed leaves go in. It comes out as a thick soup rather than a green side.

Ingredients

Equipment: Deep pan with a lid, or a steamer, Second pan, for the sauce, Masher or wooden spoon

  • 500 g young cocoyam leaves
  • 1 banana leaf
  • 250 g groundnut paste
  • Cold water, to dissolve the paste
  • Salt

Method

  1. Wrap the young cocoyam leaves in the banana leaf.
  2. Steam until soft. They can be steamed on top of other foods already cooking, such as bananas or potatoes.

The sauce

  1. In a separate pan, dissolve the groundnut paste in cold water.
  2. Boil until ready. Salt may be added to the sauce at this stage.

Together

  1. Mash the steamed leaves.
  2. Add the mashed leaves to the groundnut sauce and stir through.
  3. Serve as a thick soup with a staple.

Chef Tips

  • Steam the leaves on top of whatever else is in the pot. It is how this is actually cooked and it costs nothing extra.
  • Dissolve the groundnut paste in cold water, not hot. Hot water makes it seize into lumps, and this is the same rule that governs every groundnut sauce in Uganda.
  • Young leaves only. Mature cocoyam leaves stay fibrous and can be harsh.
  • Mash the leaves after steaming and before they go into the sauce. Whole steamed leaves in a groundnut sauce is a different, less good dish.
  • Two types are grown and both are eaten: bwayise is the wetland kind, matures in seven to nine months, and gives both leaves and tubers; koona is the upland kind, more of a biennial, drought resistant, and grown for the tubers.
  • Identifying bwayise: the leaves have no slit near the stalk and normally carry a purple dot in the middle, though some Mbale varieties are entirely bright green with no dot.
  • It is a thick soup, not a side. Serve it over a starch and eat it with your hands if you want the full effect.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Dissolving the groundnut paste in hot water.
  • Using mature leaves.
  • Skipping the mashing.
  • Boiling the leaves in water rather than steaming them in leaf.
  • Combining the two before the sauce has properly boiled.

Health Benefits

  • Cocoyam leaves are high in vitamins A and C, iron, calcium and folate.
  • Groundnut paste adds protein, niacin and the fat that helps absorb the leaf carotenoids.
  • Steamed rather than boiled, so less is lost to the water.
  • Contains peanuts.

Cultural Note

Growing a plant for both its leaves and its tubers, and having separate names and separate growing conditions for the two varieties that do each job, is agricultural knowledge held in a language rather than in a book. The distinction between bwayise and koona would be invisible to anyone shopping in English.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days.

Reheating: Warm gently with a splash of water — groundnut sauces thicken as they stand.

Serve with: Matooke, Steamed Bitter Yams, Boiled Sweet Potatoes
