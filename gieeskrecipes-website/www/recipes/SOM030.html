🍅 Dallac Bilaash

Dallac Bilaash — Been Dallac

The meatless tomato sauce Somalis eat with anjero, in two versions — a plain one where the tomatoes do the talking, and a fuller one with carrot, pepper and xawaash that stands up as a side dish on its own.

The names tell you exactly what Somalis think of a sauce with no meat in it. Been dallac translates as pretend stir-fry, from been, meaning lie. Dallac bilaash means the worthless stir-fry. It is nevertheless on tables every week, usually with anjero at breakfast, and the version with extras is what many cooks use as the base for cooking meat or fish in.

Ingredients

Equipment: Frying pan with a lid

    The plain sauce

    • 3 tbsp olive oil
    • 1 medium onion, finely diced
    • 3 cloves garlic, minced
    • 4 medium tomatoes, peeled and diced
    • 1/2 tsp salt
    • 1/4 tsp ground black pepper

    For the fuller version, add

    • 1 extra clove garlic, minced
    • 2 tbsp tomato paste
    • 32 g (1/4 cup) grated carrot
    • 45 g (1/4 cup) green bell pepper, finely diced
    • 1 jalapeno, seeded and finely diced
    • 1 tsp xawaash spice blend
    • 120 ml (1/2 cup) water
    • 40 g (1/4 cup) fresh cilantro, chopped

    Method

    1. Fry the onion in the olive oil over medium heat for 3 minutes, then add the garlic and cook for one minute more.
    2. Add the diced tomatoes and the salt and stir well. For the plain sauce, skip to step 5.
    3. For the fuller version, stir in the tomato paste, then the grated carrot, the jalapeno, the bell pepper and the xawaash, stirring after each addition.
    4. Pour in the water and stir well.
    5. Cover and cook over low heat for 10 minutes.
    6. For the fuller version, stir through the chopped cilantro.
    7. Finish with the black pepper, stir once and take the pan off the heat.

    Chef Tips

    • If you want to taste the tomatoes, make the plain one. It is quicker, easier, and the whole point of it is that nothing competes.
    • Somali cooks traditionally do not seed the tomatoes. If you do seed them, squeeze as much juice as you can out of the seeds and back into the pan.
    • The fuller version is robust enough to cook meat or fish directly in — add the protein after step 5 and let it simmer through.
    • One seeded jalapeno gives warmth rather than heat. Leave the seeds in, or add a second, if you want it to bite.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Cooking it over high heat to save time, which catches the tomato paste and makes the sauce taste burnt.
    • Adding the cilantro early, where ten minutes of simmering turns it grey and flavourless.
    • Using unpeeled tomatoes, which leaves curls of skin through an otherwise smooth sauce.
    • Adding the xawaash to the plain version — the two sauces are different dishes, and the plain one is meant to taste of tomato only.

    Health Benefits

    • Cooked tomatoes are a good source of lycopene, which the body absorbs better from cooked fruit than raw.
    • Olive oil contributes monounsaturated fat that helps the lycopene absorb.
    • Entirely plant-based and low in calories.

    Cultural Note

    The dish carries a small piece of Mogadishu social history: it is one of the recipes commonly taught to children, and for many Somali men of a certain generation it was the first thing they were shown how to cook — often by an uncle, in an era when seeing a man in the kitchen at all was worth remarking on.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Refrigerate up to 4 days. Freezes well for 3 months.

    Reheating: Reheat gently on the hob, adding a little water if it has thickened.

    Serve with: Canjeero, Taraaqad la Shiilay, Muufo
