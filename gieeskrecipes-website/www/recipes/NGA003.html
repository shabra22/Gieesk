🌿 Ewedu Elegusi

Ewedu elegusi

Ewedu thickened with a single tablespoon of blended melon seed instead of potash — and the cue is the moment the egusi starts to curdle.

This is the ewedu that solves the potash question by sidestepping it. One tablespoon of blended egusi, slaked in two tablespoons of water, goes in at the end; as soon as it begins to curdle you stir, and the curdling is the signal. It gives body without kaun and without over-blending, and it makes the dish faintly nutty in a way plain ewedu is not. Six packed cups of leaves to one cup of water is a much tighter ratio than the other documented version, so this comes out thicker from the start.

Ingredients

Equipment: Medium pot, Blender or ijabe, Small bowl

  • 6 packed cups picked ewedu leaves
  • 1 cup water
  • 1 tbsp blended egusi (melon seed)
  • 2 tbsp water, for slaking the egusi
  • 1 tbsp locust beans (iru)
  • Salt to taste
  • About 1 cup extra warm water, to thin at the end

Method

  1. Combine the blended egusi with 2 tbsp of water in a small bowl and set aside.
  2. Place a medium pot on high heat, add the 1 cup of water and bring to a boil.
  3. Add the ewedu leaves. Reduce the heat to medium and cook uncovered 5 minutes, or until the leaves are softened.
  4. Pour the boiled ewedu into a blender and process until smooth — this is the step traditionally done with the ijabe broom.
  5. Return the blended ewedu to the pot and add the iru, salt and the egusi paste.
  6. As soon as the egusi starts to curdle, stir.
  7. Stir in more warm water to your desired thickness — about 1 cup extra.
  8. Serve with stew and gbegiri.

Chef Tips

  • The curdling is the cue. Watch for it rather than timing it.
  • Slake the egusi in its two tablespoons of water first. Dropped in dry it sits in lumps.
  • Six packed cups of leaf to one cup of water is deliberately tight. The extra cup of warm water at the end is how you set the final consistency, after the thickening has happened.
  • This version carries no crayfish at all, where the Sisi Jemimah ewedu uses a heaped tablespoon. The egusi is doing the savoury work instead.
  • One tablespoon of egusi is enough. More and you have made a thin egusi soup with ewedu in it.
  • The leaves want 5 minutes or until softened, uncovered — covering it dulls the colour.
  • If you have an ijabe, use it here in preference to the blender. This recipe blends smooth, which is more than the broom would do.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Adding the egusi dry.
  • Missing the curdling and letting it set hard.
  • Using more than a tablespoon of egusi.
  • Covering the pot while the leaves cook.
  • Thinning it before the egusi has curdled rather than after.

Health Benefits

  • Melon seed adds plant protein and healthy fat to a very low-calorie green.
  • Jute leaf is rich in vitamins A and C, calcium and iron.
  • Thickened without any alkali.
  • High in soluble fibre.

Cultural Note

Ewedu elegusi is the practical answer to a real argument. The named Yoruba food writers largely refuse kaun in ewedu and get their draw from blending; the newspaper recipes use it. Thickening with a spoonful of egusi is a third route, and it is the one that needs neither an alkali nor a heavy hand with the blender.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 2 days refrigerated.

Reheating: Low heat with a little water. The egusi will tighten as it cools.

Serve with: Amala, Gbegiri, Obe Ata
