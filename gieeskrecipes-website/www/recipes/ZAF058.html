🥬 Morogo wa Dinawa

Morogo wa dinawa — dobi

Dried cowpea leaves cooked until tender, then a cup and a half of pounded peanuts stirred in and simmered twenty minutes — four ingredients, no tomato, and far more substantial than the word greens suggests.

Morogo wa dinawa is cowpea leaves, picked young in summer, blanched and squeezed into balls and sun-dried for the winter when nothing green is growing. Rehydrated, they taste close to spinach. This is the groundnut version, dobi, and the ratio is the striking part: a cup and a half of pounded peanuts to a cup of dried leaves, which makes it a dish rather than a relish. There is no tomato in it at all, which is what separates it from the everyday morogo most people cook.

Ingredients

Equipment: Pot with a lid, Mortar and pestle, or a food processor

  • 250 ml (1 cup) dried morogo wa dinawa (dried cowpea leaves)
  • 700 ml (3 cups) water
  • 375 ml (1 1/2 cups) raw peanuts, pounded
  • A pinch of salt

Method

  1. Wash the dried morogo thoroughly — it is field-picked and needs it.
  2. Put it in a pot, pour in the water, put the lid on and cook until tender.
  3. Pound the raw peanuts to a coarse powder.
  4. Add the pounded peanuts and a pinch of salt.
  5. Cook on low heat for 20 minutes, until it has thickened.
  6. Serve with sorghum porridge, pearl millet or maize meal, and meat.

Chef Tips

  • Wash it more than you think you need to. Dried morogo is sun-dried in the open and carries dust and grit.
  • No tomato, no onion. The tomato-and-onion version is a different and more modern dish; this one is leaves, water, groundnut and salt.
  • A cup and a half of peanuts to a cup of leaves looks like a lot and is meant to. The nut is a main ingredient here, not a seasoning.
  • How long the leaves take depends entirely on how they were dried. Squeezed-and-dried balls can need forty minutes of real boiling; loose crushed leaves are tender in fifteen.
  • Swiss chard or spinach will stand in if you cannot get dried morogo, but cut the water right back — fresh greens bring their own.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Not washing the dried leaves properly.
  • Adding the peanuts at the start, where they overcook and go bitter.
  • Using peanut butter instead of pounded raw peanuts — it is sweeter and oilier and changes the dish.
  • Boiling it hard after the nuts go in, which splits the oil out.

Health Benefits

  • Cowpea leaves are high in protein, iron, calcium, phosphorus and zinc — unusually so for a leafy green.
  • Groundnuts add fat and protein, which makes the iron and the carotenoids in the leaves easier to absorb.
  • Very high in fibre, and entirely plant-based.
  • Contains peanuts — not suitable for anyone with a nut allergy.

Cultural Note

Morogo wa dinawa is on Slow Food's Ark of Taste. The leaves are picked November to April, four to six weeks after the cowpeas germinate, while still tender, then blanched, kneaded to a pulp, squeezed into balls and sun-dried — a preserving method that turns a summer green into a winter vegetable, worked out long before anyone had a freezer.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days. The dried leaves keep for a year in a paper bag or clay pot.

Reheating: Warm gently with a splash of water.

Serve with: Motoho wa Ting, Seswaa, Uphuthu
