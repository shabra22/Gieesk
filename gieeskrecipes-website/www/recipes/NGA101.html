🥤 Fura da Nono

Fura da nono

Not cooked but assembled: soured milk in a bowl, pounded millet balls mashed into it, sugar to taste, eaten with a spoon.

Fura da nono is the northern drink that is really a meal, and like abula it is an assembly rather than a recipe. The nono — raw milk soured for about a day with a little of the previous batch — goes into a bowl, the fura balls are mashed into it with a spoon or by hand until the mixture is thick and speckled, and sugar is added to taste. It is drunk cold, often from a calabash, and it is the single most recognisable thing a Fulani milk seller carries. Both halves are documented separately in this collection.

Ingredients

Equipment: A wide bowl, or a calabash, Spoon

    Per serving

    • About 1 1/2 cups nono, cold
    • 2-3 fura balls
    • Sugar, to taste

    Optional

    • A pinch of ground ginger or cloves
    • Ice

    Method

    1. Pour the cold nono into a bowl or calabash.
    2. Add the fura balls and mash them into the milk with a spoon, or with clean hands, until they break down and the mixture thickens.
    3. Add sugar to taste and stir.
    4. Add a pinch of ginger if you like, and ice if the weather calls for it.
    5. Eat with a spoon, or drink it once it is loose enough.

    Chef Tips

    • Mash the fura into the nono rather than stirring whole balls around. The texture should be thick and slightly grainy throughout.
    • Cold nono. This is a hot-weather drink and it is wrong at room temperature.
    • Sugar last and to taste. How sour the nono is depends on how long it fermented, so there is no fixed amount.
    • More fura makes it a meal, less makes it a drink. A seller will ask you which you want.
    • A calabash is traditional and it keeps the drink cool longer than glass.
    • Both components keep separately — nono about three days refrigerated, fura about two — so make them ahead and assemble to order.
    • Neither component is quantified by any source. The per-serving amounts here are a serving suggestion, not a sourced ratio.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Serving it at room temperature.
    • Leaving the fura balls whole.
    • Sugaring it before tasting the nono's sourness.
    • Assembling it hours ahead, which lets the millet go gluey.
    • Using fresh milk rather than soured.

    Health Benefits

    • Live lactic acid bacteria from the soured milk.
    • Millet and milk together give a more complete protein than either alone.
    • Fermentation reduces the lactose, making it easier to digest than fresh milk.
    • Filling enough to serve as a meal.

    Cultural Note

    Fura da nono is sold by Fulani women carrying calabashes, and it is both a drink and a day's lunch. The combination is ancient in its logic — a travelling pastoralist's grain and a travelling pastoralist's milk, neither of which keeps well alone, brought together at the point of eating. It is the northern equivalent of abula in that nothing about it happens in a pot.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Assemble to order. The components keep separately — see each.

    Reheating: Not applicable — served cold.

    Serve with: Kosai or akara, Masa, Dakuwa Aya

    • The two components are documented separately in this collection because the evidence for each is different in kind: nono has a published, measurable fermentation endpoint while fura has a well-corroborated technique and no quantities at all.
    • ON WHAT NONO IS, an unreconciled disagreement that matters for this drink. The same Dobby's Signature page states 'Nunu "Pronounced No-no" is the Hausa word for fresh cow milk & it's usually collected early in the morning in calabash bowls' — and then describes the dish as 'a combination of fermented cow milk (Locally prepared yogurt) with ground millet'. That is an internal contradiction within one page. The academic sources are consistent that it is fermented: Okonkwo (2011) calls it 'an opaque white to milky coloured liquid food drink got from fermented raw milk'; Egwim et al. call it 'sour milk'. The fermented reading is followed here.
    • THE FERMENTATION ENDPOINT that makes this publishable at all comes from Atanda, O.O. and Ikenebomeh, M.J., 'Changes in the acidity and lactic acid content of "Nono", a Nigerian cultured milk product', Letters in Applied Microbiology, 1988, 6(6), 137–138: pH falls from 6.78 ± 0.19 to 4.30 ± 0.11 and lactic acid rises from 0.32 ± 0.04% to 2.50 ± 0.02% w/v at 27 ± 2°C. Full details and the time disagreement are set out at Nono.
    • SAFETY: nono is traditionally made from raw unpasteurised milk and sold in open calabashes, and Okonkwo (2011) states plainly that this practice contravenes food safety principles. See the note at Nono.
