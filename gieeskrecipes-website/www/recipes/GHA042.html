Akple — the Ewe answer to banku

Half a cup of cassava dough to a cup of corn dough, cooked ten to fifteen minutes until it forms a solid mass and no longer looks wet — and it is unfermented.

Akple is the Ewe counterpart to banku and the surprise is that it is not fermented. The author states it plainly: it is her understanding that akple is made from unfermented corn dough. A cup of white cornmeal is blended finer, mixed with a teaspoon of corn starch and half a cup of water to make the corn dough; half a cup of drained cassava dough is blended with water; the two are mixed in a saucepan with half a cup of water and a little salt, then cooked over medium heat with another half cup added all at once and stirred until it forms a solid mass. Shaped by shaking it in a wetted calabash.

Ingredients

Equipment: Blender, Saucepan, Heavy wooden spoon or stick, Calabash or bowl, for shaping

  • 1 cup corn dough — see the method
  • 1/2 cup cassava dough
  • 1 cup white cornmeal, blended finer (optional step)
  • 1 tsp corn starch (optional, for a smoother dough)
  • 1/2 cup water, for mixing the cornmeal
  • 1/2 cup water, for combining the doughs
  • Another 1/2 cup water, added during cooking
  • About 1/4 tsp salt

Method

  1. To make the corn dough: blend a cup of white cornmeal a little finer, mix it in a bowl with a teaspoon of corn starch, then mix in half a cup of water.
  2. To make the cassava dough: add a cup of water to a blender and blend the drained cassava to a dough or paste.
  3. Mix the two doughs in a saucepan with half a cup of water to a smooth creamy mixture, and add a little salt. A heavy wooden spoon or stick works well.
  4. Put the mixture on a medium heat and stir as it heats, adding another half cup of water all at once.
  5. Continue stirring until it forms a solid mass, about 10 to 15 minutes.
  6. Do not allow the dough to become lumpy or scorch on the bottom. Turn it as you stir.
  7. When the mixture becomes fairly solid and no longer looks wet, wet a calabash or bowl thoroughly.
  8. Put a spoonful of dough into the calabash and shake it vigorously, rolling the dough inside into a circle or oval.

Chef Tips

  • It is unfermented, and this is the key point. The author says so directly, which makes akple much quicker than banku — no days of souring, just dough and heat.
  • Solid mass and no longer looking wet is the doneness cue, at ten to fifteen minutes. That is the whole test.
  • The second half cup of water goes in ALL AT ONCE during cooking, not gradually. That is her instruction and it matters for the texture.
  • Turn it as you stir and watch the bottom. Lumpy or scorched are the two failure modes and both are irreversible.
  • The shaping is done by shaking a wetted calabash, not by hand — the dough rolls itself into a circle or oval inside the bowl.
  • One part cassava dough to two parts corn dough. The cassava is what gives akple its stretch.
  • Originally eaten mainly with fetri ma or fetri detsi, her own note, but it goes with fried fish and pepper sauce or shito, or palaver sauce.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Fermenting the corn dough, which would make it banku.
  • Adding the second water gradually rather than all at once.
  • Letting it go lumpy.
  • Scorching the base.
  • Shaping by hand rather than in a wetted calabash.

Health Benefits

  • Gluten-free and fat-free.
  • Cassava and maize together give a fuller amino acid profile than either alone.
  • No fermentation means no acidity, which suits sensitive stomachs.
  • Minimal salt — about a quarter teaspoon for two servings.

Cultural Note

Akple is the Volta Region's swallow and Ewe cooks will tell you it is not banku, whatever it looks like in the bowl. If the fermented-versus-unfermented distinction holds, they are right in a way that goes beyond regional pride: one is a soured dough and the other is not, and they taste quite different as a result.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Same day. It firms as it cools.

Reheating: Steam briefly.

Serve with: Fetri Detsi, Palaver Sauce, Shito
