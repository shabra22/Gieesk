🟤 Karo

Karo — kalo, kwon kal, millet bread

Millet and cassava flour mixed three to one, added gradually to three litres of boiling water per kilo, mingled half an hour until it leaves the stick clean, then settled in a flour-lined basket.

Karo — kalo in the west, kwon kal in Acholi — is the mingled millet bread, and it is the hardest thing to make well in Ugandan cooking. The reason the cassava flour is there is technical and worth knowing: millet flour alone is coarse and reacts stiffly to water, so it is difficult to turn into a paste, and cassava improves the texture and workability. The doneness cue is the one every mingled starch shares: it stops sticking to the stirring stick.

Ingredients

Equipment: Heavy pan, Strong wooden mingling stick, Basket, or a bowl, Lid or cloth

  • Millet flour and cassava flour, mixed 3 parts millet to 1 part cassava — or bought pre-mixed
  • 3 litres water per kilo of flour
  • Hot water, for the second addition
  • Raw flour, to line the basket

Method

  1. Combine the millet and cassava flour in a 3 to 1 ratio, or buy it pre-mixed.
  2. Boil the water — three litres to a kilo of flour.
  3. Add the flour gradually while stirring continuously. Adding it gradually is what prevents air pockets.
  4. Keep working it until the water has reduced by half.
  5. Add more water at that point — and it must be hot. Cold water at this stage makes the flour stiff.

The mingling

  1. Keep mixing. It takes about 30 minutes, depending on the amount, and you stop when the mixture no longer sticks to the stirring stick.

Settling

  1. Transfer it to a basket lined with raw flour.
  2. Shake the basket to let it settle into shape.
  3. Cover it and rest 10 to 15 minutes before serving.

Chef Tips

  • No longer sticking to the stick. That is the cue, and it is the same one that governs every mingled starch from posho to atapa.
  • Three litres of water to a kilo of flour. That is the only published ratio for karo and it is the number to start from.
  • Cassava flour is not a cheapening of the millet. Millet alone is coarse and stiff in water and hard to bring to a paste — the cassava is what makes it workable.
  • Add the flour gradually. Dumped in, it traps air pockets that you cannot work out afterwards.
  • Hot water for the second addition, never cold. Cold water at that point sets the flour stiff and the batch is finished.
  • Thirty minutes of real mingling. This is physical work and it is why karo is a dish people are proud of making.
  • Line the basket with raw flour before the dough goes in, or it sticks and you lose the shape.
  • The source gives the millet-to-cassava ratio two incompatible ways — three to one in its method, and one to three through one to five in its discussion. Three to one is used here and the conflict is stated rather than resolved. Test it before you commit.
  • In an Ankole family there is a special basket, the endiiro, used by the head of the family, and separate baskets for everyone else.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Using millet flour alone.
  • Adding the flour all at once.
  • Adding cold water at the halfway point.
  • Stopping before it leaves the stick clean.
  • Putting it into an unlined basket.

Health Benefits

  • Finger millet is exceptionally high in calcium for a grain, plus iron and methionine.
  • Cassava flour is naturally gluten-free and adds resistant starch.
  • No fat at all — flour and water only.
  • Very filling and slow to release its energy.

Cultural Note

Karo is ceremonial food as much as a staple: it is what eshabwe is served over at weddings and child-namings, and receiving a pet name in Tooro requires a function where millet and firinda are served for it to count. The separate baskets by seniority in an Ankole household are a detail worth keeping — food and status are not separable here.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Best fresh. It firms considerably as it cools.

Reheating: Steam briefly, or eat it cold as many people do.

Serve with: Eshabwe, Malakwang, Dek Ngor
