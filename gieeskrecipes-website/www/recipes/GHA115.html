🍩 Bofrot Cake Version

African doughnut, cake version

Baking powder instead of yeast — coarser and crisper, just as a muffin differs from a slice of bread, and bigger than a golf ball.

The modern Ghanaian bofrot, raised with baking powder rather than palm wine or yeast, adapted from a recipe used by Barbara Baeta of Flair Catering in Accra. Six ounces of bread flour has margarine cut into it until the mixture resembles cornmeal, then sugar, nutmeg and baking powder. Egg, milk and vanilla go in all at once with a few swift strokes to keep the batter from becoming tough, and it rests about thirty minutes. The oil goes to 375 and the test is a float: a small amount of dough dropped in will quickly rise to the surface. The balls are usually larger than a golf ball and a little smaller than a tennis ball.

Ingredients

Equipment: Bowl and sifter, Two table knives, or your hands, Deep fryer or deep heavy pan, Soup spoon, Paper-towel-lined colander, Long-handled slotted spoon

  • 6 oz bread flour — about an 8 fl oz cup of unsifted
  • 3/8 tsp salt — a rounded 1/4 tsp
  • 1.5 oz margarine — 3 tbsp
  • 1.5 oz sugar — 3 tbsp
  • 1/2 tsp ground nutmeg
  • 1/2 tsp vanilla
  • 1 egg
  • 1/4 cup evaporated milk plus 1/2 cup water — or 3/4 cup regular milk
  • 5/8 tsp baking powder — a rounded 1/2 tsp
  • Vegetable oil for deep frying

By a cup she means 8 fluid ounces, not 10.

    Method

    1. Sift the flour and salt into a bowl and cut in the margarine, using your hands or 2 table knives, until the mixture resembles cornmeal.
    2. Stir in the sugar, nutmeg and baking powder.
    3. In a separate bowl beat the egg, then add the milk and water, and the vanilla, and mix well.
    4. Make a hole in the centre of the flour mixture and add the liquids all at once, blending with a few swift strokes to keep the batter from becoming tough.
    5. Let the batter sit for about 30 minutes.
    6. While waiting, line a colander with paper towels.
    7. About 10 minutes before the 30 minutes are over, heat the oil in a deep fryer or deep heavy pan to 375 degrees Fahrenheit. Do not fill the pan more than halfway, because the oil will bubble up when the dough is added.
    8. When the oil is hot enough, a small amount of dough dropped in will quickly rise to the surface. If the oil is too hot, the dough will brown too quickly and the centre will not have time to cook.
    9. Slip a soup spoon into the oil to coat it, dip it in the batter, and use a finger or another soup spoon to drop the dough into the oil in the shape of a ball. Make them any size you like, but usually larger than a golf ball and a little smaller than a tennis ball.
    10. If the dough flattens out rather than remaining in the shape of a ball, there is likely too much liquid in the batter.
    11. Cook in batches until they are quite browned on all sides. They will likely turn over as they cook, but use a long-handled slotted spoon to stir and turn them as necessary.
    12. Lift them out into the colander to drain and cool.
    13. Eat them warm or at room temperature.

    Chef Tips

    • The float test is the oil cue: a small amount of dough dropped in should quickly rise to the surface. No thermometer needed.
    • A few swift strokes, no more. This is a batter, and over-mixing is what makes a tough bofrot.
    • Golf ball to tennis ball is the size, and it is bigger than most people make them. The cake version is meant to be substantial.
    • Flattening rather than holding a ball means too much liquid. Add a spoonful of flour rather than persevering.
    • Start heating the oil ten minutes before the rest is over, so both are ready together. Her own timing and it is good practice.
    • Coarser and crisper than the yeast version, just as a muffin differs from a slice of bread. Neither is better — her own verdict is that either way is tasty.
    • The thirty-minute rest is for hydration, not for rising. Nothing visible happens and the source offers no cue, so the clock is all you have here.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Over-mixing the batter.
    • Making them too small.
    • Dropping batter from a dry spoon.
    • Skipping the rest, which gives a gritty crumb.
    • Oil too hot, browning the outside before the centre cooks.

    Cultural Note

    This is the bofrot most Ghanaians under fifty actually eat — baking powder displaced palm wine the way it displaced sourdough everywhere, and the result is a coarser, crisper, larger doughnut. That it comes through Barbara Baeta's Flair Catering is a reminder that Accra's professional kitchens standardised a lot of what is now treated as home cooking.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Best the day they are made. 2 days in an airtight container.

    Reheating: A hot oven briefly.

    Serve with: Hausa Koko, Sobolo, Bissap
