🫕 Abenkwan, the Palm Oil Version

Abenkwan

A widely reprinted version that boils two cups of palm oil for ten minutes — and whose own page describes a soup made from pounded palm fruit it does not contain.

This recipe is published in at least two places and it contains a contradiction worth seeing. Its own description says the soup is prepared from the pulp of pounded boiled palm fruits; its ingredient list contains two cups of palm oil and no palm fruit at all. Those are not the same soup. What the method gives is simple and workable on its own terms: boil the palm oil ten minutes, add onions and pepper for five on high, then tomato, okra, eggplant and a pound of fish or crab, and simmer an hour or more until somewhat thickened, skimming excess oil to taste.

Ingredients

Equipment: Heavy pot, Skimming spoon, Knife

  • 2 cups palm oil — no substitutes
  • 1 cup onions
  • 1 chopped chile pepper, crushed, or 1/2 tsp cayenne pepper
  • 2 cups tomato
  • 2 cups okra, chopped
  • 1 medium eggplant, cut into chunks
  • 1 lb fish or crab meat
  • 1/2 tsp salt

Method

  1. Boil the palm oil for 10 minutes.
  2. Add the onions and pepper and cook 5 minutes on high.
  3. Add the tomato, okra, eggplant, fish or crab and salt.
  4. Simmer for an hour or more, until the soup is somewhat thickened.
  5. Skim excess oil to taste.
  6. Serve with fufu.

Chef Tips

  • Read the contradiction before you cook this. The page's own description says the soup comes from pounded boiled palm fruit; the ingredients say two cups of palm oil. Palm oil and palm fruit pulp are not interchangeable and the soups taste quite different.
  • If you want the soup the description promises, use Palm Nut Soup with tinned palm nut cream, or extract pulp from fresh palm fruit.
  • Two cups of palm oil for six servings is an enormous amount of oil, which is why the method ends with skimming. Expect to remove a good deal.
  • Boiling palm oil for ten minutes before anything else goes in cooks off its raw edge — the same principle as the palm taste disappearing in aprapransa.
  • No substitutes is printed against the palm oil, and on that the source is right: nothing else gives the colour or the flavour.
  • Somewhat thickened is a weak cue compared with the foam-and-oil test in the other version. Judge it by whether it coats a spoon.
  • The okra does the thickening here, since there is no palm pulp to supply body.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Expecting a palm-fruit soup from a palm-oil recipe.
  • Skipping the ten-minute oil boil.
  • Not skimming, leaving the soup unpleasantly greasy.
  • Substituting another oil.
  • Cutting the simmer short of the hour.

Health Benefits

  • Red palm oil is among the richest food sources of provitamin-A carotenoids and vitamin E tocotrienols.
  • Okra supplies soluble fibre, folate and vitamin K.
  • Fish or crab provides lean protein and iodine.
  • Skimming the excess oil removes a substantial share of the fat.

Cultural Note

A recipe reprinted across unrelated sites with a description that does not match its own ingredients is a good illustration of how cuisines get garbled in transmission. The palm-oil shortcut is real and people cook it, but calling it abenkwan while describing pounded palm fruit blurs two dishes that Ghanaian cooks would never confuse.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 4 days refrigerated. The oil sets hard.

Reheating: Simmer gently and skim again.

Serve with: Fufu, Omo Tuo, Rice

That is a contradiction in the published page, not a transcription error, and it has not been resolved or corrected. Palm oil is pressed from the fruit's flesh; palm nut pulp is the strained flesh itself with its fibre and water. A soup built on two cups of oil is a markedly different dish from one built on pulp.
  • NO YIELD is printed. The six servings above is an editorial reading of a pound of fish and is labelled as such.
  • DONENESS CUE: only 'until soup is somewhat thickened', which is the weakest of the documented palm nut soup endpoints. Compare 'no foam, oil visible' and 'until oil accumulates' (the KNUST standardised study cited at Palm Nut Soup).
  • WHY IT IS PUBLISHED AT ALL: this text is one of the most widely reprinted Ghanaian recipes in circulation, appearing on at least two unrelated sites, so a reader is likely to meet it. Publishing it with the contradiction stated is more useful than leaving it out and letting it be followed uncritically.
