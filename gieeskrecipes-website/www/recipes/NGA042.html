🟠 Starch

Usi — starch

One cup of cassava starch, half a cup of room-temperature water and a teaspoon and a half of palm oil, all into a cool pan together — worked until it is translucent and glassy orange.

Starch is the Delta's swallow and it is the most exactly specified recipe in the whole Nigerian record: one cup of starch, half a cup of water, a teaspoon and a half of palm oil. Everything goes into a cool, shallow, non-stick pan before any heat, which is the opposite of amala. Then it is stirred continuously on low-medium while the orange oil melds into the starch in swirls, and the endpoint is visual rather than timed — translucence, what the author calls glassy orange. Three quarters of a cup of water gives a softer result.

Ingredients

Equipment: Cool, shallow non-stick frying pan — not a pot, Wooden spatula or paddle

  • 1 cup starch (cassava starch)
  • 1/2 cup water, at room temperature — or 3/4 cup for a softer consistency
  • 1 1/2 tsp palm oil

Method

  1. Combine the starch, water and palm oil in a cool, shallow non-stick frying pan.
  2. Place the pan on low-medium heat and stir.
  3. Monitor it as the mixture thickens and the orange palm oil colour melds into the starch in swirls.
  4. Pull the cooked mass to the edge of the pan, using the pan side as a leveller.
  5. Keep it free from lumps, scraping the edges against the pan sides.
  6. Continue stirring until it achieves translucence and a glassy orange appearance.
  7. If the bottom flakes too much, sprinkle in a touch of water. If the orange is too light, add palm oil drop by drop.
  8. Serve immediately — a crisp film may form on the pan surface.

Chef Tips

  • Cool pan, cold start, everything in together. This is the opposite of amala where the flour goes into already-boiling water, and the two techniques are easy to confuse.
  • A shallow frying pan, not a pot. You need the pan wall to work against.
  • Pull the mass to the edge and use the side as a leveller. That is the specific move and it is how the lumps come out.
  • Translucence is the cue. The author's phrase is glassy orange, and the swirls go from distinct to blended as it gets there.
  • Water drop by drop if the bottom flakes; oil drop by drop if the colour is too pale. Small corrections only.
  • No time is given anywhere on the source page for any step — this runs entirely on the visual cue. Expect somewhere around ten to fifteen minutes on low-medium, but watch it rather than timing it.
  • Three quarters of a cup of water instead of half gives a softer starch. That is the author's own stated variation.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Heating the pan before the ingredients go in.
  • Using a deep pot, so there is no wall to level against.
  • Stopping while it is still opaque.
  • Adding water by the spoonful rather than a sprinkle.
  • Letting it sit in the pan after it is done.

Health Benefits

  • Cassava starch is easy to digest and gluten-free.
  • Red palm oil supplies carotenoids and vitamin E.
  • No added salt.
  • Very low in fat for the amount of energy it provides.

Cultural Note

Starch and banga is the Delta Sunday lunch, and the author's memoir piece describes first eating it as a child in Sunday best, with grandmother's fresh fish pepper soup thick with tilapia under the noon Isoko sun. It is eaten by pinching a ball between thumb and forefinger, dipping it in the soup and swallowing — and the pairing with banga is so fixed that one Itsekiri source says plainly that banga served with starch announces an Itsekiri table.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Eat immediately. Starch does not keep or reheat well.

Reheating: Not recommended. It goes hard and grainy.

Serve with: Banga Soup, Owo Soup, Fresh fish pepper soup
