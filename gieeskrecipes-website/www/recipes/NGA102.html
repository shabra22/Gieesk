Kunun aya — tiger nut drink

Tiger nuts soaked overnight, blended at one part nut to two or three of water, strained through three sieves — then stood an hour or two so the starch settles and is poured off.

Kunun aya is the tiger nut drink, and the step that separates a good one from a gritty one is the decanting. After soaking overnight, the nuts are blended repeatedly at one part nut to two or three parts water until a frothing slurry forms, flavoured with hibiscus, cardamom and alligator pepper, and strained through progressively finer sieves — three is recommended. It then stands refrigerated for one to two hours while the starch settles out, and the top liquid is decanted and the starch discarded. Most recipes omit that step, and it is the whole difference.

Ingredients

Equipment: Blender, Three sieves of progressively finer mesh, or cheesecloth, Large jug, Refrigerator

  • 2-3 cups tiger nuts
  • Water — 1 part tiger nuts to 2-3 parts water
  • 2 tbsp dried hibiscus flowers (flor de Jamaica)
  • 10-12 cardamom pods
  • 1 tsp alligator pepper (grains of paradise)
  • Sugar, to taste

Method

  1. Find fresh tiger nuts — preferably November or December, from street vendors.
  2. Wash them thoroughly and remove any stones and twigs.
  3. Soak overnight, covered by 1 to 2 inches of water.
  4. The next day, discard the soaking liquid and rinse.
  5. Blend at 1 part tiger nuts to 2 or 3 parts water, repeatedly, until a slurry forms and froths.
  6. Add the hibiscus, cardamom and alligator pepper and blend again.
  7. Pass through progressively finer sieves — three is recommended — or through cheesecloth.
  8. Stand refrigerated 1 to 2 hours to let the starch settle.
  9. Decant the top liquid and discard the starch.
  10. Sweeten to taste, refrigerate, and serve within 24 to 48 hours.

Chef Tips

  • The decant is the technique. One to two hours in the fridge, then pour off the top and throw the settled starch away — this is what most recipes skip and it is why most homemade kunun aya is gritty.
  • Three sieves, each finer than the last. One pass through a coarse sieve will not do it.
  • Blend repeatedly rather than once. You are extracting, not pureeing, and it takes several passes with fresh water.
  • Discard the soaking water and rinse before blending. It carries the bitterness.
  • November and December is the fresh tiger nut season in Nigeria. Dried nuts work but need a longer soak.
  • Twenty-four to forty-eight hours is the window. It is an unpasteurised nut milk and it does not keep.
  • One part nut to two or three parts water is the stated ratio and it is the only one in the kunu family that is cleanly given.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Skipping the settle-and-decant, which leaves it gritty.
  • Straining once through a coarse sieve.
  • Blending only once.
  • Keeping the soaking water.
  • Holding it more than two days.

Health Benefits

  • Tiger nut is exceptionally high in fibre and in resistant starch.
  • Naturally dairy-free, and a good source of magnesium and vitamin E.
  • Decanting the starch lowers the starch load considerably.
  • No added sugar unless you add it.

Cultural Note

Tiger nut is the same chufa that makes Valencian horchata, and kunun aya is its Nigerian counterpart — which is a genuine culinary parallel rather than a coincidence, since the tuber travelled from the Mediterranean and naturalised across West Africa. Its names in Nigerian languages are all different, which suggests it has been around long enough for each to coin its own.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 24 to 48 hours refrigerated, per the source. It separates and should be shaken.

Reheating: Not applicable — served cold.

Serve with: Dakuwa Aya, Chin chin or buns, Ice
