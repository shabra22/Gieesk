🌿 Ofe Oha

Ofe oha — ora soup

Thickened with three or four cocoyams boiled in their skins and pounded smooth — and the oha leaves are shredded by hand, because cutting them with a knife is said to turn the soup bitter.

Ofe oha is thickened with cocoyam rather than yam: three or four medium corms boiled in their skins with no salt for about twenty minutes, peeled, pounded or blended smooth, and then dropped into the soup in small dollops to dissolve over five or six minutes. The leaves are seasonal and they come with a rule — oha is torn by hand rather than cut with a knife, because knife-cutting is believed to make the soup bitter. Uziza goes in a minute before the oha, and the pot comes off the heat for the last two or three minutes.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large pot, Separate pot for the cocoyam, Mortar and pestle or blender, Colander

  • Assorted meats — beef, goat meat, ponmo, shaki
  • 2 cooking spoons palm oil
  • 1 bunch oha leaves
  • A handful of uziza leaves, or 1 tsp ground uziza seeds
  • 3-4 medium cocoyams (ede)
  • Smoked fish
  • Stockfish head (okporoko)
  • 4 tbsp ground crayfish
  • 1 tbsp ogiri igbo
  • 2-3 yellow scotch bonnet
  • 2 bouillon cubes
  • Salt to taste

Method

  1. Soak the stockfish and smoked fish in hot water.
  2. Boil the tough meats and the stockfish until soft.
  3. Boil the cocoyams with their skins on, no salt, about 20 minutes until tender.
  4. Peel them and pound or blend until smooth.
  5. Blend the peppers.
  6. Add the smoked fish and the blended peppers to the meat pot and cook 5 minutes.
  7. Add the palm oil and boil 6 to 8 minutes, until it is fully incorporated.
  8. Add the ground crayfish.
  9. Add the cocoyam paste in small dollops and let it dissolve and combine over 5 to 6 minutes.
  10. Add the ogiri igbo and cook 1 minute.
  11. Add the uziza and cook 1 minute.
  12. Shred the oha leaves by hand and add them. Take the pot off the heat and let it simmer 2 to 3 minutes on the residual heat.

Chef Tips

  • Tear the oha, do not cut it. Cutting with a knife is believed to make the soup bitter, and whatever the mechanism it is a rule people follow.
  • Cocoyam boils in its skin with no salt. Salt toughens it and peeling it first makes it waterlogged.
  • Small dollops, not one lump. Five or six minutes to dissolve is only achievable if the pieces are small.
  • Six to eight minutes on the palm oil before anything else, until it is fully incorporated. Raw palm oil in a finished soup tastes raw.
  • Uziza a minute before the oha. It is tougher and more peppery and needs the extra time.
  • Oha is seasonal. When it is out of season, bitter leaf or another green is the usual substitute but it is a different soup.
  • Achi or ofor can replace cocoyam as the thickener. Ofor belongs to this soup family — it is not used for nsala.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Cutting the oha leaves with a knife.
  • Salting the cocoyam water.
  • Peeling the cocoyam before boiling.
  • Adding the cocoyam paste in one mass.
  • Boiling the soup after the oha goes in.

Health Benefits

  • Cocoyam is a low-allergen starch rich in potassium and resistant starch.
  • Oha leaf supplies iron, calcium and vitamin A.
  • Uziza carries piperine-family compounds.
  • Very low in carbohydrate for a thickened soup.

Cultural Note

Ofe oha is one of the soups by which an Igbo cook is judged, and its seasonality is part of its standing — oha leaves are not available all year, so the soup carries a sense of occasion that a year-round green could not. The hand-shredding rule is the kind of instruction that survives precisely because it is easy to transmit and costs nothing to follow.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 4 days refrigerated, 3 months frozen.

Reheating: Low heat with a splash of water. The cocoyam thickening tightens cold.

Serve with: Iyan, Akpu or fufu, Eba or garri
