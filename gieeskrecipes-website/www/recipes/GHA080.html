🫔 Tubaani

Tubaani — tubani, steamed bean cakes

Two cups of bean powder with a teaspoon of alkali, wrapped in katemfe leaves and steamed thirty minutes — eaten with shea butter and pepper.

Tubaani is steamed bean cake, a northern Ghanaian snack, and the alkali is what makes it work: a teaspoon of ground saltpetre into two cups of bean powder with a tablespoon of salt, mixed with water to a smooth consistency. Spoonfuls are wrapped in katemfe leaves and steamed above boiling water on a splatter guard for thirty minutes. The oldest documented account says the alkali is added to soften the flour, and that it is eaten with shea butter and pepper — which is the detail that makes tubaani northern rather than generic.

Ingredients

Equipment: Mixing bowl, Katemfe or banana leaves, Saucepan and splatter guard, or a steamer, Lid

  • 2 cups bean powder — black-eyed pea or cowpea flour
  • 1 tsp ground saltpetre — or kanwa, see the note
  • 1 tbsp salt
  • Water, to mix
  • Katemfe leaves, for wrapping

To serve

  • Shea butter
  • Ground pepper

Method

  1. In a mixing bowl, mix the bean powder, salt and saltpetre.
  2. Add water and mix to a smooth consistency.
  3. Scoop a spoonful into a katemfe leaf, wrap, and set aside.
  4. Repeat until the mixture is used up.
  5. Bring water to the boil in a saucepan.
  6. Place a splatter guard over the saucepan and carefully arrange the wrapped parcels on it.
  7. Cover and steam 30 minutes, then take off the heat.
  8. Serve with shea butter and ground pepper.

Chef Tips

  • The alkali is structural, not seasoning. The 1968 government account says plainly that it is added to soften the flour, and without it tubaani is dense.
  • The sources name three different alkalis for the same job — ground saltpetre, kanwa described as concentrated salt, and kanwa described as potash. They are not the same chemical and no source reconciles them.
  • Smooth consistency is the mixing cue. Lumps in the paste become hard spots in the steamed cake.
  • Katemfe leaves are the documented wrapper. Banana leaf and corn husk also appear across sources; use whatever is pliable and food-safe.
  • A splatter guard over a saucepan is this author's improvised steamer and it works well — the parcels sit above the water rather than in it.
  • Shea butter and pepper to serve is the northern way and it is worth doing properly. Shea butter is nutty and slightly bitter against the plain bean cake.
  • Documented steaming times run twenty, thirty and forty minutes across four sources. Thirty is the middle and the two that agree on it may not be independent.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Leaving the alkali out.
  • A lumpy paste.
  • Letting the parcels sit in the water rather than above it.
  • Lifting the lid during steaming.
  • Serving it plain rather than with shea butter and pepper.

Health Benefits

  • Cowpea flour is high in plant protein, folate, iron and soluble fibre.
  • Steamed rather than fried, so very low in fat before the shea butter.
  • Vegan and gluten-free.
  • Shea butter supplies vitamins A and E.

Cultural Note

Tubaani is cheap, filling and steamed in leaves, which makes it portable food for a working day in the north. The shea butter spooned over it at the end is the regional signature — shea is the northern fat the way palm oil is the southern one, and the two cuisines taste different largely because of it.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 2 days refrigerated in the leaves.

Reheating: Re-steam in the wrapping.

Serve with: Shea butter and pepper, Hausa Koko, Pepper sauce
