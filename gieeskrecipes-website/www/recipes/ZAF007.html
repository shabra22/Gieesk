🍖 Amanqina

Amanqina — ditlhakwana

Trotters browned with curry powder and paprika then left to simmer for three hours in stock, until the skin slides off the bone and the liquid turns to gel — a texture dish, and unapologetic about it.

Amanqina are the feet of any animal, most often cow, sheep or chicken, and they are not a dish you meet halfway. Three hours of slow cooking turns the collagen to gelatine, and what you get is sticky, rich and faintly wobbly. South Africans are frank that it is an acquired taste and equally frank that cooked properly there is little better. In Zulu custom the trotters are one of the portions traditionally allocated by who you are in the household — unmarried men, in the case of amanqina.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large heavy saucepan with a lid, Tongs

  • 800 g cow or sheep trotters, cleaned and split
  • 4 tbsp canola oil
  • 1 medium onion, peeled and diced
  • 1 sprig bay leaves
  • 2 tbsp mild curry powder
  • 2 cloves garlic, finely chopped
  • 1 tbsp paprika
  • Salt, to taste
  • 2 L chicken stock

To serve

  • Pap or rice

Method

  1. Heat the oil in a large saucepan and fry the onion with the bay until softened.
  2. Add the curry powder and fry for 3 minutes — long enough to cook out its raw edge.
  3. Add the garlic and paprika and sauté for 1 minute more.
  4. Add the trotters and salt, then pour in the chicken stock.
  5. Bring to a bare simmer and cook slowly for 3 hours. The trotters are done when the skin pulls away from the bone with no resistance.
  6. Serve hot with pap or rice, and plenty of the liquid.

Chef Tips

  • Buy them cleaned and split by the butcher. Singeing and splitting trotters at home is a job, not a step.
  • Three minutes on the curry powder before anything wet goes in. Raw curry powder in a long braise stays raw-tasting for the full three hours.
  • A bare simmer, not a boil. A rolling pot breaks the skin up and clouds the liquid.
  • The liquid sets to a jelly in the fridge, which is the sign you did it right. Reheated, it melts back into gravy.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Cutting the cooking short. At two hours the skin is still chewy, and there is no shortcut past the collagen.
  • Boiling instead of simmering, which shreds the skin and makes the sauce cloudy.
  • Skimping on the liquid, so the pot needs topping up and the flavour thins.
  • Adding the curry powder with the stock, where it never gets the dry heat it needs.

Health Benefits

  • Exceptionally high in collagen and gelatine.
  • A good source of protein and minerals from the bone.
  • Naturally free of carbohydrate.

Cultural Note

In Zulu households the parts of a slaughtered animal were customarily allocated by household role, and amanqina went to the unmarried men. Those conventions are loosening but they are not gone, and at a family slaughter who gets which cut is still understood rather than announced.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days; the liquid will set firm. Freezes for 3 months.

Reheating: Reheat gently on the hob until the jelly has melted and the meat is hot through.

Serve with: Uphuthu, Ujeqe, Chakalaka
