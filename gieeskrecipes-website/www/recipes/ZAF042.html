🥪 Kota

Kota — spatlo, sphatlho, skhambane

A hollowed quarter loaf toasted, then packed with cheese, hot chips, split Russian sausage, a thickened tomato relish and two kinds of atchar — Gauteng's answer to a whole meal in bread.

Kota is from the English quarter, and in Pretoria it is a spatlo and in the Vaal a skhambane. The build has a logic: cheese goes in first so it melts against the hot bread, chips next for bulk, then the protein, then the sauce, and the atchar last so its sharpness is not cooked away. Whether the kota or Durban's bunny chow came first is argued in both cities and no one has the evidence to settle it. This version tightens the sauce and toasts the bread, which most street kotas do not, and is the better for it.

Ingredients

Equipment: Deep pan or fryer, Saucepan, Baking tray, Sharp knife

    The bread

    • 2 x 700 g unsliced white loaves, quartered and hollowed

    The sauce

    • 1 x 410 g tin tomato-and-onion braai relish
    • 2 tbsp Peppadews, sliced
    • 1 x 200 g punnet rosa or cherry tomatoes
    • 2 sweet red peppers, sliced

    The atchar

    • 3 tbsp jalapeno atchar
    • 3 tbsp mango atchar

    The chips

    • 3 medium potatoes, unpeeled
    • Oil, for deep-frying
    • Sea salt and freshly ground black pepper, to taste

    The rest

    • 8 smoked Russian sausages, or any sausage
    • 2 x 150 g packets sliced mature Cheddar
    • 25 g microgreens, or shredded lettuce

    Method

    1. Hollow out each quarter loaf and toast both the soft inner piece and the crust at 180C for 5 to 10 minutes.
    2. Simmer the braai relish with the Peppadews and the rosa tomatoes over medium heat for 10 to 15 minutes, until thickened, adding the sliced red peppers for the last 3 or 4 minutes.
    3. Mix the two atchars together and set aside.
    4. Cut the potatoes into fingers — thicker is better — heat the oil to 180C and deep-fry until golden. Drain on paper and toss with salt and pepper.
    5. Roast the sausages until the skins are crisp, then slice each one lengthways.
    6. Assemble in order: a sheet of cheese in the base of each quarter, then hot chips, then sausage, then sauce, then more cheese, and finally the atchar.
    7. Add a small handful of microgreens and cap it with the toasted cut-out bread as a lid, just before serving.

    Chef Tips

    • Cheese first, against the hot bread. It melts into the base and seals it against the sauce.
    • Atchar last, on top, uncooked. Its whole job is sharpness and heat is the enemy of that.
    • Thick-cut chips. Thin ones collapse under the sauce within a minute.
    • Assemble to order. A kota that has stood for ten minutes is a different and worse thing.
    • The street version usually skips the toasting and uses whatever is in the fridge — polony, a vienna, a fried egg, a slice of liver. All of it is correct.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Putting the sauce in before the cheese, so the bread goes through.
    • Cooking the atchar.
    • Using thin chips.
    • Building them in advance for a crowd — they do not hold.

    Health Benefits

    • Substantial protein from the sausage and cheese.
    • The atchar and the raw greens add vitamin C to an otherwise cooked plate.
    • It is designed to be filling and cheap, and it succeeds at both.

    Cultural Note

    The kota is informal-economy food and one of the most widespread food businesses in Gauteng, sold from spaza shops and corner stands. The late Lesego Semenya, who wrote Dijo, did more than anyone to get township cooking taken seriously as cooking rather than as sociology, and this chef-style build is his.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Not a keeping dish. The components keep 2 days separately.

    Reheating: Reheat the components and build a fresh kota.

    Serve with: Extra atchar, A cold drink, Chakalaka
