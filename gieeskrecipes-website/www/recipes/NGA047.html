Ukodo — epuru, yam and pepper soup pottage

Two kilos of goat and four to five litres of water with a pepper-soup spice mix in exact proportions — and a piece of the cooked yam mashed back in as the thickener.

Ukodo is New Year's Day food among the Urhobo, a one-pot of goat, yam and pepper-soup spice eaten for breakfast or brunch. Two things make it worth having. The spice mix is given in exact proportions — eight urheri to four erhe to two gbafilo to two umilo — which is the most precise pepper-soup specification in the whole record. And the thickening move is specific to this dish: once the yam is cooked you take it out, mash or blend one piece back into the broth to bring it to a consistency between yogurt and liquid milk, and return the rest just before serving.

Ingredients

Equipment: Very large pot with a lid, Spice grinder, Slotted spoon, Blender or masher

  • 2 kg goat meat, preferably the more fragrant he-goat, cut into bite-sized pieces
  • 4-5 litres of water
  • 2-3 heaped tbsp pepper soup spice mix — see below
  • 4-5 lemon grass leaves, bruised and tied into a bundle
  • Native salt, about 1/2 tsp or to taste
  • 2 tbsp dry pepper (red chilli flakes), or to taste
  • Fresh yellow chilies, split in half, to be fished out once cooked
  • Nigerian yam, peeled and cut into chunks about 8 cm lengthways
  • 2 tsp ground crayfish (optional)
  • Young lime leaves (optional)

Pepper soup spice mix — proportions, not weights

  • 8 skins or barks of urheri (grains of Selim)
  • 4 seeds of erhe (calabash nutmeg)
  • 2 seeds of gbafilo
  • 2 seeds of umilo

Used in the method but not listed in the original

  • Palm oil, to top before serving

Method

  1. Put the goat meat, water, pepper soup spice mix, lemon grass, salt, dry pepper, yellow chilies if using, and ground crayfish into a large pot.
  2. Bring to the boil, then turn down to simmer 30 to 40 minutes with the lid on, until the meat is halfway cooked.
  3. Add a bit more water and check the seasoning. The pepper soup spice flavour should come through distinctly, with consistent heat from the chilli flakes.
  4. Add the chunks of yam and cook until soft — a knife or fork inserted into a piece should slide in with ease.
  5. Once the yams are cooked, remove them. Fish out the lemon grass leaves and discard them.
  6. Thicken the soup to a consistency between yogurt and liquid milk by mashing or blending a piece of the yam back in.
  7. Check the seasoning — salt, chilli — and adjust, then add the lime leaves if using.
  8. Turn off the heat and set aside.
  9. When ready to serve, put the yam back in and heat through until the soup and the yam are hot.
  10. Top with palm oil before serving.

Chef Tips

  • The spice proportions are 8: 4: 2: 2 — urheri, erhe, gbafilo, umilo. That is the most precise pepper-soup spice specification in any source consulted and it is worth making a batch of.
  • Take the yam out once it is cooked. Left in, it absorbs the liquid and disintegrates, and you lose both the broth and the chunks.
  • One piece of yam mashed back in is the thickener. Between yogurt and liquid milk is the target.
  • He-goat is specified for being more fragrant. It is also tougher, which is what the thirty-to-forty-minute first simmer is for.
  • Bruise the lemongrass by rubbing it between your palms and tie it in a bundle so you can pull it out whole.
  • Knife or fork sliding in with ease is the yam cue. Ukodo yam should be soft but still a chunk.
  • The palm oil goes on at the very end, over the top, and it is not in the original ingredient list. A cooking spoon is about right.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Leaving the yam in the pot while the soup finishes.
  • Skipping the mash-back and serving it thin.
  • Losing the lemongrass in the pot because it was not tied.
  • Cooking the yam to collapse.
  • Adding the palm oil early, where it muddies the clear pepper-soup broth.

Health Benefits

  • Goat is leaner than beef or lamb and lower in saturated fat.
  • Grains of Selim and calabash nutmeg are traditionally used as digestives.
  • Yam supplies potassium and resistant starch.
  • No added fat until the palm oil at the end.

Cultural Note

Ukodo is a New Year's Day dish and that fixes it in the calendar in a way few Nigerian foods are. The Itsekiri call their version epuru. It is breakfast or brunch rather than dinner, it is eaten hot in a pepper-soup broth strong enough to clear the head, and the goat is specified as he-goat because the stronger smell is wanted rather than avoided.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 4 days refrigerated. Keep the yam chunks in the broth.

Reheating: Gentle heat until the yam is hot through.

Serve with: Cold palm wine, Boiled unripe plantain, Starch
