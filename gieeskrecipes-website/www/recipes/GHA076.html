🥣 Hausa Koko

Hausa koko — koko, millet porridge

Millet steeped twelve to twenty-four hours, milled with spices, the slurry fermented eight to twelve hours until it separates, then four volumes of boiling water to one of slurry.

Hausa koko is the breakfast porridge of northern Ghana and it is a double fermentation, documented stage by stage in the Ghanaian research literature. Millet grains steep twelve to twenty-four hours, during which fermentation starts; they are washed and milled with spices in a plate mill; the flour is mixed to a slurry and sieved through cheesecloth; the slurry ferments spontaneously eight to twelve hours, separating into supernatant and sediment, and becomes sour. Then four volumes of boiling water are poured into one volume of slurry, stirring continuously, to give the smooth gel-like porridge.

Ingredients

Equipment: Steeping vessel, Plate mill or grinder, Cheesecloth, for sieving, Fermentation vessel, Large pot and whisk

  • Millet grains
  • Spices, milled with the grain — ginger, cloves and black pepper are typical
  • Water, for steeping, for the slurry and four volumes boiling for the porridge

No household quantities are published. See the note.

    To serve

    • Sugar, or milk

    Method

    1. Steep the millet grains 12 to 24 hours. Fermentation starts during this stage.
    2. Wash the steeped grains.
    3. Mill them together with the spices in a plate mill.
    4. Mix the resulting flour with water to make a slurry.
    5. Sieve the slurry through cheesecloth.
    6. Leave it to ferment spontaneously 8 to 12 hours. It separates into supernatant and sediment, and becomes sour.

    ENDPOINT: the visible separation into supernatant and sediment, plus a sour taste. The finished porridge measures pH 3.51 to 3.99 against 6.02 to 6.53 for the raw grain.

    1. Boil four volumes of water.
    2. Stirring the slurry — supernatant and sediment together as one volume — slowly pour in the boiled water.
    3. Stir continuously to obtain a smooth gel-like porridge.
    4. Serve with sugar, and with koose or masa.

    Chef Tips

    • The separation into supernatant and sediment is the visible endpoint, and it is what tells you the eight to twelve hours have done their work.
    • Four volumes of boiling water to one of slurry, with the supernatant and sediment counted together as that one volume. That ratio is what makes the texture gel-like rather than watery.
    • Pour the boiling water INTO the slurry while stirring, not the reverse. The research describes it that way and it prevents lumps.
    • The spices are milled WITH the grain rather than added later, which is why koko tastes of ginger and clove all through rather than on top.
    • Finished koko measures pH 3.51 to 3.99 depending on the processor, from grain at 6.02 to 6.53. If you have strips, under 4 is the target.
    • Eaten with koose, a fried cowpea doughnut, or masa, a fried sour millet doughnut — and in practice very often with bread.
    • The supernatant has its own name: koko sour water. It is part of the porridge, not something to pour off.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Pouring the slurry into the water rather than the water into the slurry.
    • Discarding the supernatant.
    • Adding the spices after milling.
    • Using less than four volumes of water, which gives a paste.
    • Cutting the slurry fermentation before it separates.

    Health Benefits

    • Double fermentation substantially reduces phytate and raises B-vitamin availability.
    • Millet is gluten-free and higher in iron and magnesium than maize.
    • The low final pH makes the minerals more absorbable.
    • Very low in fat and calories before sugar.

    Cultural Note

    Hausa koko is sold from huge pots at dawn across northern Ghana and well beyond it, ladled into a bag or a bowl with a koose fritter alongside. That its fermentation has been documented in three separate papers by one research group, while nobody has published a household recipe, is a precise picture of which foods get studied and which get written down.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Same day. It continues to sour and separate.

    Reheating: Gentle heat with extra water, whisking.

    Serve with: Koose, Masa, Bread

    • PRIMARY SOURCE: Atter A., Diaz M., Tano-Debrah K., Parry-Hanson Kunadu A., Mayer M.J., Colquhoun I.J., Nielsen D.S., Baker D., Narbad A., Amoa-Awua W., 'Microbial Diversity and Metabolite Profile of Fermenting Millet in the Production of Hausa koko, a Ghanaian Fermented Cereal Porridge', Frontiers in Microbiology, 2021. Samples came from '15 small-scale processing sites'. Quantities and process confirmed across two fetches.
    • THE PROCESS, verbatim: 'The processes in Hausa koko production involve initial steeping of millet grains for 12-24 h, during which fermentation starts. The steeped grains are then washed and milled together with different spices in a plate mill. The resulting flour is mixed with water to prepare a slurry, which is then sieved using a cheese cloth. The slurry is allowed to ferment spontaneously for 8-12 h and becomes sour.'
    • THE WATER RATIO, verbatim: 'most processors boil four volumes of water, and while stirring the fermented slurry (the supernatant and sediment together representing one volume), slowly pour in the hot boiled water to obtain the smooth gel-like porridge.'
    • THAT 2021 PAPER REPORTS NO pH OR TITRATABLE ACIDITY — both fetches returned none found. Its cue, '8-12 h and becomes sour', is a time plus a descriptor with no way to test it, which on its own would NOT clear this collection's fermentation bar.
    • WHAT MAKES IT PUBLISHABLE is the follow-up paper: Atter A. et al. (with Diaz, Tano-Debrah, Parry-Hanson Kunadu, Mayer, Sayavedra, Misita, Amoa-Awua, Narbad), BMC Microbiology, 2024. Verbatim: 'The slurry is sieved and allowed to ferment for 8-12 h during which it separates into supernatant and sediment. Four volumes of boiling water are added to one volume of a slurry mixture (supernatant and sediment) and stirred continuously to obtain Hausa koko.' And the endpoint: 'The pH of the millet grains ranged from mean values of 6.02-6.53 to 3.51-3.99 in the final product, depending on the processors.' That gives a time, AN OBSERVABLE SEPARATION, and an end-product pH range. Its Table 1 covers sites in Tamale, Sunyani, Mankessim, Dodowa and Accra, but those figures were not returned.
    • A THIRD PAPER, SINGLE-PASS and NOT reportable as a household endpoint: Atter A. et al., BMC Microbiology, 2025. Isolate-screening data: 'pH measurements at 4-hour intervals over 12 hours: Initial pH range 6.04-6.07; Final pH after 12 hours: 3.29-5.34', with the 'greatest change in pH occurred within 4 to 8 h'. The medium — slurry or laboratory broth — was not confirmed, so it is recorded rather than used.
    • AN UNRECONCILED TIMING, from the same lead author: Atter A., University of Ghana thesis, December 2021, states that a starter culture 'reduced fermentation time from the normal 48 - 72 h to only 12 h during semi-industrial scale fermentation.' THAT 48-72 HOUR 'NORMAL' DOES NOT AGREE with the 8-12 h slurry stage in the papers above. The two may be describing total process time against the slurry stage alone, but no source says so and the discrepancy is left standing.
    • A FLAT CONTRADICTION FROM THE OLDEST SOURCE, recorded and not resolved: Whitby 1968 says of koko that 'The flour may also be cooked to make gruel or pap, koko, (Hausa). It is simply mixed with water and boiled till it thickens. NO FERMENTATION IS INVOLVED IN THE PREPARATION.' That is directly at odds with the 2021 and 2024 papers, which document two fermentation stages. Either the practice changed, or Whitby was describing a different koko — the GAIN varieties below suggest several exist.
    • KOKO VARIETIES, from the GAIN 2016 Karaga District study, verbatim: 'Moli koko is made from sieved corn dough'; 'Koko salli is made from sieved corn dough mixed with spices'; 'Koko Zimbuli is made from millet flour mixed with T.Z to give it a sour taste'; 'Koko nyina is koko salli mixed with broken boiled corn'. So koko names a family of porridges, only some of them millet-based.
    • SERVING, from the 2021 paper: eaten 'with koose, a fried cowpea doughnut or masa, a fried sour millet, maize, or rice doughnut; however, Hausa koko is often eaten with bread.' It names the supernatant 'koko sour water'. Fafa Gilbert's koose post corroborates the distinction: koose is eaten with 'Hausa Koko, (which is made from Millet) or Koko (which is made from milled fermented corn)'.
    • A SOURCE REFUSED: outtah.com states '2-3 days... When it's bubbly and slightly sour, it's ready' — which is a usable cue, but the site failed the sourcing bar and its 2-3 day figure contradicts every academic source.
    • THE SPICES are not named in the research beyond 'different spices'. The ginger, cloves and black pepper in the ingredient list are the usual Ghanaian koko spices and are offered as such rather than asserted as sourced.
