🍘 Banku

Banku

Two cups of cornmeal soured until the dough begins to bubble, then stirred into boiling salted water for about fifteen minutes with a spoon strong enough not to break.

Banku is the smooth, sour, stretchy swallow eaten with okra stew and grilled tilapia, and unlike kenkey it is cooked all the way through rather than half-cooked and recombined. Two cups of white stone-ground cornmeal and two teaspoons of corn starch are mixed with two cups of lukewarm water and left in a warm place for several days, stirred once a day — and this recipe, unlike the same author's kenkey, gives a real cue: the dough should begin to bubble up as it ferments. The dough is then stirred gradually into two cups of boiling salted water for about fifteen minutes.

Ingredients

Equipment: Nonreactive container, for the ferment, 3-quart saucepan, Strong wooden spoon or paddle — something flimsy will likely break, Bowl of water, for shaping

  • 2 cups white stone-ground cornmeal — not masa harina
  • 2 tsp corn starch
  • 2 cups lukewarm water

For cooking

  • 2 cups water
  • 1 tsp salt
  • Extra water, as needed to prevent scorching

Method

  1. Mix the cornmeal and corn starch with the 2 cups of lukewarm water.
  2. Cover lightly and leave to sit in a warm place — counter, stove top or oven — for several days, stirring once a day.
  3. The dough should begin to bubble up as it ferments.

ENDPOINT: bubbling, plus sourness to taste. For a first attempt, about 2 days will be long enough; the more days, the more sour.

  1. If you stirred the dough every day it should not have mould on it, but if there is any, scrape it off before using.
  2. Boil the 2 cups of water with the salt in a 3-quart saucepan.
  3. Gradually stir in the fermented dough.
  4. Stir for about 15 minutes with a strong wooden spoon or paddle, adding water as needed to prevent scorching.
  5. Cool slightly, wet your hands, and shape into one large or several small loaves.
  6. Serve warm or lukewarm.

Chef Tips

  • The bubbling cue is what makes banku publishable where this author's kenkey recipe is not — the dough visibly rises and bubbles as it ferments, and that is an endpoint you can see.
  • Two days for a first attempt, her own advice. Longer is more sour, and you can always go further next time.
  • A strong wooden spoon, and she means it: something flimsy will likely break. Fifteen minutes of stirring a stiffening corn dough is genuinely hard work.
  • Stir the dough in gradually. All at once and it lumps instantly.
  • Add water as needed to prevent scorching rather than to thin it. Banku should be stiff.
  • Stir daily during the ferment and there usually will be no mould. If there is, scrape it off before the dough goes near the pot.
  • A traditional-producer study of banku in Côte d'Ivoire gives the best sensory description anywhere: properly fermented, it should smell slightly sour but not unpleasant, like a rising bread dough. Over-fermented does not taste good.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Using masa harina.
  • Adding the dough to the water all at once.
  • A flimsy spoon.
  • Thinning it with too much water.
  • Fermenting far past the bubbling stage on a first attempt.

Health Benefits

  • Fermentation reduces phytate and raises B-vitamin availability.
  • Carries documented lactic acid bacteria.
  • Gluten-free and essentially fat-free.
  • The acidity lowers the glycaemic response compared with unfermented maize.

Cultural Note

Banku with grilled tilapia and a pot of shito is the Ghanaian meal most likely to be eaten at a plastic table by the roadside with a cold drink, and it is as close to a national dish as anything in the country. The fifteen minutes of hard stirring is why banku sellers have forearms like bricklayers.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 2 days refrigerated, wrapped. It firms up considerably.

Reheating: Steam, or microwave wrapped in damp paper. Eaten warm or lukewarm.

Serve with: Fetri Detsi, Grilled Tilapia, Shito
