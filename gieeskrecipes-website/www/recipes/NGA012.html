🍲 Egusi Soup, Fried Method

Obe egusi dindin

Ground melon seed goes straight into hot palm oil until every grain turns yellow, stir-fried ten minutes, then let down with stock — done when the oil separates at thirty minutes.

The frying method is the other half of an argument the same source publishes both sides of. Here the egusi meets the palm oil first, with no stock at all: the oil melts, the ground seed goes in, and you mix until every single grain has turned yellow. Ten minutes of stir-frying on low to medium follows, and only then does the stock go in gradually. It uses less seed and more oil than the caking method, and the finish is the oil separating out from the mix at the thirty-minute mark rather than the seed's own oil surfacing.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large heavy pot, Wooden spatula, Dry mill or blender

  • 4 cups (500 g) egusi (melon) seeds, ground
  • 3 cooking spoons red palm oil
  • Beef: best cut and shaki (cow tripe)
  • Fish: dry fish and stockfish
  • 2 tbsp ground crayfish
  • Pepper and salt, to taste
  • Vegetable: Nigerian pumpkin leaves, spinach or bitter leaf
  • 3 small stock cubes
  • 1 small ogiri okpei (optional)
  • Meat or fish stock, and hot water as needed

Method

  1. Put the red palm oil into a dry pot and heat it.
  2. Add the ground egusi when the oil melts or becomes translucent. Mix until every grain turns yellow.
  3. Stir-fry on low to medium heat for about 10 minutes.
  4. Add the meat and fish stock gradually while stirring. Once that is used up, add hot water as needed. If you are using bitter leaf, it goes in here.
  5. Cover and cook 20 minutes, stirring occasionally.
  6. Continue to 30 minutes total, until the oil has separated from the mix.
  7. Add the crayfish and pepper. Stir in the cooked stockfish, shaki and meat.
  8. Add the pumpkin leaves or spinach. Adjust the salt and the water.
  9. Cover and simmer until done.

Chef Tips

  • Every grain yellow before you stir-fry. If there are pale patches the oil has not coated them and they will stay raw-tasting.
  • Wait for the oil to melt or go translucent before the egusi goes in. Cold oil and ground seed make a paste that will not fry.
  • The oil separating at thirty minutes is the cue here — and it is a different signal from the caking method's clear oil surfacing at twenty-five, because this oil is the palm oil you put in.
  • Bitter leaf goes in early, with the stock. Soft greens go in at the end. Bitter leaf can take the full cook and needs to.
  • Stock first, hot water only when the stock runs out. Cold water into a frying egusi seizes it.
  • Less seed, more oil: 500 g and three cooking spoons here against 600 g and two in Egusi Soup, Caking Method. That is the trade-off, quantified by the site that publishes both.
  • The site itself says this method 'requires more palm oil and can cause heartburn'. That is their editorial judgement on their own recipe, and it is worth knowing they prefer the other one.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Adding the egusi to cold oil.
  • Stopping before every grain has turned yellow.
  • Adding cold water during the frying stage.
  • Putting soft greens in with the bitter leaf.
  • Calling it done before the oil separates.

Health Benefits

  • Melon seed supplies protein, magnesium, zinc and plant sterols.
  • Red palm oil is rich in tocotrienols and carotenoids.
  • Very low in carbohydrate.
  • Leafy greens add iron and vitamins A and C.

Cultural Note

That one website publishes both egusi methods, quantifies each differently, and then tells you which it prefers is a small gift to anyone trying to understand Nigerian cooking from the outside. Most of the disagreements in this collection sit between sources that never acknowledge each other. This one is an argument conducted in public by a single author with themselves.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 5 days refrigerated, 3 months frozen.

Reheating: Low heat with a splash of water. The oil will come back into suspension as it warms.

Serve with: Iyan, Amala, Eba or garri
