🍞 Pane Toscano

Pane Toscano DOP

Pane Toscano is Tuscany's famously saltless bread, with a thick, chewy crust and dense crumb designed specifically to soak up the region's salty cured meats, cheeses, and rich sauces without becoming overpowering.

Legend traces the saltless recipe to a medieval salt tax dispute with neighboring Pisa, though historians debate the true origin. What's certain is that the bread's plain, wheaty flavor was built to pair with Tuscany's intensely seasoned dishes — most famously as the base for ribollita and pappa al pomodoro.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large mixing bowl, Banneton or bowl lined with a floured towel, Baking stone or Dutch oven, Sharp blade for scoring

  • 500 g (4 cups) bread flour
  • 300 ml (1¼ cups) warm water
  • 100 g (½ cup) sourdough starter, active and bubbly (or 1 tsp dry yeast as a substitute)
  • No salt — this is intentional and traditional

Method

  1. Combine the flour, water, and starter (or yeast) in a large bowl and mix into a shaggy dough.
  2. Knead for 10 minutes until smooth and elastic. The dough will feel slightly different without salt — a little slacker.
  3. Place in a bowl, cover, and let rise for 3–4 hours at room temperature, or until nearly doubled.
  4. Shape into a round or oval loaf and place in a floured banneton or towel-lined bowl, seam-side up.
  5. Cover and proof for 1 more hour.
  6. Preheat the oven to 230°C (450°F) with a baking stone or Dutch oven inside.
  7. Turn the loaf out, score the top with a few slashes, and bake — covered for the first 20 minutes if using a Dutch oven, then uncovered — for 40–45 minutes total until deeply golden and hollow-sounding.
  8. Cool completely on a rack before slicing — cutting too early makes the crumb gummy.

Chef Tips

  • Because there's no salt to slow fermentation, watch the rise closely — it moves faster than salted doughs.
  • A sourdough starter gives the most traditional flavor and the best keeping quality.
  • Never salt this bread — it's meant to taste plain on its own, and gets its character from what it's eaten with.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Adding salt out of habit — this fundamentally changes the bread and defeats its purpose.
  • Slicing while still hot, which makes the interior gummy.
  • Under-baking — the crust should be genuinely thick and substantial, not soft.

Health Benefits

  • Naturally low in sodium — a rarity among breads.
  • Slow sourdough fermentation aids digestibility.

Cultural Note

Dante Alighieri is said to have referenced the 'saltiness of others' bread' in the Divine Comedy while in exile from Florence — a nod to how distinctive and central this saltless loaf has long been to Tuscan identity.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Keeps at room temperature wrapped in cloth for up to 4 days — it's specifically designed to stay useful even when stale, for dishes like ribollita.

Reheating: Refresh briefly in a hot oven, or use stale slices directly in soups and panzanella.

Serve with: Ribollita, Pappa al Pomodoro, Tuscan cured meats, Pecorino cheese, Crostini Toscani
