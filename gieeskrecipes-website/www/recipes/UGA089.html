Enturire

Sorghum blackened with wood ash at two parts grain to one and a half of ash, malted, fermented two days as obushera, then honey stirred in — very sweet by day's end, and potent the longer it sits.

Enturire is the Bakiga drink of Kigezi: fermented sorghum with honey, built on top of obushera. It is the only Ugandan drink with a documented ingredient ratio, and it is not the one you would expect — two parts sorghum to one and a half of wood ash, which removes the tannins and improves the flavour, aroma and colour. The doneness cue comes from a named Kabale brewer and is a taste progression: very sweet from the honey at first, changing by the end of the day, and growing more potent the longer it ferments.

Ingredients

Equipment: Sack, for soaking, Banana leaves, to cover, Drying mat, Grinding stone or mill, Large containers, A dark cool place

  • Sorghum seeds
  • Wood ash — at a ratio of 2 parts sorghum to 1.5 parts ash
  • 5 litres water, heated then cooled
  • 5 litres water, brought to the boil
  • Honey — no ratio to the sorghum is documented

The Kabale method also uses

  • Ekyanga — sorghum flour mixed with warm water and honey

Method

    The malt

    1. Put the sorghum seeds in a sack and soak them in water for two days, to induce fermentation.
    2. Drain the sack and add wood ash, at two parts sorghum to one and a half parts ash, to remove the tannins and to improve the flavour, aroma and colour.
    3. Cover the seeds with banana leaves or other heat-trapping leaves. They will turn from reddish to black.
    4. Once germination begins, stop it by sun-drying.
    5. Mill the dried seeds into flour.

    The base — obushera obutoko

    1. Heat and cool 5 litres of water. Bring another 5 litres to the boil.
    2. Add the sorghum flour, then gradually add the cooled water.
    3. Ferment the paste two days.

    The honey

    1. Add the honey after that fermentation.
    2. Cover it and let it sit 2 to 3 days to become enturire.

    The endpoint

    1. It is very sweet at first because of the honey. By the end of the day the taste changes, and the longer it stays the longer it ferments and the more potent it becomes. Taste it and stop where you want it.

    Chef Tips

    • The taste progression is the cue, and a named Kabale brewer describes it exactly: very sweet from the honey, changing by the end of the day, and growing more potent the longer it ferments. You read it by tasting.
    • Two parts sorghum to one and a half parts wood ash. That is the only explicit ingredient ratio documented for any Ugandan drink, and it is grain to ash, not flour to water.
    • The reddish-to-black colour change under the leaves is how you know the ash is doing its work.
    • No honey-to-sorghum ratio is documented anywhere. The only guidance is a brewer's line that the more honey, the better the final product.
    • No flour-to-water ratio and no fermentation temperature exist either — the sources say only cool room, dark place and warm water.
    • Three sources give three different final maturation windows: one day from the Kabale brewer, two to three days from Slow Food, three to five days from the encyclopedia account. That spread is reported rather than averaged, and tasting is what settles it.
    • It is described consistently as deceptively strong, because the honey masks the alcohol. No source gives an alcohol percentage, so that is reportage rather than measurement — but two drinks are said to be enough.
    • It keeps up to two months after production, which is unusual for a household brew.
    • Enturire is built on obushera, Obushera, and on omuramba, Omuramba. Make either of those first and you understand this one.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Skipping the wood ash, leaving the tannins in.
    • Adding the honey before the two-day fermentation.
    • Fermenting it in the light or the warm.
    • Going by a clock rather than by taste.
    • Underestimating the strength because of the sweetness.

    Health Benefits

    • Sorghum is high in fibre, iron and B vitamins and is naturally gluten-free.
    • The wood ash treatment removes tannins, which otherwise bind minerals.
    • Honey adds simple sugars and trace enzymes.
    • An alcoholic drink of undocumented and reportedly deceptive strength.

    Cultural Note

    The Slow Food booklet opens its enturire entry by saying that God created the Kiga people, saw that what he had created was beautiful, and gave them enturire for better performance. It is a social, class and gender connector across the Kigezi districts, and the reportage on it is unusually affectionate — men sharpening each other's opinions over it, women keeping it in a secret place beside their beds.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Up to two months after production, per the documented accounts.

    Reheating: Served at ambient temperature.

    Serve with: Karo, Empengyere, Roasted groundnuts

    Adapted From

    • Adapted from the enturire entry, p53 of 'Traditional Products and Dishes: Uganda — From Earth to Table', second edition 2018, Slow Food International — the two-day soak in a sack, the 2 to 1.5 sorghum-to-ash ratio with its stated purpose of removing tannins and enhancing flavour, aroma and colour, the banana-leaf covering and reddish-to-black change, the sun-drying to stop germination, the 5 litres heated and cooled plus 5 litres boiled, the two-day paste fermentation, the honey added afterwards and the 2-to-3-day rest are all as printed there.
    • The doneness cue is from 'Meet Kabale's chief enturire brewer', Daily Monitor, reporting a named practitioner: sorghum mixed with ash for up to 12 hours to change colour then soaked 12 hours more; kept in a dark place three to four days to ferment; the flour mixed with boiled cooled water to make obushera obutoko and stored up to four days; ekyanga, sorghum flour with warm water and honey, mixed in; and a day after the ekyanga is mixed it becomes enturire. The brewer's own words are that it is very sweet because of the honey, that by the end of the day its taste changes, and that the longer it stays the longer it ferments and becomes potent.
    • A third account gives at least 24 hours soaking, three days resting with ash, a three-day bushera fermentation, unfiltered honey plus omuramba, and a 3-to-5-day maturation, with the drink remaining consumable up to two months. The three final-maturation windows — one day, two to three days, three to five days — are reported as a spread and not reconciled.
    • No honey-to-sorghum ratio, no flour-to-water ratio, no fermentation temperature and no alcohol content are documented in any source reached. Mukisa and Ntaate's paper on starter cultures for enturire in Food Science & Nutrition would likely give some of these; Wiley returned 403 on both the abstract and the full text.
    • The description of it as deceptively potent, and as the quintessential Kikiga drink, is Daily Monitor reportage rather than measurement.
