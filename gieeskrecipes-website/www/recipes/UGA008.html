🌺 Malakwang

Malakwang — Amalakwang

Dried roselle leaves boiled until tender, the first water thrown away and replaced, then finished with a whole jar of peanut butter and eaten with boiled sweet potatoes — sour against sweet, which is the entire idea.

Malakwang is the signature dish of the Acholi and Luo north, and it is built on a sour leaf: roselle, the same hibiscus family that gives you the red drink. The sourness is the point and the sweet potato is the answer to it. The one technique that matters is what you do with the first boiling water — this version pours it away and starts again with fresh, which pulls the sourness back to something balanced. It is traditionally avoided at weddings, because a sour dish is read as a bad omen for a marriage.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large saucepan, A second saucepan, for the potatoes, Wooden spoon, Skewer or knife, to test

    The malakwang

    • 300 g dried malakwang leaves
    • 1.5 litres water, plus more as needed
    • 1/2 tin (about 200 g) tomatoes
    • 340 g smooth peanut butter
    • 5 ml (1 tsp) salt

    The sweet potatoes

    • 1 kg fresh sweet potatoes
    • 500 ml water

    Method

      The malakwang

      1. Boil water in a pan.
      2. Add the dried malakwang leaves.
      3. Cook for 15 minutes.
      4. Test for softness by pressing a little of the leaf against the wooden spoon.
      5. Drain the first water off completely.
      6. Add fresh hot water.
      7. Add the tomatoes and boil for 5 minutes.
      8. Add the salt and mix well.
      9. Add the peanut butter and stir until it turns into a smooth sauce.

      The sweet potatoes

      1. Peel the sweet potatoes and wash them thoroughly.
      2. Cut them to the size you want.
      3. Boil in a large enough saucepan for 20 to 25 minutes.
      4. Poke one with a knife or skewer to test for softness.
      5. Serve the malakwang hot with the sweet potatoes.

      Chef Tips

      • Throwing the first water away is the sourness-control step and it is the whole reason this version tastes balanced. Do not skip it thinking you are losing flavour.
      • There is a second, equally legitimate school that does the opposite: boil the leaves in water with magadi rock salt for about 20 minutes, then reserve the water and use four of the six cups as the base you stir the groundnut paste into. That keeps the sourness rather than taming it. Both are real Acholi practice.
      • Sour against sweet is the design. Malakwang without something sweet beside it is unbalanced, which is why sweet potato is the standard partner.
      • Odii — roasted and pounded simsim blended with groundnut — is the traditional paste rather than jarred peanut butter, and it is better. Nobody has published a roasting time or a sesame-to-groundnut ratio for it, which is the biggest single gap in northern Ugandan food writing.
      • Test the leaves against the spoon rather than by the clock. Dried leaves vary a great deal.
      • It also goes with millet bread, maize meal and boiled cassava.
      • Add hot water rather than cold when it needs loosening, so the sauce does not seize.

      Common Mistakes to Avoid

      • Keeping the first water when you meant to make this version, and ending up with something punishingly sour.
      • Adding the peanut butter to a hard boil, which splits it.
      • Serving it with a plain starch and no sweetness.
      • Under-boiling the dried leaves.
      • Salting before the second water goes in.

      Health Benefits

      • Roselle leaves are high in vitamin C, iron and calcium, and are among the more nutrient-dense greens grown in the region.
      • Groundnut paste supplies protein, healthy fat and niacin.
      • Sweet potatoes bring beta-carotene and fibre, and the fat in the sauce helps the body absorb it.
      • Contains peanuts — not suitable for anyone with a nut allergy.

      Cultural Note

      Malakwang is what the north is known for, and the sour-and-sweet pairing with sweet potato is one of the few genuinely structural flavour ideas in East African cooking — not a garnish or a condiment but two dishes designed against each other. That it is kept away from weddings is a reminder that food carries meaning as well as taste.

      Storage & Serving

      Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days.

      Reheating: Warm gently with a splash of hot water.

      Serve with: Boiled sweet potatoes, Posho, Boiled cassava
