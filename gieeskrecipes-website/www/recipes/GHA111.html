🍮 Atadwe Milkye

Atadwe milkye — ataanme nmliche, atagbe mekye

Tiger nut pudding, one part rice to four parts tiger nuts — and do not boil it, it will thicken in a few minutes.

A dairy-free pudding made from tiger nuts, which are not nuts but the tubers of a sedge, and which have been eaten since ancient Egypt. The ratio is the Ghanaian one: one part rice to four parts tiger nuts, so a cup of soaked chufa to a quarter cup of long grain rice. They are ground with a cup of water until powdery and as fine as you can get them, rinsed out with another half cup, squeezed through cheesecloth, rinsed again with two thirds of a cup and squeezed again. The milk is then heated over medium with a quarter cup of sugar, stirring constantly — and not boiled. It thickens in a few minutes and comes straight off the heat.

Ingredients

Equipment: Food processor or blender, Metal strainer, Cheesecloth, Heavy nonreactive pan — stainless steel, Nonreactive bowls — plastic, glass, ceramic or stainless steel

  • 1 cup tigernuts — chufa
  • 1/4 cup long grain white rice, or rice flour
  • 1/4 cup sugar
  • 1/4-1/2 tsp salt, to taste
  • 1 1/2 cups plus 2/3 cup water — 1 cup to grind, 1/2 cup to rinse the processor, 2/3 cup to rinse the cheesecloth
  • A few drops of food colouring (optional)
  • Milk or cream, for serving (optional)

Method

  1. Remove any shrivelled or discoloured chufa — they may just float to the top — then rinse them well several times.
  2. Put them into a nonreactive bowl to soak for several hours or overnight. In a cold kitchen with older nuts this means overnight in the refrigerator; in warm Ghana with fresh nuts it meant 3 or so hours.
  3. Avoid leaving them to soak so long that they begin to ferment.
  4. Drain, rinse once more and drain again.
  5. Put the 1 cup of chufa and the 1/4 cup of rice into a food processor with 1 cup of water.
  6. Grind and pulse several minutes until they are powdery and as fine as you can get them — somewhat like ground almonds. Push the mixture down with a spatula as needed.
  7. Scrape the ground mixture into a nonreactive bowl. Add another 1/2 cup of water to the processor to rinse out as much of the dregs as possible, and mix it in with a spoon.
  8. If you are using rice flour rather than whole rice, stir it in at this point.
  9. Place a folded cheesecloth over a metal strainer set over another bowl. Scrape the mixture in, gather the edges and squeeze as much liquid as possible into the bowl.
  10. Open the cheesecloth, pour in 2/3 cup of water, and squeeze again to force as much milk as possible through. Discard the dregs.

Optional, as done in Ghana: strain the milk once more through a clean silk scarf for a finer result.

  1. Pour the milk into a heavy nonreactive pan and add the salt and about 1/4 cup sugar.
  2. Heat over medium, stirring constantly. DO NOT BOIL IT. It will thicken in a few minutes.
  3. Immediately remove it from the heat and pour or ladle it into serving dishes.
  4. Add a few drops of food colouring if you like, or layer several colours.
  5. Allow to cool and serve topped with evaporated milk or cream.

Chef Tips

  • Do not boil it. That is the single instruction that matters and the author corrected herself on it between her draft and her tested version — it thickens in a few minutes over medium heat and comes straight off.
  • One to four, rice to tiger nuts, is the Ghanaian proportion. For a richer pudding, increase the rice.
  • The shrivelled and discoloured nuts float to the top when you rinse. That is the sorting cue and it saves picking through them.
  • Powdery and as fine as you can get them, somewhat like ground almonds. Under-grinding is the main cause of a thin, gritty pudding.
  • Nonreactive everything — bowls and pan. She repeats it three times, because tiger nut milk picks up metal.
  • Grinding the rice separately in a blender first is more elegant and makes no difference to the finished pudding. Her own verdict after making it both ways.
  • The silk scarf is how it is done in Ghana and it is optional. Her honest conclusion was that she could not reliably tell the difference.
  • Naturally off-white. Colouring it pink or yellow, or layering three colours, is a Ghanaian presentation and comes from Barbara Baeta.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Boiling it, which splits and coarsens it.
  • Under-grinding the nuts.
  • A reactive aluminium pan.
  • Soaking so long the nuts begin to ferment.
  • Skipping the second water rinse through the cheesecloth, which leaves a third of the milk behind.

Health Benefits

  • Tiger nuts are very high in resistant starch, a prebiotic fibre, plus magnesium and vitamin E.
  • Naturally dairy-free, so a pudding the lactose-intolerant can eat.
  • Vegan until the optional serving cream.
  • Lower in sugar than most set puddings.

Cultural Note

Tiger nuts are sold in small heaps at Ghanaian markets and chewed raw as a snack; turning them into a pudding is the celebration version. That the same tuber makes Spanish horchata, and was eaten in pharaonic Egypt, makes atadwe milkye one of the oldest foods anyone still cooks.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 3 days refrigerated, covered. It sets firmer as it chills.

Reheating: Served cold. Reheating splits it.

Serve with: Evaporated milk, Cream, Bissap
