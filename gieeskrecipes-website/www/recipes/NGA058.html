🦴 Nkwobi

Nkwobi — spicy cow foot

Two kilos of cow foot in an emulsion of 200 ml palm oil and a tablespoon of potash — stirred until the oil curdles and turns yellow — served in a wooden mortar.

Nkwobi is cow foot cooked until gelatinous and then dressed in the ncha emulsion. The technique is the emulsion and the cue is excellent: pour the sieved potash mixture into the palm oil in a clean dry pot, stirring with a wooden spatula as you pour, and you will notice the palm oil begin to curdle and turn yellow — keep stirring till all of it has. The clean dry pot is not a nicety; water breaks the emulsion. It arrives in a wooden mortar garnished with utazi slices and onion rings, and it is eaten with a cold drink.

Ingredients

Equipment: Pressure cooker or large pot, Clean dry pot for the emulsion, Wooden spatula, Fine sieve, Wooden mortar, for serving

  • 2 kg (4.4 lbs) cow foot, cut into sizeable pieces
  • 20 cl (200 ml) palm oil
  • 1 tbsp powdered edible potash (akanwu, kaun, keun)
  • 1 tsp ground ehu (calabash nutmeg)
  • 2 big stock cubes
  • 1 medium onion
  • 2 habanero peppers
  • Salt to taste

Garnish

  • Utazi leaves, in thin slices
  • Onion rings

Method

  1. Clean and wash the cow foot thoroughly.
  2. Boil with the stock cubes, the whole onion and salt until well done. Cow foot is slow — a pressure cooker saves a great deal of time.
  3. Dissolve the potash in a small amount of water and sieve it, leaving the sediment behind.
  4. Pour the palm oil into a clean dry pot.
  5. Pour in the sieved potash mixture, stirring with a wooden spatula as you pour.
  6. You'll notice the palm oil begin to curdle and turn yellow. Keep stirring till all the oil has turned yellow.
  7. Add the ground ehu, the blended habanero and salt to the emulsion.
  8. Fold in the cooked cow foot and mix until every piece is coated.
  9. Heat gently until the nkwobi is piping hot.
  10. Serve in a wooden mortar, garnished with thin utazi slices and onion rings.

Chef Tips

  • A clean dry pot. Water in the pot breaks the emulsion and this is the single commonest failure.
  • Pour the potash water in gradually while stirring. All at once and it will not disperse.
  • Sieve and discard the sediment. Undissolved potash is gritty and harsh.
  • The cue is unmistakable — the oil curdles and turns yellow. Keep going until all of it has, not most of it.
  • A pressure cooker for the cow foot. Two kilos of it on the stove is a very long afternoon.
  • Utazi is garnish only, sliced thin. It is bitter and a little goes a long way.
  • The wooden mortar is the serving vessel and it matters — it holds the heat and it is how nkwobi is recognised. Best served with chilled drinks: palm wine, beer or stout.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Making the emulsion in a wet pot.
  • Adding the potash water in one go.
  • Including the potash sediment.
  • Under-cooking the cow foot, which should be yielding and gelatinous.
  • Treating utazi as a vegetable rather than a garnish.

Health Benefits

  • Cow foot is exceptionally rich in collagen and gelatine.
  • Very low in carbohydrate.
  • Red palm oil provides carotenoids and vitamin E.
  • High in protein, iron and zinc.

Cultural Note

Nkwobi is bar food, and the wooden mortar it arrives in is part of the performance — hot, glossy yellow, topped with onion rings and utazi, meant to be picked at slowly over a bottle. The same emulsion dresses goat head as isi ewu and shredded cassava as abacha, which makes the three of them a single family of bar dishes built on one piece of technique.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 3 days refrigerated. The emulsion firms solid when cold.

Reheating: Low heat, stirring, until the emulsion loosens and the meat is hot.

Serve with: Cold palm wine, Chilled stout, Abacha
