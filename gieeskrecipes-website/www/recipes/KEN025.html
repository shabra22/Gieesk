🍌 Authentic Kenyan Matoke

Matoke

Kenyan Matoke is a deeply comforting dish of unripe green bananas or plantains simmered in a fragrant tomato, onion, garlic, and coriander sauce until completely tender and infused with flavour. The beloved staple of Western Kenya's Luhya community.

Matoke is one of the great underrated staples of Kenyan cuisine. Unripe green bananas or plantains — firm, starchy, and almost potato-like in texture before they cook — transform completely during simmering, becoming soft, creamy, and deeply satisfying as they absorb the tomato sauce around them. In Western Kenya, no celebration meal is complete without a pot of matoke on the table.

Heritage & Origin

Origin: Western Kenya — Luhya community

Matoke — unripe green bananas or plantains — has been a staple crop in Western Kenya for generations. In the Luhya community, banana plants are a defining feature of the agricultural landscape, grown in virtually every homestead. Cooking matoke in a tomato and onion sauce or simply steaming it has long been a central part of Luhya food culture, served at family meals, community gatherings, and celebrations alike.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large cooking pot with lid, Wooden spoon, Sharp knife, Chopping board, Measuring cups

  • 8–10 green unripe bananas or plantains — peeled and halved
  • 2 large onions — finely chopped
  • 4 medium tomatoes — diced
  • 4 garlic cloves — minced
  • 1 tbsp fresh ginger — grated
  • 2 tbsp vegetable oil
  • 1 tbsp tomato paste — optional
  • 1 cup (240ml) water or vegetable stock
  • 2 tsp salt — or to taste
  • ½ tsp black pepper
  • 1 tsp curry powder — optional
  • ¼ cup fresh coriander — chopped

Method

  1. Peel the green bananas or plantains — green bananas can be tough to peel. Score along the ridges with a knife and remove the skin in sections. Halve or quarter to make even-sized pieces.
  2. Place the peeled banana pieces in a bowl of cold salted water immediately after peeling to prevent browning.
  3. Heat the oil in a large pot over medium heat. Add the onions and cook until soft and lightly golden — about 6–8 minutes.
  4. Add the garlic and ginger. Cook for 1 minute until fragrant.
  5. Stir in the diced tomatoes and tomato paste. Cook for 6–8 minutes, stirring, until completely broken down into a thick sauce.
  6. Add the curry powder, salt, and black pepper. Stir for 30 seconds.
  7. Drain the banana pieces and add them to the pot. Stir gently to coat with the tomato sauce.
  8. Pour in the water or vegetable stock. Bring to a gentle boil.
  9. Reduce heat to low, cover tightly, and simmer for 30–40 minutes until the bananas are completely tender when pierced with a knife.
  10. Check and stir every 10 minutes, adding a little more water if the sauce is drying out.
  11. When the bananas are fully tender and have absorbed the sauce, taste and adjust seasoning.
  12. Scatter with fresh coriander and serve hot.

Chef Tips

  • Choose the firmest, most unripe green bananas available — any hint of yellow means the banana is starting to sweeten and will not hold its shape during cooking.
  • Soak the peeled pieces in cold salted water immediately — green bananas oxidise and brown very quickly once peeled.
  • Cook until completely tender — undercooked matoke is unpleasantly starchy and chewy.
  • For a richer dish, stir in a spoonful of peanut butter or coconut milk during the last 10 minutes.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Using bananas that are too ripe — they become mushy and sweet during cooking.
  • Not soaking in water after peeling — the banana pieces turn black.
  • Undercooking — the starchy texture becomes unpleasant and hard to digest.
  • Too little liquid — the bottom scorches before the bananas are fully cooked.

The Science

Unripe green bananas are high in resistant starch — a type of starch that resists digestion in the small intestine and acts more like dietary fibre. As the bananas cook in liquid, the starch granules absorb water and gelatinise, softening the texture from firm and chalky to tender and creamy. This is why fully cooking matoke is essential — undercooked starch is difficult to digest and unpleasant in texture.

Cultural Note

Matoke is one of the defining foods of the Luhya community in Western Kenya. Banana plants are a symbol of abundance and continuity in Luhya culture — planted in homesteads and harvested through the year. Serving matoke at a gathering is a gesture of warmth and generosity, and the dish remains central to celebrations, family meals, and community events throughout the region.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Refrigerate in an airtight container for up to 3 days. Not recommended for freezing as the texture changes significantly.

Reheating: Reheat gently over medium-low heat with a splash of water, stirring occasionally until heated through.
