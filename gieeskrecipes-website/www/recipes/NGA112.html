🟠 Puff Puff

Puff puff — bofrot, togbei

Two hundred and fifty grams of flour to roughly three hundred millilitres of lukewarm water, yeast-risen and never refrigerated — nutmeg is not optional.

Puff puff is yeasted dough dropped in hot oil, and the two things that decide whether it works are the yeast and the nutmeg. Yeast is what makes it rise into a near-perfect ball; baking powder gives you something crisp and hard instead. Nutmeg is not a flavour choice — without it, the source says, it will not taste like classic puff puff and will have a flat taste like plain dough. And the batter must never go in the fridge: refrigeration dampens the yeast and it will not puff. Two hundred and fifty grams of flour yields about twenty medium balls.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large bowl, Whisk or wooden spoon, Deep pot or fryer, Slotted spoon, Cling film or a cloth

  • 250 g plain flour
  • About 300 ml lukewarm water
  • Active dry yeast
  • Sugar
  • Ground nutmeg — not optional
  • A pinch of salt
  • Vegetable oil, for deep frying

Method

  1. Mix the yeast with a little lukewarm water and a pinch of sugar, and check that it foams. If it does not, the yeast is dead and the puff puff will come out flat.
  2. Combine the flour, sugar, nutmeg and salt in a large bowl.
  3. Add the lukewarm water gradually, mixing to a smooth batter — thick enough to drop from a spoon rather than pour.
  4. Add the activated yeast and mix well.
  5. Cover and leave in a WARM place to rise. Do not refrigerate.
  6. Heat the oil for deep frying — hot enough that a test drop rises and browns, not so hot it colours instantly.
  7. Scoop the batter with your hand or a spoon and drop it into the oil, letting the balls form.
  8. Fry until golden brown all over, turning them so they colour evenly.
  9. Drain on paper towels and serve warm.

Chef Tips

  • Test the yeast first. Mix it with hot water and see if it foams — if your puff puff comes out flat or akara-shaped, dead yeast is the reason.
  • Nutmeg is not optional. The source is explicit: without nutmeg it will not taste like classic puff puff, it will have a flat taste like plain dough.
  • Never refrigerate the batter. Refrigeration dampens the effect of the yeast so it will not puff when you fry it — yeast needs a warm place to be active.
  • Baking powder instead of yeast gives you crispy or hard puff puff rather than spongy. So does the wrong flour or the wrong batter consistency.
  • Self-raising flour does not work, with one exception: pizza flour, which already contains yeast, so you add none.
  • Eat it the day it is made. Cold leftover puff puff is deflated and chewy — and on selling old stock the source is blunt: you should not sell two-day-old, let alone three-day-old puff puff, you will give people food poisoning.
  • If it comes out oily even with the oil hot enough, the source leaves that one unresolved. An honest non-answer, recorded as such.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Using dead yeast without testing it.
  • Leaving the nutmeg out.
  • Refrigerating the batter.
  • Substituting baking powder for yeast.
  • Frying in oil that is not hot enough.

Health Benefits

  • Dairy-free and egg-free.
  • Nutmeg contains myristicin and other aromatic compounds.
  • Yeast-leavened rather than chemically raised.
  • Modest calories per ball at around a hundred.

Cultural Note

Puff puff is the most widespread street snack in West Africa and it travels under different names — bofrot and togbei in Ghana, beignet across the francophone countries. In Nigeria it is sold warm from a bowl at the roadside and served at every party alongside small chops. It is also the clearest case in this collection of a recipe whose value is entirely in knowing why it goes wrong.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Same day only. The source is explicit that older puff puff is a food safety risk.

Reheating: Not recommended. It deflates and goes chewy.

Serve with: Zobo, Chapman, Hot tea
