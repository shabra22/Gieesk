🌰 Yaji with Grains of Selim

Yaji — suya spice

The kuli-kuli-heaviest blend — a full hundred grams of it against only two tablespoons of ginger, with eight pieces of grain of selim ground in.

This blend goes the other way from the gingery versions: a hundred grams of kuli kuli against just two tablespoons of ginger powder, which makes it nutty and savoury rather than sharp. Eight pieces of grain of selim are crushed in with the kuli kuli at the start — the same spice the Delta pepper soups call urheri and the Igbo call uda — and then garlic, onion, pepper and salt are blended in. It is the only one of the documented blends to put a whole aromatic pod through the grinder alongside the groundnut cake.

Ingredients

Equipment: Food processor, mortar and pestle, or coffee grinder, Dry airtight container

  • 100 g kuli kuli
  • 8 pieces grain of selim
  • 2 tbsp ginger powder
  • 2 tbsp garlic powder
  • 2 tbsp onion powder
  • 1 tbsp ground pepper
  • 1 tsp salt

Method

  1. Crush the kuli kuli and the grain of selim together in a food processor, a mortar with pestle, or a coffee grinder.
  2. Add the remaining ingredients and blend to mix.
  3. Store in a dry airtight container.

Chef Tips

  • Crush the kuli kuli and grain of selim together first, before anything else goes in. The groundnut cake protects the pods from over-grinding and the two combine better.
  • A hundred grams of kuli kuli to two tablespoons of ginger is the nuttiest of the documented blends. If you find commercial yaji too sharp, this is the direction to go.
  • Grain of selim is the same spice as urheri in the Urhobo pepper soups and uda in Igbo cooking. One ingredient, three regional names.
  • Eight pieces. The other blend that uses it calls for ten strands, so the two are close.
  • A dry container, and keep it dry. This blend has more groundnut cake than the others and it will clump if it takes up moisture.
  • No cloves and no stock cube. For a blend with cloves, the only documented one is the author's own peanut-powder version, which she flags as a substitution rather than a traditional blend.
  • If you cannot get kuli kuli at all, the honest move is to say so rather than swap in peanut butter powder and call it yaji.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Adding the grain of selim with the powders rather than crushing it with the kuli kuli.
  • Over-grinding until the kuli kuli releases its remaining oil.
  • Storing it somewhere humid.
  • Expecting a sharp, gingery blend.
  • Treating the peanut-powder variant as equivalent.

Health Benefits

  • Defatted groundnut cake is high in protein and low in fat.
  • Grains of Selim is traditionally used as a digestive.
  • Garlic and onion powder carry organosulfur compounds.
  • No oil, no sugar.

Cultural Note

That the only documented yaji containing cloves is also the one its author marks as a substitution is a small puzzle in the record. The academic literature treats clove as one of yaji's seven canonical components; three of four published home blends leave it out entirely. Either the blogs have drifted from the vendors' blend or the paper is describing something the home cooks do not make.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 6 months in a dry airtight container.

Reheating: Not applicable.

Serve with: Suya, Kilishi, Dambu Nama
