🥖 Tapalapa, Millet-and-Cowpea Baguette

Tapalapa / pain de brousse

A yeasted dough of bread, millet, yellow corn and cowpea flours, kneaded 5 to 7 minutes, risen until doubled, shaped into two baguettes, risen again for ‘another hour or so’ and baked 15 to 20 minutes at 220 °C fan until golden brown and dry on the inside.

Tapalapa is the dense, savoury village bread of the Sahel, shaped like a baguette but heavier, with a strong, distinctive taste. This formula is David Karlin’s adaptation of a recipe on 196flavors.com (credited to ‘Mike’), published on 25 August 2021 as no. 68 of his ‘Around the world in 80 bakes’ and labelled ‘Tapalapa from the Gambia’. Flours, yeast and salt are stirred in a stand mixer bowl, the water added and mixed to a smooth dough; with the dough hook it is kneaded around 5 to 7 minutes, shaped into a ball, covered and left to rise until doubled in size. It is split in two and each half shaped into a baguette, left to rise another hour or so, slashed once down the middle, and baked until golden brown and dry on the inside, about 15 to 20 minutes. THE TWO RISES ARE NOT EQUALLY DOCUMENTED. The first has a volume cue (‘until doubled in size’), and the author warns that the recipe suggested an hour but it took about three in his kitchen. The second rise has a time only and no cue.

Ingredients

Equipment: Stand mixer with dough hook, Baguette tin (optional) or greased baking sheet, Sharp knife for slashing

  • 160 g bread flour
  • 70 g millet flour
  • 160 g yellow cornflour
  • 60 g cowpea flour
  • 12 g dried yeast
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 350 ml lukewarm water (around 40 °C)

Method

  1. Put the flours, yeast and salt in the bowl of a stand mixer and stir until blended. Add the water and mix until you have a smooth dough.
  2. Switch to the dough hook and knead for around 5 to 7 minutes.
  3. Form the dough into a ball, cover and leave to rise until doubled in size. The recipe suggests this might take an hour; it took the author around three hours in a cool kitchen with dried yeast, so judge by size, not the clock.
  4. Split the dough into two and form each half into a baguette shape. Set on a baguette tin or a greased baking sheet. Preheat the oven to 220 °C fan.
  5. Leave to rise for another hour or so. The source gives no cue for this second rise — time only.
  6. Slash a shallow gash down the middle of each stick.
  7. Bake until golden brown and dry on the inside, around 15 to 20 minutes.

Chef Tips

  • First-rise cue: ‘until doubled in size’. Second rise: no cue beyond ‘another hour or so’.
  • Bake cue: ‘golden brown and dry on the inside’; the dough cue is ‘smooth’.
  • The author’s first rise took about 3 hours against the printed hour; do not trust the printed times.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Going by the clock on the first rise.
  • Proofing the second rise with no check at all — the source gives you nothing to check, so poke the dough gently.
  • Underbaking: it should be dry inside.

Cultural Note

ORIGIN DISPUTED, recorded unreconciled: the author calls it ‘from the Gambia’ and makes no claim about Senegal; a reader comment (Missa, 19 June 2024) says it derives from the French baguette and comes from Senegal. A separate Senegalese page (Le Petit Sénégalais 221, 7 May 2014) calls it ‘pain de brousse’, well known to country people, made of wheat, millet and maize flours and sometimes cowpea.
