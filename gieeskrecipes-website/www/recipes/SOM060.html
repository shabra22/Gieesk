🍬 Shushumow

Shushumow — Shuushuumoow

Small ridged rolls of dough, shaped against the tines of an afro pick, deep fried and then tossed in thick syrup that dries to a crystallised shell — the crunch that goes with bitter Somali coffee.

Shushumow is also called kalkals and zinanaande, and its whole character is the ridges. Each marble of dough is flattened against something toothed — traditionally an afro pick, and a gnocchi board, fork or grater will do the same job — then rolled down into a little cylinder. The grooves catch the syrup, which crystallises as it cools into a white shell that cracks when you bite it. It is party and wedding food, and it keeps a week.

Ingredients

Equipment: Mixing bowl, An afro pick, gnocchi board, fork, grater or colander — anything with teeth, Deep skillet, Slotted spoon, Parchment paper

    For the dough

    • 256 g (2 cups) plain flour, plus extra for shaping
    • 80 ml (1/3 cup) canola oil
    • 60 ml (1/4 cup) water
    • 1 large egg
    • 1 1/2 tbsp sugar
    • 1/4 tsp salt

    For frying

    • Canola oil, to a depth of 4 cm

    For the glaze

    • 50 g (1/4 cup) sugar
    • 60 ml (1/4 cup) water

    Method

    1. Combine all the dough ingredients, stir them together, then knead for about 5 minutes into a smooth dough.
    2. Pinch off marble-sized pieces and roll each into a ball.
    3. Flatten a ball against the tines of an afro pick, stretching it into a rough oval about 6.5 cm long and 2.5 cm wide, so the teeth press ridges into it.
    4. Take hold of one long edge and roll it down into a small ridged cylinder. Repeat with the rest.
    5. Heat the oil, 4 cm deep, in a deep skillet over medium heat until it shimmers.
    6. Add the shushumow and stir the oil in circular motions so they move and colour evenly. Fry for 2 to 3 minutes, to golden brown, then drain on paper towels.
    7. For the glaze, boil the sugar and water and reduce to a thick syrup, 4 to 5 minutes.
    8. Add the fried pastries to the syrup and toss to coat every one.
    9. Turn them out onto parchment in a single layer and leave them until the shell hardens.

    Chef Tips

    • The ridges are the dish. Anything toothed will make them — a gnocchi board, the back of a fork, a box grater, even a colander.
    • Stir the oil in circles rather than moving the pieces individually. They colour evenly and the ridges stay sharp.
    • Reduce the syrup properly. A thin syrup soaks in and leaves them sticky instead of setting into a shell.
    • Spread them out to dry in one layer. Piled up, they glue themselves together into a single lump.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Rolling the dough pieces smooth, which loses the ridges and with them the point.
    • Under-reducing the glaze, leaving a sticky sweet rather than a crystallised one.
    • Frying too hot, so the outside browns while the inside stays raw.
    • Tipping them into a bowl while the syrup is still wet, which sets them in a block.

    Health Benefits

    • Portioned small — a tray of about 27 pieces serves six to eight.
    • Dairy-free as written.
    • Most of the sugar sits on the outside as a shell rather than through the dough.

    Cultural Note

    Shushumow appears at weddings, parties and Eid alongside the other festive sweets, and its natural partner is qaxwo, the bitter unsweetened Somali coffee. The sugar shell is doing what the coffee needs it to do.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Airtight container at room temperature, up to 7 days.

    Reheating: Not needed. A few minutes in a low oven re-crisps them if the shell has softened.

    Serve with: Bitter Somali coffee, Shaah Cadays, Xalwo
