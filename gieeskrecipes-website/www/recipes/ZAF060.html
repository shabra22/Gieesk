🥓 Biltong

Biltong

Beef salted at six per cent of its weight for three hours, the salt wiped off dry, two hours in vinegar and Worcestershire, coated in toasted coriander and hung in a gentle breeze near thirty degrees until it has lost half its weight.

Biltong is older than the Great Trek and it is not jerky: jerky is cooked, biltong is cured and air-dried, and the coriander is what makes it taste South African. The method here is the percentage one, which is the most technically specified of the several in circulation and the one with real food-safety logic behind it — a heavy short salt cure that is then removed, rather than a light salt that stays on. The number that tells you when it is done is weight loss, not days: forty to fifty per cent for wet biltong, up to seventy for dry.

Ingredients

Equipment: Sharp knife, Non-reactive plastic or ceramic container — not metal, Kitchen scale, for the weight-loss test, Biltong box, or a cool airy space with a small fan, Hooks or S-hooks, Dry pan and a mortar or grinder, for the spices

    The meat

    • 2 kg top rump or topside, with the fat layer left on

    The salt cure

    • 120 g coarse sea salt (60 g per kg of meat)

    The wet cure

    • 120 ml red wine vinegar
    • 120 ml Worcestershire sauce
    • 60 g honey, or brown sugar

    Optional

    • 6 g bicarbonate of soda

    The spice

    • 40 g coriander seeds
    • 20 g fennel seeds
    • 10 g black peppercorns
    • 4 g dried chilli flakes

    Method

    1. Trim off any connective tissue but keep the fat. Cut the meat along the grain into strips about 3 cm thick, each with fat on one side.

    The salt cure

    1. Salt the strips all over with the coarse salt and leave them for 3 hours, flipping them a few times.
    2. Wipe the salt off with a cloth or paper towel. Do not wash it off with water.

    The wet cure

    1. Mix the vinegar, Worcestershire sauce and honey in a non-reactive container and steep the meat in it for 2 hours.
    2. If you are using bicarbonate of soda, add it for the last 30 minutes, turning the meat at the 15-minute mark.

    Spicing and drying

    1. Toast the coriander, fennel and peppercorns in a dry pan until fragrant, then grind them coarse with the chilli flakes.
    2. Lift the meat out, let it drip, and press the spice mix onto every surface.
    3. Weigh each strip and write the weight down — this is how you will know when it is finished.
    4. Hang the strips with space between them at close to 30C, with a gentle constant breeze. Avoid a strong draught.
    5. Check from day 4. It is wet biltong at 40 to 50 per cent weight loss, and dry biltong at up to 70 per cent. Expect 4 to 7 days.
    6. Slice across the grain to serve.

    Chef Tips

    • Weigh it. Days are a guess; weight loss is the actual measurement, and it is the difference between biltong and a guess that went wrong.
    • Gentle airflow, not a gale. Too much air case-hardens the outside and seals moisture inside, which is how biltong spoils in the middle while looking perfect.
    • Wipe the salt off, never rinse it. Washing it puts water back onto a surface you have just spent three hours drawing water out of.
    • Keep the fat on. It bastes the meat as it dries and it is where a lot of the flavour lives.
    • Cut along the grain to dry, slice across the grain to eat. Reverse either and it is stringy.
    • The salt ratios in circulation vary hugely — anything from 18 g to 60 g per kilogram. The high figure works because the salt comes off after three hours. Do not combine a 6 per cent salt load with a rub that stays on.
    • Bicarbonate of soda is genuinely optional. It takes the edge off the acidity slightly; nobody claims it is necessary.
    • A dehydrator on 35C does the job in about three days for thick pieces, turning twice a day.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Leaving a 6 per cent salt cure on the meat instead of wiping it off after three hours.
    • Drying it in a strong draught, which case-hardens it.
    • Judging it by days rather than weighing it.
    • Using a metal container for the vinegar cure.
    • Hanging the pieces touching each other, so they stay damp where they meet.
    • Drying it somewhere warm and humid, where it will spoil rather than cure.

    Health Benefits

    • Exceptionally high in protein and very low in carbohydrate.
    • A good source of iron, zinc, creatine and vitamin B12.
    • Air-dried rather than cooked, so nothing is added but salt, vinegar and spice.
    • Salt content is high — this is a snack, not a staple.

    Cultural Note

    Biltong predates refrigeration and it predates the trek: drying and spicing meat with salt, vinegar and coriander is how you moved protein across a country with an ox wagon. The coriander is the giveaway — it comes from the Cape's spice trade rather than from Europe, which makes biltong a colonial-era hybrid rather than a transplanted Dutch food.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Vacuum-sealed or wrapped airtight: 1 to 2 weeks in the fridge, several months frozen. Paper bags in a cool dry place for short keeping. No source claims biltong is shelf-stable at room temperature for long periods.

    Reheating: Never — it is eaten as it is.

    Serve with: A cold beer, Droewors, Rugby
