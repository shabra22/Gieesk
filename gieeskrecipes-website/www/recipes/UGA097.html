🧪 Ofuta

Ofuta — ofutaku

Dried bean pods and banana peelings burnt to ash, the ash mixed with water and sieved into a brown liquid — added early to greens so they keep their chlorophyll colour.

Ofuta is a cooking liquid made from ash, and it does a job that cooks everywhere want done: it keeps boiled greens green. You burn dried bean pods and banana peelings, mix the ash with water, sieve it, and the brown liquid goes into the pot early. The Lugbara use it for greens like osubi, the bean leaves, and it is one of three alkaline preparations in their kitchen. Nobody outside West Nile seems to write this down.

Ingredients

Equipment: A fire or brazier, Bowl, Fine sieve or cloth, Storage jar

  • Dried bean pods
  • Banana peelings
  • Water

No quantities are documented

    Method

    1. Burn the dried bean pods and banana peelings to ash.
    2. Mix the ash with water.
    3. Sieve it to produce a brown liquid.
    4. Add it early to the pot when cooking greens like osubi, so they keep their green chlorophyll colour.

    Chef Tips

    • Add it early, not late. It goes in at the start of the boil, which is when it can protect the chlorophyll.
    • Sieve it properly. Ash particles left in are gritty and will spoil the dish.
    • Dried bean pods and banana peelings — both are kitchen waste, which is the point. This costs nothing.
    • It is an alkaline liquid and a little goes a long way. Too much and greens go slippery and soapy.
    • Two related Lugbara preparations do adjacent jobs: a'itipa, A'itipa, is a salt from burnt salty leaves, and magadi is a mineral salt from saline water. Either can be added early for the same colour-keeping effect.
    • The Lugbara cooking philosophy on boiling is worth having in full: boiling takes away the sharp juicy taste, so ofutaku, magadi or a'itipa go in early to keep the leaves green, and sweeteners — avocado, kamura odu which is oil from a tree's bark, and sesame oil — are added at the table afterwards.
    • No quantities are documented for this or for any of the ash preparations. Nothing has been estimated.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Adding it at the end of cooking.
    • Using it unsieved.
    • Using too much, which turns greens soapy.
    • Burning treated or painted material for the ash.
    • Storing it in a metal container, which it will attack.

    Health Benefits

    • Adds potassium and trace minerals from the plant ash.
    • Helps retain the chlorophyll and some of the water-soluble vitamins in boiled greens.
    • Contains no fat, sugar or sodium of its own.
    • Made entirely from kitchen waste.

    Cultural Note

    Alkaline cooking liquids made from plant ash turn up across Africa and in Mexican nixtamalisation, and they are one of the oldest chemistry tricks in cooking. That the Lugbara have three of them — an ash liquid, an ash salt and a mineral salt — and distinguish between them is knowledge held entirely in a language with very little written about its food.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Keeps indefinitely in a sealed glass or plastic jar.

    Reheating: Not applicable.

    Serve with: Cowpea Leaf and Groundnut Soup, Sauteed Garlic Dodo, Pumpkin Greens
