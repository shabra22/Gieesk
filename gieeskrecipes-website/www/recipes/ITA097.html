🥩 Carpaccio di Manzo

Carpaccio di Manzo

Paper-thin slices of raw, high-quality beef fillet, dressed simply with olive oil, lemon, and shaved Parmigiano — an elegant Venetian invention that relies entirely on impeccable meat.

Invented at Harry's Bar in Venice in the 1950s for a countess advised to avoid cooked meat, carpaccio takes its name from the Renaissance painter Vittore Carpaccio, whose vivid reds were said to resemble the dish's raw beef.

Ingredients

Equipment: Very sharp knife, Plastic wrap, Meat mallet or rolling pin

  • 400 g (14 oz) beef tenderloin, very fresh and well-trimmed
  • 3 tbsp extra virgin olive oil
  • 1 tbsp lemon juice
  • 60 g (¾ cup) Parmigiano-Reggiano, shaved
  • Arugula leaves
  • Coarse sea salt and black pepper, to taste
  • Capers, for garnish (optional)

Method

  1. Wrap the beef tenderloin tightly in plastic wrap and freeze for 30 minutes to firm it up, making it much easier to slice thinly.
  2. Using a very sharp knife, slice the beef as thinly as possible against the grain.
  3. Arrange the slices between two sheets of plastic wrap and gently pound with a mallet or rolling pin to spread them even thinner.
  4. Arrange the beef slices on chilled plates in a single, even layer.
  5. Drizzle with olive oil and lemon juice, then season with salt and pepper.
  6. Top with shaved Parmigiano, a scattering of arugula, and capers if using.
  7. Serve immediately, well chilled.

Chef Tips

  • Use only the freshest, highest-quality beef from a trusted source, given it is served entirely raw.
  • Partially freezing the meat before slicing is the key trick to achieving paper-thin, even slices.
  • Serve on chilled plates to help keep the beef at a safe, appetizing temperature while plating and serving.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Using anything less than impeccably fresh beef, given the dish depends entirely on the quality of raw meat.
  • Slicing the beef too thick, losing the delicate, almost translucent texture that defines good carpaccio.
  • Dressing the dish too far ahead of serving, which can cause the beef to lose its bright color.

Cultural Note

Carpaccio was invented at the famed Harry's Bar in Venice and has since become a staple of refined Italian dining rooms worldwide, though the original remains closely tied to its Venetian birthplace.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Best assembled and eaten immediately; not intended for storage given it is raw beef.

Reheating: Not applicable; carpaccio is always served raw and cold.

Serve with: Crusty bread, A light Italian white wine
