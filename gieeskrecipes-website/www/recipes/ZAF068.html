🥖 Gatsby

Gatsby

Cheap tenderised steak bashed flat, braised three quarters of an hour in masala and caramelised onion, piled into a long roll with vinegar-salted slap chips and squashed down hard so the whole thing becomes one thing.

The gatsby was invented in Athlone in 1976 by Rashaad Pandy, who needed to feed the workers renovating his shop and filled a big round loaf with slap chips, polony and achaar, then cut it into wedges. One of the workers, nicknamed Froggy, called it a Gatsby smash after the film that had been on at the local cinema, and the name stuck. The steak came later. Two things make it: the meat is deliberately cheap and mechanically abused, which is the point rather than a compromise, and the finished sandwich gets squashed firmly so nothing stays a separate layer.

Ingredients

Equipment: Meat mallet, or the bottom of a wine bottle, Large frying pan with a lid, Deep pan or fryer, Thermometer (useful for the two-stage chips)

    The masala steak

    • 500 g cheap tenderised steak
    • 10 ml (2 tsp) meat tenderiser
    • 15 ml (1 tbsp) BBQ spice
    • 30 ml (2 tbsp) oil
    • 2 medium onions, sliced into rings
    • 5 ml (1 tsp) crushed ginger
    • 7.5 ml (1 1/2 tsp) crushed garlic
    • 15 ml (1 tbsp) hot leaf masala
    • 5 ml (1 tsp) paprika
    • 5 ml (1 tsp) turmeric
    • A pinch of chilli powder, or more
    • 22 ml (1 1/2 tbsp) brown vinegar
    • 5 ml (1 tsp) sugar
    • 30 ml (2 tbsp) tomato paste (optional)
    • 2 bay leaves
    • Water, as needed

    The slap chips

    • 1 kg large potatoes, cut into thick chips
    • Vegetable oil, for frying
    • 60 ml (1/4 cup) brown vinegar
    • 5 ml (1 tsp) salt
    • 5 ml (1 tsp) chilli powder

    To assemble

    • 2 long French loaves, firm and fresh but not too crusty
    • 1 iceberg lettuce, finely shredded
    • 2 tomatoes, sliced
    • Salt and pepper, to taste

    Method

    1. An hour ahead, bash the steaks flat with a mallet. Sprinkle generously with the meat tenderiser and BBQ spice and leave them to mellow for about an hour.

    The steak

    1. Heat the oil and fry the onion rings slowly over medium heat until deeply browned and caramelised. Lift them out onto paper towel.
    2. Turn the heat up high and fry the steaks on both sides until browned. Do not cook them through at this stage.
    3. Add the ginger, garlic, leaf masala, paprika, turmeric, chilli, vinegar, sugar, tomato paste and bay leaves, and return the caramelised onions.
    4. Reduce the heat to very low, cover, and simmer gently for 45 to 50 minutes, adding water so the steaks stay swimming in liquid.

    The chips

    1. Soak the cut chips in cold water for 20 minutes, then drain and pat thoroughly dry.
    2. Fry at 150C for 6 to 8 minutes until soft but pale. Lift out and rest for 10 minutes.
    3. Fry again at 180C for 2 to 3 minutes until crisp and golden. Season immediately with the vinegar, salt and chilli powder.

    Assembly

    1. Lift the steaks out and turn the heat up to reduce the sauce to a syrupy consistency.
    2. Slice the loaves lengthwise and smear the reduced sauce on both halves.
    3. Layer the shredded lettuce, then the tomato seasoned with salt and pepper, then the sliced steak.
    4. Crown with the hot chips and the remaining pan sauce.
    5. Close it and squash down firmly on the whole sandwich, to press everything into one cohesive thing.
    6. Cut into portions and eat immediately.

    Chef Tips

    • Use cheap tenderised steak and bash it. Rump is a waste of money here — the texture of mechanically abused meat braised in masala is the dish, and a good cut just tastes out of place.
    • The squash at the end is not a joke. It is what turns a stack of ingredients into a gatsby.
    • Two-stage chips are worth the trouble: 150C to cook them through, a ten-minute rest, then 180C to crisp them. Single-fried chips go soft under the sauce within minutes.
    • Season the chips the second they come out, while the surface is still hot enough to take the vinegar.
    • Firm bread, but not a crusty artisan loaf. Too crusty and it shreds your mouth and refuses to compress.
    • Caramelise the onions properly and separately before the meat goes in. Rushed onions leave the sauce thin.
    • It is meant to be shared. A gatsby is sized for four and sold by the quarter.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Using an expensive cut of steak.
    • Single-frying the chips.
    • Cooking the steak through before the braise.
    • Assembling it and not compressing it.
    • Letting it stand. A gatsby is eaten immediately or it is a soggy loaf.

    Health Benefits

    • Steak provides protein, iron and B12.
    • The masala's turmeric, ginger and garlic are all worth having.
    • It is a very large fried-potato sandwich, which is the honest summary.

    Cultural Note

    The gatsby is Cape Flats food and it carries the community's history in its shape: invented out of necessity in Athlone the year of the Soweto uprising, named after a film showing at the local cinema, built to feed several people cheaply from one loaf. The original filling was polony and achaar; the masala steak version people now think of as definitive came later.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: None. This is an immediate dish.

    Reheating: The masala steak keeps 3 days and reheats well; the sandwich must be built fresh.

    Serve with: Peri-Peri Sauce, Mango atchar, A cold drink
