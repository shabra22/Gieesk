🌶️ Ofada Stew

Ofada stew — ayamase

Forty unripe habaneros to two green peppers — the heat-to-sweet ratio inverted — with the puree boiled bone dry before it ever meets the oil.

This is the hot one. Where the other ayamase recipes build on five or six green bell peppers with three scotch bonnets for heat, this one uses forty unripe habaneros against just two tatashe, which flips the dish from a sweet green stew with a bite into something the site compares to vindaloo. The technique is different too: the blended peppers are cooked in a separate pot on high heat until all the water dries up, and only then do they go into the bleached oil to fry dry a second time. Twelve minutes on low to medium is the bleaching time, with the oil going clear like vegetable oil.

Ingredients

Equipment: Two pots, one clean and dry for the oil, Blender, Dry mill, Extractor fan or open window

  • 40 pcs unripe habanero peppers
  • 2 green tatashe peppers or green bell peppers
  • 1 locust bean seasoning — see note
  • 20 cl red palm oil, at least
  • 1 big onion
  • 1 handful crayfish
  • 850 g assorted meat and fish — beef, shaki, dry fish, stockfish
  • Stock cube
  • Salt to taste

Method

  1. Wash and blend the peppers with the onion, deseeding the tatashe or bell pepper.
  2. Grind the crayfish and the locust bean seasoning in a dry mill.
  3. Cook the meat and fish with a stock cube until done.
  4. Pour the pepper blend into a separate pot and cook on high heat till all the water dries up.
  5. In a clean dry pot, in a well-ventilated kitchen, bleach the palm oil until it turns clear. It should look like vegetable oil when done — about 12 minutes on low to medium heat.
  6. Cool the oil slightly, then add the boiled puree and fry till all the water has dried from the pepper.
  7. Add the crayfish, the locust bean seasoning, and the meat and fish. Stir.
  8. Salt to taste, simmer, and serve.

Chef Tips

  • Forty habaneros is not a typo and it is not for everyone. If you want the sweeter green stew, use Ayamase or Ayamase with Boiled Eggs instead.
  • Ventilate, and do not leave the pot. The source is explicit: the oil will catch fire if overheated. Its other advice — turn off your smoke alarm — is worth ignoring.
  • The puree is dried twice: once alone on high heat, then again in the oil. That double drying is what concentrates it.
  • Clear, like vegetable oil, is this source's endpoint. Note that another named source describes the endpoint as the oil 'darkening slightly', which is not the same state at all.
  • Cool the oil slightly before the puree goes in. Hot oil and wet puree spits violently.
  • Deseed the tatashe but not the habaneros. The sweet pepper's seeds are bitter; the habanero's are the point.
  • '1 locust bean seasoning' is printed with no unit. Two heaped tablespoons of iru is the figure other named sources use for a comparable batch.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Scaling the habaneros down without adjusting anything else, which leaves it thin.
  • Adding the puree to oil that is still at bleaching temperature.
  • Skipping the separate dry-off and putting wet puree into the oil.
  • Bleaching in a pot with any water in it.
  • Working with the kitchen closed.

Cultural Note

Ofada stew takes its name from the rice, not the other way round — the sauce itself is ayamase, and ofada is a community in Obafemi Owode Local Government Area in Ogun State whose name became the blanket trade term for almost all locally produced Nigerian rice. One named source argues it should have protected designation of origin status so that real Ofada rice can be told apart from the rest.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 1 week refrigerated, 3 months frozen.

Reheating: Gentle heat on the stove.

Serve with: Ofada rice, Boiled white rice, Eko
