🍽️ Kikomando

Kikomando

Chapati chopped into strips and covered with beans simmered in palm oil until the stock reduces by half and a creamy layer rises — two chapatis to a portion of beans, which is how it is sold.

Kikomando is chapati cut up with beans over it, and it is the cheapest filling meal in Kampala. It was created in the early 2000s and caught on among builders and site workers because it is fast and it fills you up. The name is the interesting part: it is credited to the musician Bobi Wine, who sang about the combination, and it refers to the 1985 film Commando — eat it and you are strong like Schwarzenegger. The bean stew is the whole craft, and the secret is not throwing away the water the beans were boiled in.

Ingredients

Equipment: Saucepan, Sharp knife or scissors

    The beans

    • 480 ml (2 cups) beans, boiled and drained
    • 480 ml (2 cups) bean stock — the water they were boiled in
    • 2 large tomatoes, diced
    • 1 large onion, diced
    • 1 clove garlic, crushed
    • Palm oil, the orange kind
    • Salt and pepper

    To assemble

    • 8 chapatis — two per person

    Method

    1. Put the pan on high heat, add the palm oil and the onions, and let the onions fry until translucent.
    2. Add the tomatoes and let them cook until tender, stirring so they do not burn.
    3. Add the beans, salt and pepper, and keep stirring.
    4. Pour in the bean stock and bring it to the boil.
    5. Once it has boiled, reduce the heat and let it simmer down until the liquid has reduced by about half and thickened well.

    Assemble

    1. Chop the chapatis into strips.
    2. Pile the strips on a plate and spoon the beans over the top.
    3. Serve hot.

    Chef Tips

    • Keep the bean water. It is the thickener, and the stew is thin and dull without it — this is the single thing that separates a good kikomando from a bad one.
    • A slow simmer is what produces the creamy layer on top. Rushed beans never get it.
    • Boil the beans properly soft before they go into the stew.
    • Orange palm oil, not a neutral oil. It is what gives it the colour and the particular richness.
    • Two chapatis to one portion of beans is the street ratio, and it is the best guide to proportions there is.
    • Sliced avocado is the original addition and it is still the best one.
    • Upmarket versions add meat stew, gravy, chicken or liver. Some people eat it for breakfast with tea.
    • Nobody has written this down properly, which is funny for a dish this common — the bean stew below is quantified but the chapati half almost never is.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Draining the bean stock away.
    • Boiling the stew hard instead of simmering it down.
    • Using undercooked beans.
    • A neutral oil instead of palm oil.
    • Cutting the chapati long before serving so it goes stiff.

    Health Benefits

    • Beans are high in plant protein, fibre, folate and iron.
    • Entirely plant-based and genuinely cheap, which is the point of it.
    • Very filling for the cost — it was designed to carry a day of physical work.
    • The chapati brings most of the fat; the beans do the nutritional work.

    Cultural Note

    Kikomando is class-marked food and openly so: it fed builders and students, and the name is a joke about strength. That it is better documented than the rolex — with a coined origin, a named musician, a film reference and even a vendor claiming to have started it — is a small irony, given nobody has bothered to write down how to make it properly.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: The bean stew keeps 3 days refrigerated. Cut the chapati fresh.

    Reheating: Warm the beans with a splash of water; refresh the chapati in a dry pan.

    Serve with: Avocado, Ugandan tea, Chilli

    Adapted From

    • The bean stew is adapted from Sophia Musoki, 'Chapati and Beans', A Kitchen In Uganda (6 February 2018), where she names the dish kikomando — the quantities, the keep-the-stock rule and the slow-simmer-for-cream note are hers. She explicitly declined to publish the chapati half and never did.
    • The two-chapatis-to-one-portion street ratio, the early-2000s origin among builders and site workers, and the pricing history are from Daily Monitor's reporting on the dish.
    • The name's attribution to the musician Bobi Wine and the reference to the 1985 film Commando are documented in the Daily Monitor and elsewhere. A named vendor, Geoffrey Isma, claims to have begun selling chapati and beans in Ntinda in 2000; that is his own claim rather than an established fact.
