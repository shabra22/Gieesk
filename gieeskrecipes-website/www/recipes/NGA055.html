🥗 Abacha na Ugba

Abacha — African salad, ncha

Shredded dried cassava and fermented oil bean dressed in an emulsion of thirty centilitres of palm oil and two tablespoons of potash — mixed entirely off the stove.

Abacha is dried shredded cassava and it has already been through a process before you meet it: the tubers are cooked, sliced with a special grater, soaked in cold water overnight, washed thoroughly and sun-dried. To make the salad you rehydrate it, then dress it with the ncha emulsion — thirty centilitres of palm oil into which potash water is poured, carefully leaving the sediment behind, until the oil turns into a yellow paste. Everything is mixed off the stove, because any heat or water in the pot will break the emulsion. An optional reheat at the end is the only time it sees a flame.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large bowl, Wooden spatula, Sieve, Colander

  • 3 handfuls abacha (dried shredded cassava)
  • 2 cups ugba (ukpaka)
  • 30 cl red palm oil
  • 2 tbsp powdered potash (food tenderiser), dissolved in approximately 1 cup cold water
  • Fish — mackerel, dry fish or stockfish
  • 1 large onion
  • Salt and dry pepper, to taste
  • 4 tbsp ground crayfish
  • 2 stock cubes
  • 1 tsp ground ehu seeds

Method

  1. Soak the abacha in cold water to rehydrate it, then drain thoroughly in a colander.
  2. Dissolve the potash in approximately 1 cup of cold water and let the sediment settle.
  3. Pour the red palm oil into a clean dry bowl.
  4. Pour the water from the potash mixture into the oil, making sure not to pour the sediments.
  5. Stir very well and you'll see the oil turn into a yellow paste.
  6. Add the ground crayfish, ground ehu seeds, stock cubes, dry pepper and salt, and mix.
  7. Fold in the ugba, the drained abacha, the sliced onion and the fish.
  8. Mix thoroughly, all off the stove.
  9. If you want it warm, reheat to your desired temperature at medium heat.

Chef Tips

  • Everything off the stove. The emulsion forms cold and heat is what breaks it — the optional reheat comes after it is fully mixed.
  • Leave the sediment behind. The instruction is explicit: pour the water from the potash mixture, making sure not to pour the sediments.
  • The yellow paste is the cue. Red oil going opaque yellow and thickening is the whole technique.
  • Drain the abacha properly. Wet abacha dilutes the emulsion and the dressing slides off.
  • Two tablespoons of potash to 30 cl of oil is a much higher alkali load than the ncha recipe's 2 teaspoons to half a cup. Start lower and build if you are unsure.
  • Ehu is calabash nutmeg — the same spice as ehuru — and a teaspoon of it is what makes abacha taste of abacha.
  • Garden eggs, sliced, and a handful of utazi are the usual additions if you have them.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Mixing it over heat.
  • Tipping the potash sediment into the oil.
  • Using abacha that is still wet.
  • Adding the potash water all at once.
  • Leaving out the ehu, which flattens the whole dish.

Health Benefits

  • Fermented oil bean is high in protein and B vitamins.
  • Red palm oil supplies carotenoids and vitamin E.
  • No cooking, so the nutrients in the fresh additions survive.
  • Gluten-free.

Cultural Note

Abacha is bar food and market food, sold in a bowl with a cold drink and eaten at any hour. It is also one of the few genuinely raw-assembled dishes in the Nigerian repertoire — nothing is cooked at the point of making it, because the whole thing depends on an emulsion that heat would destroy. The cooking happened days earlier, when the cassava was boiled, sliced, soaked and dried.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 2 days refrigerated. The emulsion will separate; stir it back.

Reheating: Gentle warmth only, after mixing. Do not cook it.

Serve with: Cold palm wine, Ugba, Chilled stout
