🍲 Mogodu

Mogodu — ulusu — mala mogodu

Tripe soaked an hour in coarse salt, rinsed hard, simmered three hours in plain water until tender, then finished in a pan of onion, chilli, garam masala and tomato with amasi stirred through.

Mogodu is the Sunday dish of the townships and the standing order at half the taverns in Gauteng, and it is also ulusu in isiZulu and isiXhosa households. Everything turns on two things. The cleaning, where every cook has a method and they genuinely disagree — salt, vinegar, scraping with the back of a knife, or plain mechanical washing. And the three hours, which is the one number nobody argues about. Cutting the fat away is the single step that prevents the commonest complaint, which is a pot that turns to jelly as it cools.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large bowl, for the soak, Saucepan with a lid, Large frying pan, A knife with a decent edge, for scraping

    The tripe

    • 1 kg tripe
    • Coarse salt, for sprinkling and soaking
    • 2 litres water, for soaking
    • 2 litres water, for cooking
    • Salt and pepper, to taste

    The pan

    • 60 ml (4 tbsp) ghee or vegetable oil
    • 1 medium onion, roughly chopped
    • 1 large red chilli, finely chopped
    • 5 ml (1 tsp) garlic, crushed
    • 10 ml (2 tsp) garam masala
    • 5 ml (1 tsp) paprika
    • 2 bay leaves
    • 1 x 400 g can chopped tomatoes

    To finish

    • 250 ml (1 cup) amasi or plain yoghurt
    • 10 ml (2 tsp) parsley, chopped

    Method

    1. Put the tripe in a large bowl, sprinkle coarse salt right through it, and add 2 litres of water. Soak for about an hour.
    2. Scrape the surfaces with the back of a knife or a hard brush, and cut away the visible fat.
    3. Rinse thoroughly under cold running water until the water runs clear.
    4. Cut the cleaned tripe into bite-sized pieces.
    5. Put it in a saucepan, season with salt and pepper, and add the remaining 2 litres of water.
    6. Bring slowly to the boil over low heat and cook for 3 hours, until tender. Drain.

    The sauce

    1. Heat the ghee or oil in a large pan over medium heat.
    2. Add the onion, chilli and garlic and cook until the onion softens.
    3. Stir in the garam masala, paprika and bay leaves, season, and cook over low heat for a minute.
    4. Add the canned tomatoes and cook for 2 minutes.
    5. Stir in the amasi or yoghurt and simmer over low heat until the sauce thickens.
    6. Add the cooked tripe and simmer for 10 minutes.
    7. Garnish with parsley and serve with pap, samp or dombolo.

    Chef Tips

    • Cut the fat away. This is the step that stops mogodu congealing when it cools, which is the complaint people actually have about it.
    • Three hours. Every source lands between two and a half and three and a half, and under-cooked tripe is rubber.
    • The rinse water running clear is the practical test for clean. There is no fixed standard and that is the one every cook uses.
    • Check for grit, small stones and grass in the lining. It came out of a rumen and it is not always as clean as it looks.
    • Shop-bought tripe is worth par-boiling for 10 minutes, draining and washing before the real cook.
    • Some cooks soak in water with salt and vinegar, others in coarse salt alone, others just wash it hard. No source gives a vinegar ratio, so a good splash in the soaking water is as precise as it gets.
    • Squeeze the pieces as you rinse them — it pushes out what the water alone leaves behind.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Leaving the fat on.
    • Cutting the three hours short.
    • A cursory rinse.
    • Boiling it hard after the amasi goes in, which splits it.
    • Seasoning the water heavily before you know how salty the tripe is after its salt soak.

    Health Benefits

    • Tripe is high in protein and very low in fat once trimmed.
    • A good source of vitamin B12, selenium and zinc.
    • Amasi adds live cultures and calcium.
    • Offal makes full use of the animal, which is both economical and the older way of eating.

    Cultural Note

    Mogodu is a Sunday and a payday dish, and it is a marker: the tavern that does good mogodu has a reputation. It is one of several offal dishes — with skopo, mala and amanqina — that were dismissed as poor food by people who did not eat them, and are now on restaurant menus in Johannesburg at prices that would surprise the grandmothers who perfected them.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days. Better on the second day.

    Reheating: Warm gently on the hob with a splash of water.

    Serve with: Pap or Uphuthu, Dombolo or Ujeqe, Samp
