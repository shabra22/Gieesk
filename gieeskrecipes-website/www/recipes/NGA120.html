🧂 Kaun

Kaun — akanwu, kanwa, akaun, keun, ngu, trona

The alkali behind nkwobi, isi ewu, abacha, owo, ukwa and ewedu — how to prepare it, what it actually is, and why it should not be used freely.

Almost nothing in the southeastern or Delta repertoire works without this, and almost nobody explains it. Kaun is a mineral lump that gets pounded, dissolved in a little water and sieved, and the clear liquid is what goes into the pot — never the sediment. It emulsifies palm oil into the yellow sauce that defines nkwobi and isi ewu, it tenderises breadfruit and tough meat, and it keeps ewedu green. Two things are worth knowing before you use it: the English name is wrong, and the dose matters more than most sources admit.

Ingredients

Equipment: Mortar and pestle, Small bowl, Fine sieve, Spoon

  • A small lump of kaun, or powdered edible potash
  • Water, a small quantity — see the dish-specific amounts below

Documented quantities, by dish

  • Nkwobi: 1 tsp dissolved in a cup of the cow leg stock
  • Isi ewu: 1 tbsp dissolved in about 4 tbsp water
  • Ncha for abacha: 2 tsp dissolved in 1/4 cup water
  • Ukwa: 7 g to 700 g of breadfruit, i.e. 1% by weight
  • Ewedu: 1 tsp, boiled in the water before the leaves
  • Okra (ila asepo): 1/2 tsp, at the start
  • Owo: a teaspoon's worth of pounded native salt
  • Masa: 1/4 tsp ground akaun in the batter

Method

  1. Pound the lump of kaun in a mortar into a powder.
  2. Put the powder in a small bowl and add a small quantity of water. Stir.
  3. Let it stand a few minutes. It will not all dissolve — some particles will settle at the bottom.
  4. Pour or sieve off the CLEAR LIQUID and leave the sediment behind. The sediment is gritty and harsh and should never go into food.
  5. Use the liquid gradually, in the quantity your dish calls for, and stop when you reach the effect you want.
  6. For an emulsion, add it slowly to palm oil in a clean dry vessel, stirring continuously, until the oil curdles and turns yellow and thick.
  7. For tenderising or for greens, add it to the cooking water at the start.
  8. If you are substituting baking soda, mix it with a small quantity of water the same way — but add it near the END of cooking rather than the start.

Chef Tips

  • THE NAME IS WRONG, and it is worth knowing. Kaun is 'actually erroneously called potash' — it is chemically a mineral called TRONA, described as 'a crude mixture of various salts and other components': salts of sodium (Na2CO3), clay, and metals including potassium, silicon, magnesium, calcium, iron, aluminium and titanium. 'Potash' implies potassium carbonate; this is principally a SODIUM carbonate mineral, the same family as washing soda. It is also a crude mineral carrying clay and metal impurities, not a refined food additive.
  • SAFETY, and this is the part that belongs beside every recipe using it: 'A high amount of Kaun when consumed can induce uterine contraction and therefore lead to abortion, especially in early stages of pregnancy.' The same source notes it may contain impurities that 'may have adverse effects on the human body if consumed in large quantities.' Use the documented quantities, not more, and if you are pregnant leave it out.
  • Always sieve and discard the sediment. Every source that gives any technical instruction says this, and it is the most consistent advice about kaun anywhere.
  • Add it gradually and stop at the effect. For an emulsion the target is thick and not fluid; too much makes it soapy and will not reverse.
  • A clean DRY vessel for emulsions. Water in the pot breaks the emulsion before it forms, which is why abacha is mixed off the stove.
  • Baking soda, ngu (burnt palm fruit stalk) and dried plantain-peel ash water are all offered by various sources as substitutes. They are chemically different bases of different strength and NO source quantifies the substitution — so swap cautiously and taste.
  • What it does, by function: emulsifier with palm oil and water; tenderiser for tough meat, bone, cowpeas, cow-foot and stockfish; vegetable enhancement for okra, okra leaves, dried okra and ewedu; flavour enhancer in nkwobi and gbegiri; plus preservative and colour additive.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Letting the undissolved sediment into the food.
  • Adding it all at once rather than gradually.
  • Using it in pregnancy, or using a heavy hand.
  • Swapping baking soda in at the same quantity and the same stage.
  • Making an emulsion in a wet pot.

Health Benefits

  • Used in the documented quantities it contributes negligible calories.
  • It tenderises tough cuts and legumes, which shortens cooking times considerably.
  • It preserves the green colour of leafy vegetables.
  • No nutritional claim is made for it beyond those functions — see the safety note, which matters more.

Cultural Note

Kaun is mined from dry lake beds across the Sahel and traded south, and it has been doing this job for a very long time. It is the thing that makes nkwobi yellow, ukwa tender and ewedu green, and it runs quietly through a dozen dishes in this collection without ever being the point of any of them. That its English name is chemically wrong, and that no recipe mentions the dose warning, is why it gets the last entry rather than a footnote.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Indefinitely as a dry lump in a sealed jar. The dissolved liquid should be made fresh.

Reheating: Not applicable.

Serve with: Nkwobi, Isi Ewu, Abacha

All Nigerian Recipes gives two functions more compactly: 'to make palm oil curdle' and 'as a food tenderizer', which map onto the two dish families — curdling for the emulsion dishes, tenderising for the long-cooked ones.
  • SUBSTITUTES, and a genuine gap. Baking soda: 'mix the baking soda with a small quantity of water. Pour into the palm oil and stir' — with NO QUANTITY for either the soda or the water, 'a small quantity' being the only measure. Ngu, described as burnt palm fruit stalk, noted as available primarily in Nigerian villages. And 1Q Food Platter's abacha page offers a fourth, 'dried plantain water' — plantain-peel ash water — which appears in no other source. The sources treat potash, baking soda, soda water and plantain-peel water as INTERCHANGEABLE for forming ncha. They are chemically different bases of different strength and NO SOURCE QUANTIFIES THE SUBSTITUTION. That is a real gap, not a settled point.
  • AN UNRESOLVED AMBIGUITY IN THE SOURCES, flagged: All Nigerian Recipes lists NGU both as a local name for potash AND as a distinct substitute material ('burnt palm fruit stalk'), and never resolves whether ngu IS potash or a different alkali made from palm-stalk ash. Taken with the plantain-peel ash water, this suggests a FAMILY of plant-ash alkalis of varying strength rather than one substance under several names. Not reconciled.
  • A CONNECTION WORTH DRAWING, and probably the root of the naming confusion: the iru fermentation protocol uses a calabash 'rubbed with wood ash'. Wood ash is genuinely potassium-carbonate-rich — that is, actual potash. So Nigerian cooking uses TWO DIFFERENT ALKALIS: real potash from wood ash in fermentation, and trona, misnamed 'potash', as kaun. They are not the same substance.
  • ALL THE DOCUMENTED QUANTITIES in this collection, gathered here for comparison since no single source does it: nkwobi 1 tsp in a cup of stock (Sisi Jemimah) or 1 tbsp (All Nigerian Recipes); isi ewu 1 tbsp in ~4 tbsp water; ncha 2 tsp in 1/4 cup water (Afrolems) against 2 tbsp in ~1 cup (All Nigerian Recipes abacha); ukwa 7 g to 700 g, 1% by weight; ewedu 1 tsp (Tribune); okra 1/2 tsp (9jafoodie); owo a teaspoon's worth of pounded native salt (Dobby) against 1/2 tsp (Guardian), 2 tsp (All Nigerian Recipes) and 2 1/2 to 3 tbsp for a much larger batch (Bawo); masa 1/4 tsp ground akaun; dan wake 2 tsp. The owo spread in particular is roughly fifteenfold and is the single most dangerous number in this collection to average — which is why nothing here is averaged.
  • ON THE RELATED CONDIMENT TANGLE, since the fermented seeds are routinely confused with each other and sometimes with the alkali. From Kitchen Butterfly's 'The Benefits of Fermented Seeds – Une, Iru, Ogiri': IRU or UNE is 'from the perennial decidous tree, Parkia biglobosa', the locust bean, called iru by the Igarra; OGIRI is 'fermented melon seeds'; DAWADAWA is 'from soybeans'; OKPEHE is 'from African mesquite seeds'; OGIRI-IGBO is 'produced from castor oil seed (Ricinus communis)'; OWOH is 'from cotton seeds (Gossypium hirsutum)'. Processing: seeds 'placed in a calabash...covered...for about 24 to 36 hours for fermentation', with a de-hulling route of either 7 days soaking in hot water or about 8 hours boiling.
  • TWO CONTRADICTIONS IN THAT TAXONOMY, reported and NOT reconciled. FIRST, on ogiri: she defines it as fermented MELON seed, but Sisi Jemimah's ikokore glosses 'Ogiri Ijebu' as fermented LOCUST beans, Tribune Online does the same, and All Nigerian Recipes lists 'Ogiri Okpei (traditional locust bean seasoning)'. Three sources gloss ogiri as locust bean and one defines it as melon seed — and Sisi Jemimah's egusi offers 'Small Chunk Ogiri OR 2 Tablespoons Locust Beans' as alternatives, implying they are different, which contradicts her own ikokore gloss. The qualifiers may well dissolve part of this, since 'Ogiri Ijebu', 'Ogiri Okpei' and 'Ogiri-Igbo' may be four different products — she herself identifies ogiri-igbo as castor seed, distinct from her melon-seed ogiri — but NO SOURCE explains what Ogiri Ijebu or Ogiri Okpei is actually made from. That is the gap and it is not guessed at. SECOND, on dawadawa: her gloss of 'from soybeans' is narrower than common usage, in which dawadawa is the Hausa name for the same Parkia biglobosa locust bean the Yoruba call iru. Soy dawadawa is a well-established modern northern product, so she may be documenting that. She is a food historian and is not called wrong here; her gloss is recorded as narrower than the consensus, and no other source consulted defines dawadawa at all.
  • HOW THEY DIFFER IN USE — NOT ESTABLISHED. No source consulted explains when to reach for iru rather than ogiri rather than dawadawa. A clean regional pattern does emerge from the recipes themselves: iru is the Yoruba condiment and appears in nearly every Yoruba dish here; ogiri in some form is the eastern one. But two separate sources name 'Ogiri Ijebu' as the correct condiment for an Ijebu Yoruba dish, which cuts against a clean east-west split.
