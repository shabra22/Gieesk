Waakye — waatchy, rice and beans

The quickest of three documented versions — a tin of black-eyed peas, a cup of rice and baking soda standing in for the millet leaves that give waakye its colour.

Waakye is rice and beans and it is the breakfast of Ghana, sold from huge pans with a tray of accompaniments beside it. The colour — reddish-brown rather than white — traditionally comes from dried millet or sorghum leaves, and the documented household versions substitute baking soda. This is the fastest of three: a 15.5-ounce tin of black-eyed peas, drained and lightly rinsed, one cup of white rice, half to one teaspoon of baking soda, half a teaspoon of salt, a tablespoon of oil, half a cup of chopped onion and two cups of water. Her note is that it cooks in about thirty minutes without burning.

Ingredients

Equipment: Rice cooker, or a pot with a lid, Colander, Measuring cups

  • 15.5 oz tin black-eyed peas, drained and lightly rinsed — about 1 1/2 cups
  • 1 cup white rice
  • 1/2-1 tsp baking soda
  • 1/2 tsp salt
  • 1 tbsp vegetable oil
  • 1/2 cup chopped onion
  • 2 cups water

Method

  1. Drain and lightly rinse the tinned black-eyed peas.
  2. Put the rice, peas, baking soda, salt, oil, onion and water into a rice cooker.
  3. Cook until done — about 30 minutes, without burning.
  4. Fluff and serve with the waakye tray: stew, shito, gari and boiled egg.

Chef Tips

  • Baking soda is the substitute for the traditional colouring agent. Waakye gets its reddish-brown colour from dried millet or sorghum leaves, and the soda approximates the alkaline effect.
  • Half to one teaspoon of soda is the printed range. More gives a deeper colour and, past a point, a soapy taste — start at half.
  • A rice cooker is what makes this the easy version, and her note is that it cooks in about thirty minutes without burning. Burning is the usual waakye problem.
  • Lightly rinse the tinned peas. The canning liquid is salty and would skew the seasoning.
  • Two cups of water to one cup of rice plus a tin of beans. The beans are already cooked, so the water is all for the rice.
  • Waakye is a tray, not a dish. It is properly eaten with stew, shito, gari, boiled egg, wele and sometimes spaghetti, all piled together.
  • Three documented versions use 2, 2.5 and 3 cups of water respectively for a cup of rice. This is the driest.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Skipping the baking soda, which gives white rice and beans rather than waakye.
  • Too much soda, which tastes soapy.
  • Not rinsing the tinned peas.
  • Letting it catch on the base.
  • Serving it without the tray of accompaniments.

Health Benefits

  • Rice and black-eyed peas together provide complementary amino acids, making a complete protein.
  • Black-eyed peas are high in folate, iron and soluble fibre.
  • Very low in fat as cooked.
  • Gluten-free and vegan.

Cultural Note

Waakye is sold from dawn, wrapped in leaves or in a plastic bag, and the tray beside the pan is half the point — stew, shito, gari, egg, wele, spaghetti, assembled to order. It came south from northern Ghana and became the country's breakfast, and the reddish colour from millet leaves is the first thing a Ghanaian checks for.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 3 days refrigerated, 3 months frozen.

Reheating: Steam, or microwave with a splash of water.

Serve with: Shito, Waakye Stew, Gari and boiled egg
