Fura — millet balls

Millet dough moulded into balls, boiled twenty minutes, pounded in a mortar and re-rolled — the double cook is the whole technique.

Fura is millet dough cooked twice, and the double cook is what defines it. Ground millet is mixed with spices, bound with water and moulded into medium balls. Water is brought to the boil for about seven minutes, the balls go in and boil twenty minutes, and then — this is the part that matters — they are transferred to a mortar, pounded thoroughly, re-moulded into smaller balls and dusted with corn flour. They are then mashed into nono to drink. No quantities are published anywhere for fura, which is stated rather than filled.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large bowl, Large pot, Mortar and pestle, Slotted spoon

  • Ground millet
  • Spices — see the note; the source names none individually
  • Water, as a binder
  • Corn flour, for dusting

To serve

  • Nono
  • Sugar to taste

Method

  1. Mix the dry flour ingredients and spices, add water as a binder, and mould into medium balls.
  2. Boil water for about 7 minutes, then drop the balls in and boil 20 minutes.
  3. Transfer the boiled balls to a mortar and pound thoroughly.
  4. Re-mould into small balls and sprinkle with corn flour.
  5. To serve, pour nono into a bowl, add the mashed fura balls and sugar to taste, and eat with a spoon.

Chef Tips

  • Boil, pound, re-form. That double cook is the technique and every source that describes fura at all agrees on it.
  • Twenty minutes in the water. One academic account gives a range of twenty to forty minutes for cooking fura balls, so twenty is the lower end.
  • Pound thoroughly while hot. Cooled fura balls will not come together in the mortar.
  • Dust the re-moulded balls with corn flour so they do not stick to each other.
  • Medium balls to boil, small balls to serve. The first size is for cooking through; the second is for mashing into nono.
  • No quantities exist for fura in any source consulted. The source names 'ground millet mixed with a host of other ingredients and spices' without itemising or measuring any of them — so build it by feel, a stiff dough that holds a ball.
  • Fura on its own is bland by design. It exists to be mashed into nono, which is where the flavour comes from.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Skipping the pounding stage, which leaves it dense.
  • Letting the balls cool before pounding.
  • Under-boiling, so the centres stay raw.
  • Not dusting with corn flour, so they stick together.
  • Expecting it to taste of much on its own.

Health Benefits

  • Millet is gluten-free and rich in magnesium and phosphorus.
  • Boiled rather than fried.
  • Low glycaemic index.
  • A good source of fibre and plant protein.

Cultural Note

Fura is carried. Pastoralist Fulani travelling with cattle could cook millet balls once, pound them, and have something that would keep for a day or two to be mashed into whatever milk was available — which is precisely what fura da nono is. It is a food shaped by movement rather than by a kitchen.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 2 days refrigerated. Best the day it is made.

Reheating: Mash into warm or cold nono rather than reheating.

Serve with: Nono, Fura da Nono, Sugar to taste
