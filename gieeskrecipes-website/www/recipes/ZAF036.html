🐠 Durban Fish Curry

Durban fish curry

A fierce tamarind-and-tomato gravy built over an hour, with firm fish added only for the last ten minutes and boiled eggs dropped in whole — the south-coast Indian curry, and it is genuinely hot.

This is a coastal Indian curry from the KwaZulu-Natal south coast, where families fished for themselves. The structure is the opposite of a stew: the gravy is cooked for half an hour before the fish appears, so the fish spends ten minutes in it and no longer. Tamarind rather than vinegar does the souring, dissolved in water first, and the heat is not moderated — two tablespoons of chilli powder in one pot. Boiled eggs are optional and traditional in equal measure.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large flat-bottomed saucepan, Blender, Bowl, for dissolving the tamarind

    The gravy

    • 5 tbsp oil
    • 2 onions, chopped
    • 4 green chillies
    • 1 tbsp garlic, crushed
    • 1 kg jam or Roma tomatoes, liquidised
    • 2 curry leaves
    • 2 tbsp chilli powder
    • 1 tbsp ground black pepper
    • 1 tbsp mustard seeds
    • 1 tbsp cumin seeds
    • 1/2 tsp ground turmeric
    • Salt, to taste

    The souring

    • 150 g black tamarind paste
    • 475 ml (2 cups) water

    The fish

    • 2 kg firm-textured fish such as yellowtail, cut into squares
    • 6 to 8 free-range eggs, boiled and peeled (optional)

    To finish

    • Fresh coriander, to garnish
    • Spring onions, to garnish
    • Basmati rice or garlic rolls, to serve

    Method

    1. Heat the oil in a large flat saucepan and fry the onions until softened.
    2. Add the green chillies, garlic, 1 cup of the liquidised tomatoes, the curry leaves and salt.
    3. Add the dry spices — chilli powder, black pepper, mustard seeds, cumin seeds and turmeric — and cook for 10 minutes.
    4. Dissolve the tamarind paste in the 2 cups of water and strain out any fibres, then stir the rest of the tomatoes into the tamarind water.
    5. Add that mixture to the pan and cook for 20 minutes, until the gravy has darkened and thickened.
    6. Add the fish and simmer with the lid slightly open for 10 minutes, or until just cooked through.
    7. Add the boiled eggs whole if you are using them, and let them warm through.
    8. Garnish with coriander and spring onions and serve with basmati rice or garlic rolls.

    Chef Tips

    • Dissolve the tamarind in water first and strain it. Added as paste it stays in lumps and the sourness is uneven.
    • The lid stays slightly open for the last ten minutes. Sealed, the fish steams and breaks up.
    • Do not stir once the fish is in. Move the pan rather than the spoon.
    • Two tablespoons of chilli powder is authentic and it is a lot. Halve it and build up if you are unsure — the gravy is easy to make hotter and impossible to make milder.
    • Firm fish only. Yellowtail, kingklip or snoek hold together; hake and sole disintegrate.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Adding the fish early, which turns it to shreds over an hour in the gravy.
    • Stirring after the fish goes in.
    • Using a soft-fleshed fish.
    • Adding the tamarind undissolved.

    Health Benefits

    • A lean, high-protein fish dish rich in omega-3 fatty acids.
    • Tamarind, turmeric, chilli and cumin all go in generously, so little salt is needed.
    • Boiled eggs add complete protein and choline.

    Cultural Note

    Indenture shaped this cuisine down to its ingredient list: the rations were rice, dholl, salted fish and oil, and when rice ran short, crushed maize took its place. What grew out of that was not Indian food transplanted but a distinct South African cuisine — and its distinctiveness was partly enforced, since apartheid-era segregation kept these kitchens separate for generations. That is a real culinary gain out of a real injustice, and it is worth saying both halves.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Refrigerate up to 2 days. The gravy keeps better than the fish.

    Reheating: Warm gently on the hob, without stirring.

    Serve with: Basmati rice, Garlic rolls, Mango atchar
