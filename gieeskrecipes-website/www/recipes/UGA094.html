🐜 Alakena

Alakena — ngwen, okok

Termites collected during their emergence, cleaned and ground to a paste-like consistency, with groundnuts or vegetables if you want them — and the winged ones dried and kept for later.

Alakena is the Lango termite dish, made from Macrotermes species — ngwen generally, and okok for the soldier termites. It is the northern counterpart to the Ganda nswaa, and the treatment is different: rather than roasting them as a snack, the termites are cleaned and ground into a paste. It is also the best-cited of the thinly documented Ugandan dishes, with real nutritional figures behind it, and those figures are why it is worth taking seriously as food rather than as curiosity.

Ingredients

Equipment: Collecting containers, Bowl of water, Grinding stone, or a mortar and pestle, Drying mat

  • Termites (Macrotermes species), locally ngwen
  • Soldier termites, okok

Optional

  • Groundnuts
  • Vegetables

No quantities are documented

    Method

    1. Collect the termites during their emergence.
    2. Clean them.
    3. Grind them into a paste-like consistency.
    4. Add groundnuts or vegetables if you want them.

    To keep them

    1. Dry the winged termites and store them for later.

    Chef Tips

    • Collect during the emergence. Termites come out of the ground in swarms after rain, and that window is the only time you get them.
    • Ground to a paste, not roasted whole. That is what makes this alakena rather than a termite snack, and it is the northern treatment.
    • Dried winged termites keep, which is how a household eats them out of season.
    • Groundnuts and vegetables are optional additions, and groundnut is the obvious one — it turns the paste into a sauce.
    • Two named types go in: ngwen is the general term and okok the soldier termites.
    • The nutritional figures are worth knowing because they are unusually well documented for a dish like this: lysine at 1.0 to 1.3 milligrams per gram, iron at 70.7 to 111.8 milligrams per hundred grams, and zinc at 4.4 to 16.2. Those iron figures are extraordinary.
    • No quantities are documented in any source. This is the honest shape of the record and nothing has been estimated.
    • The Ganda pounded-termite treatment in Nswaa — cleaned, roasted, sun-dried, pounded to flour and prepared as luwombo — is the closest documented parallel and is worth reading alongside this.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Trying to buy them out of season.
    • Roasting them whole and calling it alakena.
    • Skipping the cleaning.
    • Storing undried termites.
    • Grinding them so coarse the paste never forms.

    Health Benefits

    • Documented iron at 70.7 to 111.8 mg per 100 g, which is exceptional for any food.
    • Documented zinc at 4.4 to 16.2 mg per 100 g and lysine at 1.0 to 1.3 mg/g.
    • High in protein and in fat, with almost no carbohydrate.
    • Wild-harvested, seasonal, and with no farming input at all.

    Cultural Note

    Two communities, the same insect, two completely different dishes — the Baganda pound termites into flour for luwombo, the Lango grind them into a paste called alakena. That is the kind of divergence that only becomes visible when you record the local names rather than writing up a country's food as one cuisine.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Dried termites keep for months. The paste, 2 days refrigerated.

    Reheating: Warm gently.

    Serve with: Karo, Boiled Sweet Potatoes, Malakwang
