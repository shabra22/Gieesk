🥣 Midunu Groundnut Soup

Nkatenkwan

A chef's meatless version — a cup and a half of groundnut paste into eight cups of stock, and a warning about what happens if you stop before the oil separates.

This is a Ghanaian chef's groundnut soup and it is built the other way round from the household versions: no meat at all, just groundnut paste and tomato paste bloomed with cayenne, slowly let down with two cups of stock and cooked covered for twenty minutes, then the remaining six cups stirred in. Ginger, onion, optional mushroom powder and salt follow, and it boils thirty to forty minutes until the oil separates and is released from the groundnut paste. Blanched or sautéed vegetables go in at the end. Her warning about under-cooking it is the most useful sentence written about this dish.

Ingredients

Equipment: Heavy pot with a lid, Whisk or wooden spoon, Separate pan, for the vegetables

  • 375 ml (1 1/2 cups) smooth natural peanut butter or groundnut paste
  • 60 ml (1/4 cup) tomato paste
  • 7.5-15 ml (1/2-1 tbsp) ground cayenne pepper, to taste
  • 2 litres (8 cups) stock, divided
  • 5 ml (1 tsp) fresh ginger, grated
  • 15 ml (1 tbsp) mushroom powder (optional)
  • 60 ml (1/4 cup) finely minced onion
  • 10 ml (2 tsp) salt
  • Chilli, to taste

To finish

  • Blanched or sautéed vegetables of choice — green beans, eggplant, carrots, okra and/or cauliflower

Method

  1. Mix the peanut butter and tomato paste with the cayenne pepper in a pot and slowly blend in 2 cups of the stock.
  2. Cook on medium heat, covered, stirring occasionally, for 20 minutes.
  3. Add the remaining 6 cups of stock, stirring it in slowly.
  4. Reduce the heat to medium-low.
  5. Add the ginger, the mushroom powder if using, the onion and the salt.
  6. Boil 30 to 40 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the oil separates and is released from the groundnut paste.
  7. Add the blanched or sautéed vegetables and the chilli and cook a few minutes more.
  8. Serve, or reduce it further to use as a sauce.

Chef Tips

  • Her warning is the single most useful thing in the groundnut soup literature: if you do not let the soup cook until the oil separates, it can cause discomfort during digestion. The endpoint is not cosmetic.
  • Two cups of stock first, blended in slowly, then the other six. Adding all eight at once leaves you with lumps of undissolved paste.
  • Covered for the first twenty minutes, uncovered for the thirty to forty. The lid comes off when the long reduction starts.
  • A cup and a half of paste to eight cups of stock is a soup, not a stew — this is thinner than the diaspora versions and it is meant to be.
  • Vegetables are blanched or sautéed SEPARATELY and stirred in at the end, so they keep their colour and bite. It is a chef's touch and it works.
  • Mushroom powder is her optional umami rather than a traditional ingredient, and it stands in neatly for the dried fish or shrimp a household cook would use.
  • Reduce it down further and it becomes a sauce — her own note, and a good way to use leftovers.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Stopping before the oil separates, which her source explicitly warns causes digestive discomfort.
  • All eight cups of stock at once.
  • Cooking the vegetables in the soup rather than separately.
  • Leaving the lid on for the whole cook.
  • Expecting the thickness of a diaspora groundnut stew.

Health Benefits

  • Vegetarian as written with a vegetable stock.
  • Groundnut paste supplies plant protein, niacin, folate and vitamin E.
  • The separately cooked vegetables retain far more vitamin C than ones boiled in the soup.
  • Lower in fat per serving than the meat-based versions.

Cultural Note

A Ghanaian chef writing down her groundnut soup with metric measures, a stated endpoint and a physiological reason for it is exactly what the rest of this dish's documentation lacks. That the soup is meatless is also worth noting: groundnut soup is often assumed to need meat, and one of the country's best-known chefs publishes it without any.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 4 days refrigerated, 3 months frozen.

Reheating: Gentle heat with stock or water. Add fresh vegetables rather than reheating the originals.

Serve with: Omo Tuo, Fufu, Rice
