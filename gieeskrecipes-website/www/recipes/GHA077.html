🟡 Koose

Koose — akara, bean fritters

Two hundred grams of peeled black-eyed beans with just sixty millilitres of water — the low hydration is what makes them fluffy.

Koose is the fried bean fritter eaten with Hausa koko for breakfast across northern Ghana, and the striking thing about this recipe is how little water it uses: two hundred grams of peeled black-eyed beans to sixty millilitres, with a habanero, two onions and optional spring onions. That low hydration is what lets the batter hold air. The author promises the secret to a fluffy koose but the method is video-only, so no frying temperature, no oil quantity and no doneness cue are published — all of which is stated rather than filled in.

Ingredients

Equipment: Blender, Bowl and whisk, or a wooden spoon, Deep pot or fryer, Slotted spoon

  • 200 g peeled black-eyed beans
  • 1 large habanero chilli
  • 2 medium onions
  • 60 ml water
  • 20 g chopped spring onions (optional)
  • Salt, to taste

Oil for deep frying — no quantity is published

    Method

      The published method for this recipe is video-only. No written steps exist in the source.

      1. Peel the black-eyed beans if not already peeled, rubbing off the skins and rinsing them away.
      2. Blend the beans with the habanero, onions and the 60 ml of water to a thick paste.
      3. Beat the paste hard to whip air into it — this is what makes koose fluffy.
      4. Season with salt.
      5. Heat the oil for deep frying.
      6. Drop spoonfuls into the oil and fry until golden brown all over.
      7. Drain and serve hot with Hausa koko.

      The order above is standard practice for this fritter family; it is NOT from this source.

        Chef Tips

        • Sixty millilitres of water to two hundred grams of beans is a very stiff paste, and that is the point. A wet batter cannot hold air and gives you flat, oily fritters.
        • Beat the paste hard after blending. Air whipped into the bean paste is what makes a koose fluffy, and it is the one thing every cook says and almost nobody writes down.
        • Peeled beans, not whole. The skins make the fritter grey and coarse.
        • Be aware the method here is reconstructed. The author explicitly promises the secret to a fluffy koose and that secret is in her video, not her text.
        • No frying temperature and no oil quantity are published anywhere in this source. Medium-hot oil is the convention — too hot and you get raw middles.
        • Koose is bigger and more ginger-forward than Nigerian akara, by the 1968 government survey's account.
        • Eaten with Hausa koko at breakfast. The pairing of a sour millet porridge with a hot savoury fritter is one of the better breakfasts anywhere.

        Common Mistakes to Avoid

        • Adding more water to make blending easier.
        • Skipping the beating stage.
        • Using unpeeled beans.
        • Oil too hot, giving raw centres.
        • Treating the reconstructed step order as the author's own.

        Health Benefits

        • Black-eyed beans are high in plant protein, folate, iron and soluble fibre.
        • Vegan and gluten-free.
        • Eaten with koko it makes a complete-protein breakfast from cereal and legume.
        • Peeled beans are easier to digest than whole.

        Cultural Note

        Koose and koko at dawn is the northern Ghanaian breakfast, bought from two different sellers standing side by side — one with a pot of porridge, one with a pan of oil. That the only published quantities come with no method, and the promised secret sits in a video, is a neat illustration of how much of this cuisine is still taught by demonstration.

        Storage & Serving

        Storage: Same day. They go soft and oily.

        Reheating: A hot oven briefly.

        Serve with: Hausa Koko, Bread, Rice Water
