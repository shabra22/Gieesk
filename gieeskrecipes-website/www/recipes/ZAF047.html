🥩 Trinchado

Trinchado

Rump marinated in Worcestershire sauce, browned hard and braised an hour and a half in red wine, garlic and chilli, then finished with cream off the boil — Johannesburg's Portuguese restaurant dish, served with chips and bread for the sauce.

Trinchado is genuinely contested: it is widely described as a South African-Portuguese restaurant invention rather than a dish of Portugal, and the links usually drawn are to Angola and Mozambique. The word means roughly cut up or carved, which is the only thing about it nobody disputes. What is certain is where you eat it — the Portuguese restaurants of Johannesburg's southern suburbs — and that it comes with chips and crusty bread because the sauce is the point.

Ingredients

Equipment: Snug heavy-based pot with a tight lid, Large frying pan, Jug

    The beef and marinade

    • 1 kg rump steak, cut into large cubes
    • 4 tbsp Worcestershire sauce

    The braising liquid

    • 300 ml beef stock
    • 300 ml red wine
    • 1 heaped tbsp tomato paste
    • 2 tsp sugar

    The pot

    • 30 g butter
    • 2 tbsp vegetable oil
    • 150 g onion, finely chopped
    • 2 red chillies, finely chopped
    • 4 tsp garlic paste, or 5 to 6 cloves, minced
    • 2 tbsp plain flour
    • 3 bay leaves
    • Salt, to taste
    • Plenty of freshly ground black pepper

    To finish

    • 150 ml double cream

    Optional

    • 1 to 2 tbsp peri-peri sauce
    • 20 pitted black olives

    Method

    1. Cut the rump into large bite-sized chunks and turn them through the Worcestershire sauce in a bowl. Cover and marinate in the fridge for at least 30 minutes.
    2. Combine the stock, red wine, tomato paste and sugar in a jug and mix until the paste has dissolved evenly.
    3. Heat half the butter and half the oil over medium-high heat and brown the beef in batches, without crowding the pan. Set each batch aside and keep the leftover marinade.
    4. Reduce the heat to medium, add the rest of the butter and oil with the onion and chillies, and cook for 5 minutes until soft and translucent.
    5. Add the garlic and cook for 30 seconds, until fragrant.
    6. Sprinkle over the flour, stir it through and cook for a minute, scraping the base of the pot.
    7. Pour in the stock and wine mixture along with the leftover marinade, scraping up the browned bits.
    8. Reduce the heat to low and simmer for 2 to 3 minutes, until slightly thickened. Add the bay leaves, return the beef with its juices and stir to coat.
    9. Put the lid on tightly, drop to the lowest heat, and simmer very gently for 1 1/2 hours. Stir every 10 to 15 minutes and add a splash of wine or water if it dries out; leave the lid ajar for a few minutes if it is too wet.
    10. Check the beef is tender, then taste before adding any salt and finish with plenty of black pepper.
    11. Stir in the cream — less if it is already saucy, more to loosen it — and heat gently for a minute or two. Do not let it boil after the cream goes in.
    12. Take it off the heat and rest it for 10 minutes before serving with chunky chips or white bread rolls.

    Chef Tips

    • Use a snug heavy pot rather than a wide pan, or the sauce reduces too fast over ninety minutes.
    • Taste before you salt. Between the Worcestershire sauce and the stock there is already a lot of salt in the pot.
    • Do not boil it after the cream. A hard boil splits the sauce and there is no coming back.
    • Dry-aged or very lean rump can be tender at an hour rather than ninety minutes, so start checking early.
    • Keep the leftover marinade and put it in. It is Worcestershire sauce and beef juices, which is exactly what the sauce wants.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Boiling it once the cream is in.
    • Cooking it in a wide pan, so the liquid reduces away before the beef is tender.
    • Salting early and ending up with an oversalted sauce.
    • Crowding the pan while browning, which greys the beef instead of colouring it.

    Health Benefits

    • Rump is a lean cut, high in protein, iron, zinc and B12.
    • Slow braising makes the connective tissue digestible.
    • There is no getting around the cream: this is a rich dish.

    Cultural Note

    That the best-known Portuguese dish in South Africa is probably not a dish of Portugal is a good illustration of how this strand works. The Portuguese influence here arrived sideways — through Mozambique, through Madeira, through migration to Gauteng — and what it produced belongs to Johannesburg rather than to Lisbon.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days. Better the next day.

    Reheating: Reheat very gently on the hob — the cream will split over high heat.

    Serve with: Chunky chips, Crusty white bread rolls, A green salad
