🍖 Denningvleis

Denningvleis

Lamb knuckles browned until genuinely dark, stewed down with five onions, allspice and bay, then turned sweet-sour with vinegar and sugar — one of the oldest Cape Muslim recipes, and special-occasion food.

Denningvleis takes its name from dendeng, a West Sumatran word for dried and spiced meat, which is as direct a line back to the Indonesian archipelago as any dish in the Cape carries. It is a bredie by structure but it is not a tomato stew: the sourness is the point, traditionally from tamarind and in many modern kitchens from brown spirit vinegar with sugar to balance it. What makes or breaks it is the browning, which goes further than most cooks are comfortable with.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large heavy-based saucepan with a lid, Tongs

  • 2 kg lamb knuckles
  • 1 tbsp olive oil
  • 5 medium onions, finely chopped
  • 2 green peppers, finely chopped
  • 3 allspice berries (pimento)
  • 3 bay leaves
  • 2 tbsp minced garlic
  • 1 1/4 tsp salt
  • 1 tbsp ground black pepper
  • 1 green chilli, finely chopped
  • 6 tbsp brown spirit vinegar
  • 4 tbsp sugar
  • 1.2 L (5 cups) hot water, added in two stages

Method

  1. Heat the oil in a large saucepan over medium-high heat and brown the lamb well all over, in two batches if the pan is crowded. The browner the better — this is the dish's colour and most of its flavour.
  2. Lift the meat out. Add the onions, green pepper, allspice and bay leaves and fry until browned.
  3. Return the lamb with the garlic, salt, black pepper and chilli. Fry for 6 to 8 minutes, stirring as needed, until the meat and onions are dark brown.
  4. Add 3 cups of hot water, enough to cover, and cook over medium heat until most of the liquid has cooked away.
  5. Add the vinegar and the sugar and stir through, then turn the heat down to medium-low.
  6. Add the remaining 2 cups of hot water and cook until the meat is falling off the bone.
  7. Serve with buttery mashed potato and roasted vegetables.

Chef Tips

  • Brown it further than instinct allows. Steps one to three all end in the word brown, and a pale denningvleis is simply a different, lesser dish.
  • Tamarind is the traditional souring agent. If you have tamarind paste, use about 2 tablespoons dissolved in hot water in place of the vinegar, and cut the sugar to 2 tablespoons.
  • The vinegar goes in only after the first lot of liquid has cooked away, so it seasons a concentrated stew rather than being diluted from the start.
  • Thin the sauce with a little more hot water if it tightens too far. It should coat the meat, not set around it.
  • Five onions is not a typo. They cook down to almost nothing and they are the body of the sauce.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Under-browning the meat and onions, which gives a grey, thin-tasting stew that no amount of vinegar rescues.
  • Adding the vinegar and sugar at the start, where they interfere with browning and the sourness cooks off.
  • Using boneless lamb. Knuckles are the cut, and the bone and gristle are what make the sauce.
  • Crowding the pan during the initial browning, so the meat steams in its own juices.

Cultural Note

Denningvleis is made for occasions rather than weeknights, and it is one of the oldest recipes the Cape Muslim community still cooks. The word itself is evidence of the journey: dendeng, from West Sumatra, carried to the Cape by people who did not choose to travel, and kept in the language of the kitchen for three and a half centuries.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days. It is notably better on the second day.

Reheating: Reheat gently on the hob with a splash of hot water.

Serve with: Buttery mashed potato, Geelrys (yellow rice), Roasted vegetables
