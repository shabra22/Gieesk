🍆 Abacha with Garden Eggs

Abacha — Igbo salad

The version that names the emulsion out loud — one cooking spoon of palm oil, one teaspoon of soda water and a ground uda, stirred vigorously into a paste called ncha.

This abacha is smaller, fresher and more vegetable-forward than the big bowl version, with garden eggs, smoked prawns and anghara leaves in it. Its real contribution is terminological: it is the source that says the name out loud — add a ground uda to the oil, add the soda water, stir vigorously to form a paste called ncha. It also offers four different alkalis as interchangeable, including one that appears nowhere else: dried plantain water, the ash water from burnt plantain peel. The abacha is steamed five minutes and soaked five.

Ingredients

Equipment: Steamer or pot, Bowl, Spoon, Spice grinder

  • 1 cup ugba
  • 1 cup abacha
  • 4 pieces garden eggs, cubed
  • 1/2 cup cow skin
  • 1/4 cup smoked prawns
  • Stockfish pieces
  • Pepper, to taste
  • Seasoning, to taste
  • 2 tbsp anghara leaves, chopped
  • 1 uda (African nutmeg), ground
  • 1 cooking spoon palm oil
  • 1 tsp soda water, or dissolved baking soda, or dissolved potash, or dried plantain water

Method

  1. Steam the abacha about 5 minutes, then soak it about 5 minutes and drain.
  2. Put the palm oil in a bowl and add the ground uda.
  3. Add the soda water and stir vigorously with a spoon to form a paste — this is the ncha.
  4. Add the pepper and seasoning to the ncha.
  5. Fold in the ugba, the drained abacha, the cow skin, the smoked prawns and the stockfish.
  6. Add the cubed garden eggs and the chopped anghara leaves, mix, and serve.

Chef Tips

  • This is the recipe that names ncha. If you only learn one thing from it, learn that the emulsion has a name and that it is the base of several dishes.
  • Stir vigorously. The emulsion needs mechanical work and a gentle fold will not produce it.
  • Four alkalis are offered as interchangeable: soda water, dissolved baking soda, dissolved potash, and dried plantain water. They are chemically different bases of different strength and no source quantifies the substitution — start with the teaspoon specified and taste.
  • Dried plantain water is plantain-peel ash water and it appears in no other source. It is the gentlest of the four.
  • Steam five, soak five. The steaming softens and the soaking rehydrates; skipping either leaves the abacha chewy.
  • Garden eggs go in last, cubed and raw. They are there for crunch and bitterness against the rich emulsion.
  • One ground uda per batch. Uda is the Igbo name for grains of Selim, the same spice as urheri in the Delta pepper soups.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Folding gently instead of stirring hard.
  • Swapping one alkali for another at the same quantity without tasting.
  • Skipping the soak, so the abacha stays tough.
  • Cooking the garden eggs.
  • Leaving out the uda, which is the characteristic aroma.

Health Benefits

  • Garden eggs are high in fibre and very low in calories.
  • Fermented oil bean supplies protein and B vitamins.
  • Smoked prawns add lean protein and iodine.
  • Uda is traditionally used as a digestive.

Cultural Note

That two careful sources describe the same emulsion and only one of them names it is a small illustration of how cooking knowledge moves. The technique is universal in the southeast; the word ncha travels less reliably than the thing it describes, which is why a reader can make abacha for years without ever learning that the sauce has a name of its own.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 1 day refrigerated. Better eaten fresh.

Reheating: Serve at room temperature. Do not reheat.

Serve with: Cold palm wine, Chilled stout, Ugba
