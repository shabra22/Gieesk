🍟 Mogo Chips

Mogo — mhogo

Cassava boiled in salted water with a little oil until soft, drained and cooled, then fried golden and dusted with chilli powder — made this way in Uganda by a grandmother whose son still asks for them.

Mogo is cassava — the word is Swahili, mhogo — and mogo chips are the Indian-Ugandan snack that travelled to Britain and stayed. The method is boil-then-fry, and the cooling between the two stages is what gets them crisp, exactly as with the Ugandan party potatoes in Fried Party Irish Potatoes. The chilli powder and lime finish is the one the diaspora remembers: mogo chips doused in chilli powder and fresh lime, eaten at the roadside.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large pan, Kadai or deep frying pan, Slotted spoon, Colander

  • 1 kg cassava (mogo), fresh or frozen
  • Oil — for the boiling water and for frying; no quantity is given in the source
  • Red chilli powder, or black pepper powder — no quantity given
  • Salt — no quantity given

The chilli and lime finish

  • 1 lime, zest and juice
  • Fresh coriander

Method

  1. Boil plenty of water in a pan.
  2. Add a little oil and salt.
  3. Add the cassava and cook until done.
  4. Drain and cool the chips.
  5. Heat oil in a kadai and carefully add a few chips at a time.
  6. Fry until light golden and crispy.
  7. Remove with a slotted spoon.
  8. Sprinkle with chilli powder or pepper powder, and lime juice and zest if you want the full version.
  9. Serve with chutney.

Chef Tips

  • Cool them between boiling and frying. It is the same rule as the Ugandan party potatoes in Fried Party Irish Potatoes and it is what produces a crust rather than a chip that collapses in the oil.
  • A little oil in the boiling water, which is specified and unusual. It keeps the pieces from sticking to each other as they soften.
  • Fry a few at a time. Crowding drops the oil temperature and you get greasy mogo.
  • Remove the tough central fibres. Fresh cassava has a woody core that has to come out, and a chip with one in it is inedible.
  • Chilli powder and fresh lime is the finish the Ugandan-Asian diaspora remembers most, and it is worth doing properly rather than just salting them.
  • A no-deep-fry version exists and works: steam the chips 18 to 20 minutes until a knife goes in easily, toss them hard in a colander to roughen the surfaces while warm, then pan-fry in butter and oil with cumin seeds, garlic, chilli, turmeric and lime.
  • Only the cassava is quantified in the source. Oil, chilli powder and salt are all given without amounts and nothing here has been estimated.
  • Cassava must be properly cooked. Boil it through before frying, always.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Frying them warm.
  • Leaving the woody core in.
  • Crowding the pan.
  • Under-boiling, so the inside stays hard.
  • Salting only and skipping the chilli and lime.

Health Benefits

  • Cassava is high in carbohydrate, vitamin C and resistant starch.
  • Naturally gluten-free and dairy-free.
  • Boiling before frying means less oil is absorbed than in a raw fry.
  • Deep-fried, so it is a snack.

Cultural Note

Mogo chips are the clearest case of a food that belongs to a community rather than a country: a Swahili root vegetable cut like a British chip, spiced like a Gujarati snack, and remembered as Ugandan by people who left in 1972. That the recipe now lives on British food blogs written by grandchildren is the whole story of that community in one dish.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Boiled cassava keeps 2 days refrigerated before frying. Fry to order.

Reheating: A hot oven for a few minutes.

Serve with: Chutney, Potato Bhajia, Ugandan tea
