🥘 Jollof Rice with Tomato Stew

Jollof rice

Built on a pre-made tomato stew and chicken stock with no water at all — and the whole measurement is a water level that matches the level of the rice.

This is the jollof that assumes you already have tomato stew in the fridge, which most Nigerian kitchens do. Three and three quarter cups of parboiled long grain rice are parboiled, then rinsed and drained — note the order, which is the reverse of the party method. Chicken stock and two to three cups of the tomato stew go into the pot and come to the boil, the drained rice goes in with curry powder, salt and pepper, and the only measurement given for the liquid is a level: it should be the same level as the rice. Cover and cook until the water dries.

Ingredients

Equipment: Heavy pot with a lid, Sieve, Grill or frying pan, for the chicken

  • 3 3/4 cups (750 g) long grain parboiled rice
  • 2-3 cups tomato stew
  • Chicken — whole chicken, drumsticks or chicken breast
  • Chicken stock, from cooking the chicken
  • 2 medium onions
  • 3 bouillon cubes
  • 2 tsp thyme
  • 2 tsp curry powder (Nigerian curry powder)
  • Pepper and salt, to taste

Method

  1. Prepare the tomato stew beforehand.
  2. Wash, cut and cook the whole chicken with the thyme, bouillon cubes and 2 chopped onion bulbs. Keep the stock.
  3. Grill or fry the chicken pieces when they are done.
  4. Parboil the rice, then rinse and drain it in a sieve.
  5. Pour the chicken stock and the tomato stew into the pot and bring to the boil.
  6. Add the drained rice, the curry powder, salt and pepper. The water level should be the same level as the rice.
  7. Cover and cook on low to medium heat until the water dries.
  8. Taste to confirm. If the rice was properly parboiled it should be done.

Chef Tips

  • The water level should match the level of the rice. That is the measurement, and it is how most Nigerian cooks actually judge it.
  • Parboil first, THEN rinse and drain. That is the reverse of the party method, which rinses and then parboils, and it washes off the loose starch after the first cook rather than before.
  • No water at all — only chicken stock and tomato stew. That is what makes this version taste richer than a stock-and-water one.
  • Taste to confirm, and the source's own caveat is useful: if the rice was properly parboiled it should be done. Most jollof failures happen at the parboil.
  • Nigerian curry powder is specified, and it is a different product from the Caribbean-style powder the party version calls for. Neither source treats them as interchangeable.
  • Grill or fry the chicken after boiling it. Boiled-then-grilled is the standard Nigerian treatment and it is what gives the browned exterior over cooked-through meat.
  • Two to three cups of tomato stew is the range, and it is where the pepper-to-tomato ratio hides — this page never states one, so it inherits whatever your stew is.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Rinsing before parboiling rather than after.
  • Adding water on top of the stock and stew.
  • Liquid above the level of the rice, which gives mushy jollof.
  • Using Caribbean curry powder where Nigerian is specified.
  • Serving the chicken boiled rather than grilled or fried.

Health Benefits

  • Built on stock rather than water, so more protein and minerals carry into the rice.
  • Tomato stew supplies lycopene, more available once cooked.
  • High in protein with the chicken.
  • Gluten-free.

Cultural Note

Most Nigerian kitchens keep a pot of tomato stew in the fridge, and this is the jollof that assumes it — which makes it the most realistic of the three documented versions. It also explains why the tomato-to-pepper ratio is never stated: the stew already exists, made the way that household makes it, and the jollof simply inherits it.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 4 days refrigerated, 3 months frozen.

Reheating: Steam, or microwave with a splash of water under a damp cover.

Serve with: Moi Moi, Dodo (fried plantain), Coleslaw
