🫖 Mujaja Tea

Mujaja

Mujaja leaves brought to the boil with water and sugar and taken off the moment they turn fragrant and the water changes colour — no tea leaf in it at all.

Mujaja is a Ugandan aromatic herb, and mujaja tea is a herbal infusion with no tea in it. It is what you drink when you are unwell, or in the evening, or when you simply want something warm that is not black tea with milk. The whole method is three lines long and the only judgement in it is knowing when to stop: the leaves release a fragrant aroma and the water changes colour, and that is the moment.

Ingredients

Equipment: Saucepan, Strainer

  • A generous handful of mujaja leaves
  • 960 ml (4 cups) water
  • Sugar, to taste

Method

  1. Put the water, mujaja leaves and sugar in a clean saucepan and bring to the boil.
  2. Take it off the heat as soon as the leaves start releasing a fragrant aroma and the colour of the water has changed.
  3. Strain the leaves out.
  4. Serve hot.

Chef Tips

  • The aroma and the colour change together, and that is the cue. Boiled beyond it the infusion turns bitter and flat.
  • The sugar goes in with the water rather than after, which is how the recipe is written — it draws the flavour out as it heats.
  • No leaf quantity is given in the source. A generous handful to four cups is a reasonable starting point and you should adjust to taste.
  • Strain it. Leaves left in keep infusing and the cup goes bitter as it cools.
  • It is a herbal infusion, not tea — there is no black tea leaf in it at all.
  • Lemongrass tea is made the same way and is equally common in Ugandan households.
  • Good with ginger added, though that is not in the original.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Boiling it past the aroma.
  • Leaving the leaves in the pot.
  • Adding milk — this is not that kind of tea.
  • Using dried leaves without increasing the quantity.
  • Sweetening after straining rather than during.

Health Benefits

  • A caffeine-free hot drink.
  • Aromatic herbal infusions are traditionally taken in Uganda for colds and for settling the stomach.
  • Contains no fat and only what sugar you choose to add.
  • Made from a single fresh herb and water.

Cultural Note

Herbal infusions from garden plants — mujaja, lemongrass, mint — are ordinary in Ugandan households and sit alongside the very sweet milky black tea rather than replacing it. Which herb you reach for is largely a matter of what is growing outside.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Best fresh. Refrigerate up to 2 days and serve cold.

Reheating: Warm gently. Do not re-boil it.

Serve with: Mandazi, Daddies, Kabalagala
