🥟 Samoosas

Samoosa

Spiced mince rolled into pastry triangles and deep-fried — sealed with a flour-and-water paste, filled only once the mince is properly cold, and folded so there is no gap for the oil to find.

South Africa spells it samoosa, not samosa, and the difference in the spelling reflects a real difference in the dish: the local one is smaller, thinner-skinned and more heavily spiced than most of its relatives. They belong to both the Durban Indian and Cape Muslim kitchens and appear at every occasion in both, which is why the standard batch size is twenty-five or thirty rather than six.

Ingredients

Equipment: Medium saucepan, Small bowl, for the sealing paste, Deep pan or fryer, Slotted spoon

    The filling

    • 500 g mince — beef or lamb
    • 2 tbsp olive oil
    • 1 onion, chopped
    • 2 cloves garlic, crushed
    • 1.5 cm piece fresh ginger, finely grated
    • 2 tbsp garam masala
    • 2 tbsp curry powder
    • 1 tbsp ground cumin
    • 1 tbsp ground coriander
    • Salt and pepper, to taste
    • 3 tbsp fresh mint, chopped
    • 3 tbsp fresh coriander, chopped

    The pastry and seal

    • 10 sheets spring roll pastry, cut into long strips
    • 15 g flour
    • 60 ml (1/4 cup) water

    To fry

    • 1 L sunflower oil

    Method

    1. Sauté the chopped onion in the olive oil in a medium pot until softened.
    2. Add the garlic, ginger and all the ground spices and cook for 2 to 3 minutes.
    3. Add the mince and cook for 10 minutes, until browned and cooked through and the pan is dry. Season with salt and pepper.
    4. Take it off the heat, stir through the chopped mint and coriander, and let the filling go completely cold. This is not optional — warm filling steams the pastry from the inside.
    5. Mix the flour and water into a smooth thin paste. This is your glue.
    6. Lay a strip of pastry down. Place 1 tbsp of cold filling near one end and fold the corner over it diagonally to make a triangle.
    7. Keep folding along the strip, corner over corner, so the triangle builds up layers, then brush the final flap with the flour paste and press it down firmly.
    8. Check every seam is sealed. Any gap lets hot oil in and the samoosa bursts.
    9. Deep-fry in hot oil for 3 to 5 minutes, until golden, and drain on paper towel.

    Chef Tips

    • Completely cold filling. This is the single rule that decides whether the pastry stays crisp or goes soggy before it reaches the fryer.
    • Cook the mince until the pan is genuinely dry. Wet filling is the other route to a soggy samoosa.
    • The flour-and-water paste is the seal. Water alone does not hold, and a seam that opens in the fryer loses the filling.
    • Fold firmly enough that there are no air pockets. Trapped air expands and splits the pastry.
    • They freeze beautifully raw. Freeze them flat on a tray, then bag them, and fry from frozen with a minute or two extra.
    • For a vegetarian version, use spiced potato and peas with the same method.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Filling them while the mince is still warm.
    • Sealing with water instead of flour paste, so they open in the oil.
    • Overfilling, which makes them impossible to fold tightly and prone to bursting.
    • Frying in oil that is not hot enough, so the pastry absorbs it instead of crisping.

    • High in protein for their size, at roughly 130 calories each.

    • Portioned small by design — a batch of nearly thirty is meant for a table.

    Cultural Note

    Samoosas cross every line in South African food: they are Durban Indian and Cape Muslim, they are sold at school fetes and taxi ranks and served at iftar and at Diwali, and nobody regards them as belonging to anyone in particular any more. The local spelling with the double o is worth keeping.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Raw, frozen, up to 3 months. Fried, eat the same day.

    Reheating: Re-crisp in a hot oven for 8 minutes. Never a microwave.

    Serve with: Mango atchar, Mint chutney, Tea, at an iftar or a family gathering
