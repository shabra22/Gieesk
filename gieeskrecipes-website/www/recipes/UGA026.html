🫙 Spicy Mukene Paste

Mukene paste

Silverfish, mushrooms and aromatics fried, blended with a cup and a half of oil and simmered an hour until it darkens to a deep brown with clear oil floating on top.

This is a modern Ugandan condiment rather than an old one, and its inventor is honest about that: it came from reading about Asian shrimp paste and asking what a landlocked country with a surplus of cheap lake fish could do instead. It works exactly the way a shrimp paste works — a spoonful into greens, a groundnut stew, a pan of noodles. The quarter cup of salt and the cup and a half of oil are preservatives, not seasoning, and they are why a jar keeps two months on a shelf.

Ingredients

Equipment: Blender, Wide pan, Clean jars or bottles with lids

  • 240 ml (1 cup / 250 g) mukene
  • 240 ml (1 cup) mushrooms, sliced
  • 7 cloves garlic, whole
  • 4 large tomatoes, roughly chopped
  • 1 stick spring onion, roughly chopped
  • 60 ml (1/4 cup) ginger, roughly chopped
  • 1 scotch bonnet or other chilli, roughly chopped
  • 2 large onions, roughly chopped
  • 1 green pepper, roughly chopped
  • 355 ml (1 1/2 cups) oil
  • 60 ml (1/4 cup) salt
  • 15 ml (1 tbsp) smoked paprika
  • 15 ml (1 tbsp) black pepper

For soaking

  • 15 ml (1 tbsp) lemon juice

Method

  1. Soak the mukene 20 minutes in clean water with the tablespoon of lemon juice. Rinse it twice and set aside.
  2. Pour half a cup of the oil into a clean pan over medium heat. Add the garlic, ginger and spring onion and let them cook until wilted but not burnt, then lift them out and set aside.
  3. In the same oil, add the rinsed mukene and the mushrooms and fry about 10 minutes over medium heat, until it starts turning golden brown.
  4. Return the spring onion, ginger and garlic and stir well. Add the salt, chilli, green pepper, onion and tomatoes, and cook another 10 minutes.
  5. Take the pan off the heat and let it cool for about 20 minutes.

Blending and the long simmer

  1. Pour the cooled mixture into a blender with the remaining cup of oil and blend to a purée.
  2. Return the purée to the same pan over low heat.
  3. Simmer for about an hour or more, until it has cooked right down to a paste, turned a deep rich brown, and a silky oil floats on top.
  4. Take it off the heat and let it cool completely, then bottle.

Chef Tips

  • The garlic and ginger go in whole and the rest roughly chopped — everything gets blended, so knife work here is wasted effort.
  • Fry the aromatics and take them out before the fish goes in. They would burn in the ten minutes the mukene needs.
  • Soaking the fish first lets it absorb flavour, which it cannot do cooked dry.
  • The mushrooms and tomatoes are there for umami, not bulk. Do not leave them out.
  • The quarter cup of salt and the cup and a half of oil look excessive and are the preservation system. Cut them and you have a paste that spoils.
  • The doneness cue is the deep rich brown colour and the silky oil floating on top. An hour is a minimum, not a target.
  • Low heat for the whole hour. This is the one point where it will burn, and burnt fish paste is beyond rescue.
  • It is not a traditional dish and its author does not claim it is — she built it from an Asian shrimp-paste model and says it might be apeta depending how you treat it.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Reducing the salt or the oil.
  • Simmering it too hot and scorching the paste.
  • Stopping before the colour has deepened and the oil has separated.
  • Blending it hot.
  • Cooking the mukene dry.

Health Benefits

  • Silverfish eaten whole make this high in calcium, omega-3 and vitamin B12.
  • Garlic, ginger, chilli and paprika all go in in quantity.
  • Very high in sodium by design, because the salt is the preservative — use it a spoonful at a time.
  • Contains fish.

Cultural Note

This is worth having in a Ugandan collection precisely because it is new. A cook looking at an abundant, cheap, unglamorous local ingredient and building a pantry staple out of it is how cuisines actually move, and the honesty about where the idea came from is more interesting than a false claim of antiquity would be.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Up to 2 months in a clean sealed jar at room temperature. Longer refrigerated.

Reheating: Not needed — it is a condiment, used a spoonful at a time.

Serve with: Sauteed Garlic Dodo, Binyebwa, Noodles
