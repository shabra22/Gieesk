🧅 Nokoss

Nokoss — nokos, the seasoning base

The pounded base nearly every Senegalese dish starts from — onion, garlic, nététou, chilli and pepper, used a third at a time.

EasyDifficulty

If rof is what goes inside the fish, nokoss is what goes into the pot. One onion, two cloves of garlic, a handful of nététou, two bird's eye chillies, half a bell pepper, an optional bouillon cube and salt, pounded or chopped together. Its distinguishing feature against every other West African pepper base is the nététou — fermented locust bean — which brings the savoury depth that tomato and onion alone cannot. Senegalese cooks add it in stages rather than all at once: a third with the browning meat, a third into the water, the last third near the end.

Ingredients

Equipment: Mortar and pestle, or a blender, Knife

  • 1 onion
  • 2 cloves garlic
  • A handful of nététou
  • 2 bird's eye chillies
  • 1/2 bell pepper
  • 1 bouillon cube, if you wish
  • Salt

A second documented formula, from the same author's thiébou yap

    1 onion, 1/2 bell pepper, 1 tsp pepper, 8 cloves garlic and salt

      Method

      1. Pound or chop the onion, garlic, nététou, chillies and bell pepper together with the salt and the optional bouillon cube.

      Use it in thirds, not all at once

      1. Add a third with the meat or fish as it browns.
      2. Add a third to the water once the pot comes to the boil.
      3. Add the last third near the end, then taste and adjust the salt.

      Chef Tips

      • The nététou is what makes this Senegalese rather than generically West African. Without it you have a pepper base; with it you have nokoss.
      • In thirds, by the author's own method. One third with the browning, one third into the broth, one third at the end — it layers rather than flattening into one note.
      • Pounded, not blended smooth, if you have a mortar. The texture carries through into the finished broth.
      • Taste and re-salt after the last third goes in. Nététou is salty and variable, so seasoning at the start is guesswork.
      • Her other documented formula uses eight cloves of garlic and a teaspoon of pepper with no nététou listed — a much more garlic-forward base. Both are hers and both work.
      • Make it fresh. It is ten minutes of work and it does not keep as well as a dried spice mix.
      • Note this is a different preparation from nokos, the dry spice blend published in a Senegalese chef's fonio book, despite the near-identical name.

      Common Mistakes to Avoid

      • Leaving out the nététou, which is the point of it.
      • Adding it all at once at the start.
      • Blending it to a smooth purée.
      • Salting fully before the last third goes in.
      • Confusing it with the dry nokos spice blend.

      Health Benefits

      • Nététou is high in plant protein and B vitamins, and its alkaline fermentation improves amino-acid availability.
      • Garlic and onion supply organosulfur compounds and quercetin.
      • Capsaicin from the bird's eye chillies.
      • Replaces much of the salt and bouillon a dish would otherwise need.

      Cultural Note

      Every West African cuisine has its aromatic base and the differences between them are the differences between the cuisines — Nigerian obe ata leans on tomato and pepper, Ghanaian chalé sauce on blended raw tomato, and Senegalese nokoss on fermented locust bean. Senegalese cooks add it in thirds, which is a technique rather than a recipe.

      Storage & Serving

      Storage: 2 days refrigerated, or freeze in portions. Best fresh.

      Reheating: Not applicable — added to the pot raw.

      Serve with: Ceeb bu Weex, Thiébou Yap, Thiéboudiene Diaga
