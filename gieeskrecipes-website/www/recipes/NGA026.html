Ata dindin — fried pepper stew

Dried pepper soaked at least an hour, blended rough, sieved dry and fried in unbleached palm oil through five timed stages — twenty-seven minutes in all.

Ata dindin is the fried pepper stew that goes with ekuru, and it is built in stages rather than all at once: onions five minutes on low, iru one minute, the pepper blend five minutes, fried meats ten, smoked fish four, crayfish two. Each addition gets its own window. The dried pepper is soaked in hot water for at least an hour first and the blend is sieved so no moisture goes into the oil. Notably the palm oil here is not bleached, even though the same author bleaches it for her agoyin sauce — a divergence she never explains.

Ingredients

Equipment: Saucepan, Blender, Sieve, Wooden spoon

  • 3 cooking spoons palm oil
  • 1 cup dried pepper (ata ijosi)
  • 1 red bell pepper, or 3 if you cannot source dried pepper
  • 1 scotch bonnet
  • 1 1/2 medium onions — 1 for the blend, 1/2 chopped
  • 2 tbsp locust beans (iru)
  • 2 bouillon cubes
  • 1 cup fried assorted meats
  • 1 smoked fish or eja shawa (optional)
  • 1/2 cup smoked prawns (optional)
  • 2 tbsp ground crayfish
  • Salt to taste

Method

  1. Soak the smoked fish or shawa in hot water to clean it, then shred into smaller pieces.
  2. Soak the dried pepper in hot water for at least an hour.
  3. Blend the dried pepper, scotch bonnet, 1 onion and the bell peppers roughly. Sieve out any moisture and set aside.
  4. Put the palm oil in a saucepan on low heat. Add the half chopped onion and saute 5 minutes — keep it on low so the onions do not burn.
  5. Add the locust beans and saute 1 minute.
  6. Turn to medium, add the blended pepper mix, the bouillon cubes and salt. Fry 5 minutes, stirring occasionally.
  7. Add the fried meats and continue frying another 10 minutes, stirring throughout.
  8. Add the shredded smoked fish and the prawns and fry 4 minutes.
  9. Add the crayfish powder and fry 2 minutes.

Chef Tips

  • An hour minimum soaking the dried pepper, in hot water. Under-soaked ata ijosi stays gritty however long you fry it.
  • Sieve the blend. Water in the oil spits and stalls the frying.
  • Blend rough, not smooth. Ata dindin should have body you can see.
  • Low heat for the first five minutes. Burnt onions at the base poison the whole pot and there is no recovering it.
  • The palm oil here is NOT bleached — and the same author bleaches it for her agoyin sauce. She never explains the difference. Unbleached gives you a red stew; bleached gives you the near-black agoyin.
  • Ten minutes with the meat in is the longest stage and it is where the oil and pepper marry. Keep stirring.
  • Crayfish last, two minutes, no more. It is there for aroma and long frying destroys it.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Short-soaking the dried pepper.
  • Skipping the sieve.
  • Frying the onions on medium or high.
  • Bleaching the palm oil for this one.
  • Adding the crayfish early.

Health Benefits

  • Capsaicin from the dried pepper is associated with raised thermogenesis.
  • Red palm oil provides vitamin E and carotenoids.
  • Fermented locust bean supplies B vitamins and glutamates.
  • Crayfish and smoked fish add protein and iodine.

Cultural Note

Ata dindin means fried pepper, and the name is the method. It is the all-purpose Yoruba sauce — made in quantity, kept under its own oil, and spooned onto whatever needs it: ekuru, eko, boiled yam, plantain, rice. The staged frying is what separates it from a quick stew, and a well-made batch will keep for a fortnight in the fridge.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 2 weeks refrigerated under a film of its own oil, 3 months frozen.

Reheating: Low heat. It is a fried stew and reheats well.

Serve with: Ekuru, Eko, Boiled yam
