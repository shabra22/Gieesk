🍝 Genovese Napoletana

Genovese

A confusingly-named but entirely Neapolitan pasta sauce, beef slow-braised with a mountain of onions until they collapse into a naturally sweet, deeply savory ragù, with no connection to Genoa or pesto at all.

Despite its name, genovese has no ties to Liguria — the most credible theory is that it was introduced to Naples by cooks from Genoa centuries ago, and the name simply stuck, describing an onion-heavy beef sauce entirely distinct from anything found in Genoa's own cuisine.

Ingredients

Equipment: Heavy-bottomed pot with lid, Large pot for pasta

  • 1 kg (2.2 lb) beef chuck or shin, whole or in large pieces
  • 1.5 kg (3.3 lb) yellow onions, thinly sliced
  • 2 carrots, chopped
  • 1 celery stalk, chopped
  • 150 ml (⅔ cup) dry white wine
  • 100 ml (scant ½ cup) extra virgin olive oil
  • 400 g (14 oz) rigatoni or ziti
  • Grated Parmigiano-Reggiano, for serving
  • Salt and pepper, to taste

Method

  1. Warm the olive oil in a heavy pot and brown the beef well on all sides.
  2. Add the onions, carrots, and celery, and cook over medium heat, stirring occasionally, until they begin to soften and release their liquid.
  3. Reduce the heat to low, cover, and cook very slowly for 2–2.5 hours, stirring occasionally, until the onions have completely collapsed into a thick, jam-like sauce and the beef is fork-tender.
  4. Add the wine partway through and let it incorporate.
  5. If the sauce looks dry at any point, add a splash of water; if too wet toward the end, uncover to let it reduce.
  6. Remove the beef, shred or slice it, and season the sauce to taste.
  7. Bring a large pot of salted water to a boil and cook the pasta until al dente.
  8. Toss the pasta with the onion sauce, serving the beef alongside as a second course, with Parmigiano at the table.

Chef Tips

  • This dish is entirely about patience — the onions need genuine time to break down into a naturally sweet, thick sauce.
  • Traditionally, the pasta (dressed in the onion sauce) and the beef are served as two separate courses of the same meal.
  • Low heat throughout is essential; rushing with high heat will brown rather than melt the onions.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Rushing the cooking time, which prevents the onions from properly breaking down into a rich sauce.
  • Cooking over heat that's too high, causing the onions to caramelize and brown rather than melt down softly.
  • Confusing this with Ligurian pesto-based dishes due to the name — genovese in Naples refers to this entirely different onion-beef sauce.

Health Benefits

  • Onions provide fiber and antioxidant compounds, especially when cooked slowly; a good source of protein from the beef.

Cultural Note

Genovese is a beloved Neapolitan classic despite its misleading name, with historians generally agreeing it was brought to Naples by Genoese cooks centuries ago, the recipe itself having nothing to do with Liguria today.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Refrigerate up to 4 days; flavor deepens further the next day.

Reheating: Reheat gently on the stovetop, adding a splash of water if needed.

Serve with: A medium-bodied red wine
