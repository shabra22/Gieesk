🥕 Chakalaka

Chakalaka

Onion, pepper and chilli braised soft, grated carrot and cabbage cooked down into them, then baked beans, chutney, vinegar and sugar — made a day ahead and served cold, which is how it is meant to be.

Chakalaka goes on the plate at every braai, every funeral and every Sunday lunch in the country, and there is no set recipe for it: it runs from mild to searing depending on who made it. The most repeated account of its origin puts it in the Johannesburg townships and the mining compounds around them, with Mozambican mineworkers cooking tinned tomato and beans with chilli into a relish. That attribution is not firmly documented and it is worth holding loosely. What is certain is that it belongs to Gauteng and to the mines.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large heavy saucepan, Box grater

  • 60 ml cooking oil — sunflower or canola
  • 250 g white onion, finely diced
  • 150 g bell peppers — a mixture of green, red and yellow, finely diced
  • 30 g red chillies, finely diced
  • 150 g cabbage, finely chopped
  • 200 g carrots, coarsely grated
  • 250 g tomatoes, roughly chopped
  • 1/2 tsp ground turmeric
  • 1/2 tsp leaf masala or curry powder
  • 1/2 tsp salt
  • 15 g seasoned salt, such as Aromat
  • 1 x 400 g tin baked beans
  • 1 tbsp hot fruit chutney
  • 1 tbsp brown vinegar
  • 1 tbsp brown sugar

Method

  1. Braise the onions, bell peppers and chillies in the heated oil until softened.
  2. Once soft, add the chopped cabbage and grated carrot and let them soften down.
  3. Add the chopped tomatoes.
  4. Season with the turmeric, leaf masala or curry powder, salt and seasoned salt.
  5. Stir in the baked beans, the hot fruit chutney, the brown vinegar and the brown sugar, and cook on medium heat for a few minutes so the flavours come together.
  6. Cool completely, then refrigerate. It is at its best served cold the following day.

Chef Tips

  • Make it the day before. This is not a convenience, it is the recipe — the flavours need overnight and it is served cold.
  • The carrot is grated coarsely and everything else is diced or chopped. The mix of textures is the point.
  • Fifteen grams of seasoned salt on top of half a teaspoon of salt is a lot. Taste before you add the second lot.
  • Baked beans go in at the end. Cooked long, they break down and the relish turns to sludge.
  • Heat is personal and unregulated here. Thirty grams of red chilli is assertive; halve it or double it as your household prefers.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Serving it hot and fresh from the pan, which is how it tastes least like itself.
  • Adding the beans early.
  • Cooking the carrot and cabbage to nothing — they should still have some body.
  • Salting to taste before the seasoned salt has gone in, and then again after.

Health Benefits

  • Six vegetables in one relish, high in fibre and vitamin A from the carrot.
  • Baked beans add plant protein.
  • Entirely plant-based, and low in calories for how much flavour it carries.

Cultural Note

Where the word chakalaka comes from is not established in any source, and claims about its origin should be treated with some care — the most widely repeated version, Mozambican mineworkers in Johannesburg, rests on thin documentation, and other accounts place it in Sophiatown. What is not in doubt is that it is mining-compound and township food from Gauteng, and that it is now on more South African tables than almost anything else.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Refrigerated, up to a week. It improves for the first few days.

Reheating: Not reheated — it is a cold relish.

Serve with: Uphuthu, Braaied meat, Ujeqe
