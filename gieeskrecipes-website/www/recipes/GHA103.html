🌾 Fonio Jollof

Fonio jollof

Jollof made with fonio instead of rice — the West African grain goes in for only three minutes before the stock.

A Fulani chef's jollof, made with fonio rather than rice. Fonio is the ancient West African cereal — faster-cooking, gluten-free and far more nutritious than rice — and it needs only three minutes in the pan before the stock goes in, against the fifteen to twenty a rice jollof needs. Five hundred grams of fonio, half a kilo of yellow onions, five hundred grams of plum tomatoes, two hundred grams of tomato paste, a habanero, a bulb of garlic, two teaspoons of curry powder, three bay leaves and a hand of thyme. The vegetables should be tender but retain a slight firmness.

Ingredients

Equipment: Heavy pot with a lid, Blender, Wooden spoon

  • 500 g fonio, or rice
  • 1/2 kg yellow onions
  • 500 g fresh plum tomatoes
  • 200 g tomato paste
  • 1 habanero pepper or spicy chilli
  • 1 bulb garlic
  • 2 tsp curry powder
  • 1 little hand fresh thyme leaves
  • 3 bay leaves
  • 1-2 cups chicken or vegetable stock
  • 1 cup mixed vegetables, finely chopped — carrot, scallions, capsicum
  • 1 litre oil — see the note on this quantity

Method

  1. Blend the onions, tomatoes, habanero and garlic.
  2. Heat the oil and add the curry powder, cooking 2 minutes.
  3. Add the blended sauce and cook about 10 minutes.
  4. Add the tomato paste and the bay leaves and cook 10 minutes.
  5. Add the fonio and cook 3 minutes.
  6. Add the stock and the thyme and cook 10 minutes.
  7. Stir through the mixed vegetables.
  8. Cook until the vegetables are tender but retain a slight firmness.

Chef Tips

  • Fonio needs three minutes in the pan before the stock, not fifteen. It is the fastest-cooking grain in West Africa and overcooking it gives porridge.
  • Tender but retaining a slight firmness is the vegetable cue, and with fonio cooking this fast the vegetables are the thing to judge by.
  • One to two cups of stock for five hundred grams of fonio is far less liquid than rice would take. Fonio absorbs less and faster.
  • The oil quantity printed is one litre for five hundred grams of fonio, which cannot be right as a cooking quantity — it appears identically in both readings of the source and is almost certainly a misprint or a deep-frying figure. Start with a quarter cup.
  • Two hundred grams of tomato paste is a lot and it is what gives this jollof its depth without a long reduction.
  • Fonio is gluten-free, very low glycaemic and higher in amino acids than rice. It is worth seeking out for its own sake.
  • The chef is Sierra Leonean and works in Ghana, so this is a Fulani interpretation rather than a Ghanaian traditional dish — the Fulani are spread across the whole Sahel.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Cooking the fonio as long as rice.
  • Using a litre of oil as printed.
  • Rice-quantity stock, which drowns it.
  • Cooking the vegetables to softness.
  • Adding the fonio before the paste has cooked out.

Health Benefits

  • Fonio is gluten-free, low glycaemic and richer in the amino acids methionine and cystine than rice.
  • Vegan as written with vegetable stock.
  • High in fibre and iron.
  • A generous fresh vegetable load cooked only to firmness.

Cultural Note

Fonio has been grown in West Africa for thousands of years and was nearly lost to rice imports; chefs putting it back into familiar dishes is how a crop gets saved. A Fulani chef from Sierra Leone cooking it as jollof in Accra is also a neat answer to the question of whose dish jollof is.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 3 days refrigerated. Fonio firms up considerably.

Reheating: Steam, or microwave with a splash of water.

Serve with: Shito, Grilled fish, Kelewele
