🍮 Eko

Eko — agidi, eko tutu

One cup of corn flour to 700 ml of cold water, stirred in any direction, with a cold-water test that tells you exactly when it is done.

Eko is set corn pudding, eaten cold in firm blocks with akara, pepper soup or stew. It starts cold — flour and water whisked together in the pot before any heat — and the stirring is constant but, unusually for a Nigerian starch, in any direction you like. The test for doneness is the best thing about this recipe: drop a little into cold water, and if it sets when cooled the eko is done. Wrapped in uma leaves it takes the classic torpedo shape; in any container it sets just as hard.

Ingredients

Equipment: Medium pot, Wooden spatula, Uma leaves or containers, Small bowl of cold water, for the test

  • 1 cup corn flour (corn starch)
  • 700 ml (approx. 25 fl oz) cold water

For shaping

  • Uma leaves, or small containers

Method

  1. Mix the corn starch with the 700 ml of cold water in a pot, adding the water gradually to avoid lumps.
  2. Put the pot on the stove at medium-low heat.
  3. Stir continuously with a wooden spatula, in any direction, keeping it constantly moving.
  4. When the mixture thickens, keep stirring. If lumps form despite the stirring, reduce the heat.
  5. When it has thickened like custard, pour water down the sides of the pot, cover, and simmer 10 minutes on medium heat.
  6. Stir well to combine the agidi with that water.
  7. Test it: put a small quantity in cold water. If it sets when cooled, the eko is done.
  8. Transfer to containers and cool completely at room temperature.
  9. Wrap with uma leaves for the classic agidi shape.

Chef Tips

  • Cold water, cold start. Flour and water go into the pot together before any heat — the opposite of amala, where the flour goes into boiling water.
  • Any direction. This is the one Nigerian starch where the stirring direction genuinely does not matter, which is worth knowing given akara's one-direction rule.
  • The cold-water test is the cue and it is reliable. A teaspoon into cold water; if it firms, the pot is done.
  • Lumps mean the heat is too high. Turn it down rather than stirring harder.
  • The water poured down the sides before the covered simmer is not an error — it steams the pudding and is stirred back in afterwards.
  • Cool completely at room temperature before refrigerating. Rushing it into the fridge gives a wet, weeping block.
  • Uma leaf is Thaumatococcus daniellii, the same leaf used for moin moin, and it gives eko both its shape and a faint grassy scent.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Adding the flour to hot water.
  • Cooking it on high heat, which lumps it.
  • Skipping the covered ten-minute simmer.
  • Not testing in cold water and under-cooking it, so it never sets.
  • Refrigerating it before it has cooled.

Health Benefits

  • Fat-free and very low in calories.
  • Easy to digest, which is why it is given to convalescents and children.
  • Gluten-free.
  • No added salt or sugar.

Cultural Note

Eko is what akara is eaten with when bread is not in the picture, and the pairing is one of the oldest street breakfasts in the southwest. The same pudding appears across the country under different names — agidi in the southeast, and as a set form of the fermented corn pap eaten everywhere — and in Ghana and Togo as kenkey and koko. Wrapped in a leaf it keeps all day without refrigeration, which is most of why it exists.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 4 days refrigerated, wrapped or submerged in a little water.

Reheating: Eaten cold. For eko tutu, serve it at room temperature or chilled.

Serve with: Akara, Ata Dindin, Pepper soup
