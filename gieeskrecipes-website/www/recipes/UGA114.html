🥭 Mengu I'di

Mengu i'di

Mangoes cooked, the juice squeezed out while they are cooling, and sugar stirred in — the squeezing has to happen warm.

Mengu i'di is cooked mango juice, and the one instruction that matters is the timing: the juice is squeezed out while the fruit is cooling, not hot and not cold. Hot, you cannot handle it; cold, the pulp holds on to the liquid. It is a Lugbara drink and it belongs to the same family as the pineapple-peel omunanansi — a drink made by cooking fruit that is too ripe, too plentiful, or both.

Ingredients

Equipment: Saucepan, Sieve or cloth, Jug

  • Mangoes
  • Sugar
  • Water, for cooking

No quantities are documented

    Method

    1. Cook the mangoes.
    2. Let them begin to cool.
    3. Squeeze the juice out while they are cooling.
    4. Mix in sugar to taste.
    5. Serve.

    Chef Tips

    • Squeeze while cooling. That is the whole technique and the source is specific about it — hot fruit burns your hands and cold pulp will not give up its juice.
    • Very ripe mangoes are what this is for, the same way omunanansi is for over-ripe pineapples. It is a glut drink.
    • Squeeze by hand through cloth rather than blending, as with the passion fruit in Lemongrass Passion Fruit Juice. Blending shreds fibre into the juice.
    • Sugar to taste, at the end. No quantity is documented.
    • It belongs to a set of Lugbara drinks: ebe'de or ibe'de is the unsweetened self-service tea named after the scooping action; kuruku chai is lemongrass boiled in tea, Kuruku Chai; anya i'di is millet porridge with groundnut or sesame paste; maize milk is Maize Milk.
    • No quantities and no cooking time are documented anywhere. Nothing here is estimated.
    • Chill it before serving if you can. Warm fruit juice tastes markedly more acidic.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Squeezing the fruit hot.
    • Waiting until it is cold.
    • Blending instead of squeezing.
    • Using underripe mangoes.
    • Sweetening before cooking.

    Health Benefits

    • Mangoes are very high in vitamins A and C.
    • Cooking loses some vitamin C but makes the fruit keep.
    • No fat and no dairy.
    • Sweetened to taste, so the sugar is under your control.

    Cultural Note

    Cooking fruit to make a drink rather than eating it fresh is a preservation move, and West Nile mangoes come all at once. It is the same logic as omunanansi's boiled pineapple peelings — nothing is wasted and nothing is eaten only one way.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days.

    Reheating: Served cold, or warm if you prefer.

    Serve with: Mukati, Akarafi, Kuruku Chai
