🌺 Malakwang with Simsim and Groundnut

Malakwang — amalakwang

Sour roselle leaves boiled ten minutes and drained, then blended with equal weights of groundnut and simsim paste — two hundred grams of each — before the rest of the water goes back in.

Malakwang is the sour roselle leaf, and this version uses both pastes in equal measure: two hundred grams of groundnut and two hundred of sesame. That balance is northern practice — simsim is as much a northern staple as groundnut, and odii, the roasted mix of the two, is the region's signature condiment. The leaves are boiled and then drained completely before the pastes go in, which is what keeps the sourness in check. Three varieties exist: white, bitter and sour.

Ingredients

Equipment: Saucepan with a lid, Wooden spoon or mingling stick

  • 3 to 4 clusters of malakwang
  • 200 g groundnut paste
  • 200 g simsim paste
  • 2 tumpecos of water or stock
  • Salt and pepper, to taste

Method

  1. Separate the leaves from the stems and wash them thoroughly.
  2. Put the greens in half the water in a saucepan.
  3. Bring to the boil covered and cook about 10 minutes.
  4. Drain the water off.
  5. Add the simsim and groundnut pastes and mix until well blended.
  6. Add the remaining water and cook, adding salt and pepper to taste.
  7. Taste and adjust the seasoning before serving. Serve immediately.

Chef Tips

  • Equal weights of groundnut and simsim paste. That is what makes this northern rather than central, and leaving the sesame out gives you a different and less interesting dish.
  • Drain the boiling water off completely before the pastes go in. Malakwang is sour, and that water carries a lot of it.
  • A tumpeco is a Ugandan enamel mug, and it is a real unit of measure in a Ugandan kitchen. Two tumpecos is roughly two mugfuls — the source gives no millilitre equivalent and none is invented here.
  • Half the water for the boil, half after. The second half is what makes the sauce.
  • Serve immediately. Groundnut and simsim sauces thicken and stiffen as they cool.
  • Three varieties are named in Acholi: white is makwa, bitter is ma wach, and sour is ma boot. Which you have changes how much the boiling water matters.
  • Malakwang is traditionally avoided at weddings, because its sourness is read as a bad omen. That is worth knowing before you serve it at one.
  • Local salt — magadi — is sometimes added to reduce the sourness, and a boneless dry fish stirred in is a common enrichment.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Leaving the simsim out.
  • Not draining the boiling water.
  • Adding all the water at once.
  • Letting it sit before serving.
  • Serving it at a wedding without knowing what it signifies.

Health Benefits

  • Roselle leaves are high in vitamin C, iron, calcium and organic acids.
  • Groundnut and simsim pastes together give protein, calcium, magnesium and vitamin E.
  • No added oil — the fat is all in the two pastes.
  • Contains peanuts and sesame.

Cultural Note

Malakwang emerged as a drought-period food and became embedded in Acholi cultural practice, which is a common pattern — the dishes that get loved hardest are often the ones that got people through something. The wedding prohibition is the sharpest example in Ugandan food of a taste carrying a meaning.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days.

Reheating: Warm gently with a little water.

Serve with: Boiled Sweet Potatoes, Posho, Kalo
