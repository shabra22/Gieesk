🥜 Groundnut Sauce with Dried Fish

Binyebwa n'ekyennyanja ekyakalilye

Dried fish soaked an hour, fried onions, and a cup of groundnut paste simmered uncovered for a full hour — with the salt going in only when the sauce is nearly ready.

Groundnut sauce is the national sauce of Uganda and this is the dried-fish version, which is how it is most often eaten away from the lake. Two instructions are worth taking seriously. The fish soaks a full hour in two cups of water before it goes anywhere, and the sauce simmers uncovered for an hour, which is what cooks the groundnuts properly rather than just heating them. The salt goes in at the end, not the start.

Ingredients

Equipment: Bowl, for soaking, Large saucepan, Wooden spoon

  • 1 small dried fish, coarsely chopped, bones removed
  • 4 to 5 cups water
  • 10 ml (2 tsp) vegetable oil
  • 2 onions, chopped
  • 10 ml (2 tsp) cayenne pepper (optional)
  • 5 ml (1 tsp) curry powder
  • 240 ml (1 cup) groundnut paste
  • Salt, to taste

Listed in the source but never used in its method

  • 4 tomatoes, chopped

Method

  1. Soak the dried fish in 2 cups of the water for about 1 hour, until soft. Drain and pat dry.
  2. Fry the onions in the oil in a large saucepan until brown, about 5 minutes.
  3. Add the fish, cayenne pepper, curry powder, the remaining water and the groundnut paste.
  4. Simmer uncovered for about 1 hour.
  5. Add salt when the sauce is nearly ready.
  6. Taste and correct the seasoning before serving.

Chef Tips

  • An hour of soaking for the fish, and an hour of uncovered simmering for the sauce. Neither is padding — groundnut paste that has only been warmed through tastes raw and chalky.
  • Uncovered is deliberate. It reduces as it cooks and that is what thickens it.
  • Salt at the end. Groundnut sauce reduces by a good deal in an hour and salted early it will finish over-seasoned.
  • The printed ingredient list includes four chopped tomatoes which its method never uses. They are listed here for completeness and you can add them with the onions if you want them; the source does not.
  • Fry the onions properly brown, not just soft. Five minutes at a real heat.
  • Remove the fish bones before soaking, as the recipe says. Afterwards they are far harder to find.
  • Groundnuts were introduced to Uganda around 1862 and localised farming began at Serere in 1930 — it is a relatively recent crop for something so central.
  • Pounding groundnuts in a mortar rather than machine-grinding them is said to release the oils better, and that is the traditional preference.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Cutting the hour-long simmer.
  • Covering the pan.
  • Salting at the start.
  • Using dried fish without soaking it.
  • Frying the onions only until translucent.

Health Benefits

  • Groundnuts supply plant protein, niacin, folate, magnesium and vitamin E.
  • Dried fish adds animal protein, calcium and vitamin B12.
  • Only two teaspoons of added oil across five servings.
  • Contains peanuts and fish.

Cultural Note

Binyebwa is the sauce that makes Ugandan food Ugandan — it goes over matooke, over sweet potato, into luwombo, into malakwang, into cassava leaves. Its ubiquity is why it turns up in this collection in half a dozen forms rather than one, and the versions genuinely differ.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days. It thickens considerably.

Reheating: Warm gently with a splash of water.

Serve with: Matooke, Posho, Boiled Sweet Potatoes
