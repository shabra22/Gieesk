🧈 Edo Egusi Soup

Egusi — with une

Four cups of egusi worked into a thick paste and lowered into seven or eight cups of stock a teaspoonful at a time, kept whole — knots of cream through the soup.

This is the Edo way with egusi, and it is a matter of keeping the seed in discrete pieces rather than letting it disperse. Four cups of ground melon seed are worked with a cup of blended onion and chilli into a thick paste, then spooned into the simmering stock a teaspoonful at a time and left alone for twenty to thirty minutes while the mounds cook through. Three leaves follow in order: pumpkin, waterleaf, then bitter leaf with the lid off. The author calls the result knots of cream, and it feeds six to eight.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large pot, Blender, Teaspoon, for the mounds, Mixing bowl

  • 4 cups egusi (melon seeds), dry-ground or milled
  • 1 cup blended onions and fresh chilies, to taste (about 5 small onions)
  • 1/2 - 1 cup palm oil
  • 2 tsp fresh une (iru, locust beans)
  • Pinch of salt
  • Ground crayfish, to taste
  • 7-8 cups of stock
  • Cooked meat and fish, quantity and variety to preference — beef, tripe, stockfish, oxtail, biscuit-bone, snails and pomo
  • 2 cups pumpkin leaves (ugu), cut
  • 1 cup waterleaf, cut
  • 3 tbsp bitter leaf, washed

Method

  1. Make a thick paste of the ground egusi and the blended onion and chilli mixture.
  2. Heat the palm oil on low-medium for a minute and add the une or iru.
  3. Slowly add the stock.
  4. Scoop teaspoonfuls of the egusi paste into the stock, keeping the pieces whole.
  5. Simmer 20 to 30 minutes, until the balls cook through — they change from a thickened, orange appearance.
  6. Gently add the cooked meat and fish, and season.
  7. Add the pumpkin leaves, then the waterleaf. Stir, cover, and cook 5 to 10 minutes until wilted.
  8. Finish with the bitter leaf, lid off, for another 5 to 10 minutes.

Chef Tips

  • A teaspoon, not a tablespoon. The mounds want to be small enough to cook through in twenty to thirty minutes.
  • Lower them in and leave them. Stirring at this stage is how you end up with the dispersed, watery style instead.
  • A thick paste, not a batter. If it will not hold its shape on the spoon there is too much liquid in the onion blend.
  • Leaves in order: pumpkin first, then waterleaf, then bitter leaf. Waterleaf releases water, which is why it follows rather than leads.
  • Lid off for the bitter leaf. Covered, the bitterness concentrates — the same principle as the ten-minute open par-boil in Omoebe with Banga Juice.
  • Une is the Edo word for iru, fermented locust bean. Two teaspoons of the fresh kind is a small amount with a large effect.
  • Seven to eight cups of stock to four cups of egusi is a generous ratio. The balls need room, which is the opposite of what a thick caked egusi needs.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Scooping with a tablespoon, so the centres stay raw.
  • Stirring once the mounds are in.
  • A paste too loose to hold its shape.
  • Waterleaf before pumpkin leaf.
  • Covering the pot for the bitter leaf.

Health Benefits

  • Melon seed is high in protein, magnesium and zinc.
  • Three different leaves give a broad micronutrient range.
  • Very low in carbohydrate.
  • Fermented locust bean contributes B vitamins and glutamates.

Cultural Note

Knots of cream is the author's phrase for what distinguishes this soup, and it captures the whole intention: the egusi is meant to be encountered in pieces, soft set mounds you break into with the swallow, rather than as a uniform thickness. That the Edo and the Yoruba do it this way while the Igbo disperse theirs is one of the cleaner regional distinctions in Nigerian cooking — though the sources disagree about who belongs on which side.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 4 days refrigerated. The balls firm up and hold.

Reheating: Low heat, no stirring, so the mounds survive.

Serve with: Iyan, Starch, Eba or garri
