🍠 Asaro

Asaro — yam porridge, ebe

Yam cut small so it cooks faster, simmered twenty to twenty-five minutes in a dried-down tomato base with a cup and a half of water, then mashed to a complete puree.

Asaro is yam porridge, and the version here is the mashed kind rather than the chunky kind. Small cubes go into a tomato, pepper and onion blend that has already been boiled down for fifteen minutes until the water has gone, with only a cup and a half of liquid added — then cooked until very soft and worked with a wooden spoon until completely pureed. Palm oil rather than vegetable oil gives it the orange colour it is known for. The suya bites scattered over the top in the original are a modern addition, not part of the dish.

Ingredients

Equipment: Medium pot, Wooden spoon, Blender

  • 8 pieces of medium-size yam cubes
  • 2 cooking spoons tomato, pepper and onion blend
  • 1 1/2 cups water
  • 2 cooking spoons palm oil
  • 1 tbsp dry crayfish, ground
  • Seasoning cube, to taste
  • Salt to taste

Optional topping, the author's own addition

  • 2 sticks suya or more, oven-dried and chopped

Method

  1. Blend the tomato, pepper and onion and bring to the boil for about 15 minutes, or till the water dries up.
  2. Peel the yam and cut it into small cubes. This gets the yam cooking faster.
  3. Add the yam to the tomato pot with 1 1/2 cups of water and all the other ingredients, and cook 20 to 25 minutes, or until the yam is very soft. Halfway through, taste and correct the seasoning.
  4. Lower the heat and use a wooden spoon to mash the yam until completely pureed.
  5. Turn off the heat and serve.
  6. If you are adding the suya, put it in the oven about 5 to 10 minutes to dry out a little, cool it, chop into bites and sprinkle over the top.

Chef Tips

  • Small cubes, deliberately. It is the one thing the author explains: it gets the yam cooking faster and it also makes mashing easier.
  • Dry the tomato blend out first — fifteen minutes, until the water has gone. Wet base plus wet yam gives you soup.
  • A cup and a half of water is not much. Yam releases its own moisture and you want a porridge, not a boil.
  • Mash on low heat, in the pot, with a wooden spoon. The residual heat keeps it pliable.
  • Palm oil, not vegetable oil. The colour and the flavour both come from it.
  • Taste halfway, as the recipe says. Yam absorbs salt and seasoning unevenly and it is much easier to correct early.
  • The suya topping is a modern composition and the author does not flag it as hers. It is good, but it is not asaro.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Cutting the yam in large chunks and then trying to mash it.
  • Not drying out the tomato base.
  • Adding too much water.
  • Mashing off the heat, when it has already stiffened.
  • Using vegetable oil, which gives a pale, flat porridge.

Health Benefits

  • Yam provides potassium, vitamin C and resistant starch.
  • Red palm oil supplies carotenoids and vitamin E.
  • Lower glycaemic response than rice or white bread.
  • Naturally gluten-free.

Cultural Note

Asaro is household food rather than party food — the thing made on a weekday from yam that is a little past its best. Its author frames it exactly that way: month end, money dwindling, no restaurant meals, which in Nigerian English becomes 'month don pregnant'. Yam porridge is what a kitchen produces when it is being sensible.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 3 days refrigerated.

Reheating: Low heat with a splash of water. It stiffens considerably when cold.

Serve with: Suya, Fried fish, Dodo (fried plantain)
