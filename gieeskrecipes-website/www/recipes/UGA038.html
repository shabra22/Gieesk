🌾 Roasted Simsim

Simsim

Sesame, salt and fifteen millilitres of water stirred continuously in a dry pan for ten minutes — the water is what carries the salt onto the seed before it flashes off.

This is the base for a great deal of northern Ugandan cooking: roasted sesame, eaten as it is, ground into paste, or mixed with groundnut. The one detail that makes it work is fifteen millilitres of water going in with the salt. It seems pointless in a dry roast, and it is what gets the salt to stick to the seed rather than sitting in the bottom of the pan. Then it evaporates and the roasting proper begins.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large saucepan or frying pan, Wooden spatula, Large tray

  • 500 g sesame seeds
  • 2.5 ml (1/2 tsp) salt
  • 15 ml water

Method

  1. Put all the ingredients in the saucepan and mix well.
  2. Turn the heat on and fry for about 10 minutes, stirring continuously.
  3. Test it by squashing a few seeds between your thumb and first finger.
  4. They should be golden brown, with a lovely aroma.
  5. Turn the heat off and pour the seeds out onto a large tray, spreading them out to stop them cooking any further.

Chef Tips

  • The fifteen millilitres of water is the technique. It carries the salt onto the seed and then evaporates — without it the salt never adheres.
  • Stir continuously for the whole ten minutes. Sesame is small and burns in seconds once it starts to colour.
  • The test is squashing a few seeds between thumb and finger. They should crush easily and smell strongly nutty.
  • Pour them out onto a tray the moment they are done. Left in a hot pan they keep cooking and go bitter.
  • Spread them thin on the tray. A heap holds its heat.
  • This is the starting point for sesame paste, for simsim snaps, and for the sesame-and-groundnut mixes that go into northern porridges and vegetables.
  • Toasted sesame goes stale faster than raw. Roast what you will use in a fortnight.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Leaving the water out.
  • Stirring intermittently.
  • Leaving them in the pan after the heat goes off.
  • Roasting past golden brown.
  • Heaping them on the tray.

Health Benefits

  • Sesame is one of the richest plant sources of calcium.
  • High in magnesium, iron, zinc and plant protein.
  • Contains lignans and vitamin E.
  • Dry-roasted with no oil at all.

Cultural Note

Simsim is grown across northern Uganda and is one of the ingredients that most clearly distinguishes northern cooking from the banana-based south. Roasted like this it goes into everything — porridge, vegetables, pastes and snacks.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 2 weeks in an airtight container.

Reheating: A minute in a dry pan if they soften.

Serve with: Millet Porridge with Tamarind, Simsim Sesame Snaps, Ugandan tea
