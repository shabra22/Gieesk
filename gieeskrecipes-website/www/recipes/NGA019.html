Obe ata — Nigerian pepper mix, stew base

The blend almost every Nigerian stew starts from, and the only source that puts numbers on it: roughly 50% bell pepper, 30% tomatoes, 15% onions, 5% scotch bonnet.

Obe ata is the base, not a dish — the blended pepper mix that goes into jollof, into stews, into rice and into nearly everything else with a red sauce. Every recipe assumes you know the ratio and almost none of them state it. This one does, and the number is surprising: it is pepper-led rather than tomato-led, half bell pepper against under a third tomato. Made in quantity and kept in the fridge or freezer, it turns a two-hour stew into a thirty-minute one.

Ingredients

Equipment: Blender, Freezer bags or jars, Sieve (optional, to drain excess water)

  • 4 bell peppers
  • 6 tomatoes, medium size, or 1 can plum or chopped
  • 2 onions, medium size
  • 3 scotch bonnet peppers
  • Water, for blending — see note

Method

  1. Remove the stalks from the peppers and tomatoes and wash them. Peel the onions.
  2. Remove the seeds from the peppers if you are using a home blender — the seeds blunt the blades and taste bitter.
  3. Cut everything into pieces that your blender will handle.
  4. Blend with some water to a smooth puree.
  5. Use at once, or portion into bags or jars and refrigerate or freeze.

Chef Tips

  • The ratio is the point: roughly 50% bell pepper, 30% tomatoes, 15% onions, 5% scotch bonnet. That makes the base sweet and pepper-forward rather than acidic.
  • Deseed the bell peppers for a home blender. Commercial blenders cope; domestic ones chew the seeds and the bitterness carries through.
  • Blend with as little water as you can get away with. Every drop has to be boiled off later.
  • If it comes out loose, let it drain in a sieve before you freeze it — or boil it down before frying.
  • Freeze flat in bags. It thaws in minutes and you can snap off what you need.
  • This is the base for red stews. For the green ayamase family the architecture is completely different — green peppers, bleached oil, no tomato at all.
  • Tatashe rather than bell pepper is the traditional reading, and the same author identifies tatashe as poblano. Bell pepper is the widely accepted substitute, including by her.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Blending with too much water.
  • Leaving the bell pepper seeds in.
  • Making it tomato-led, which gives a thin acidic stew.
  • Freezing it in one solid block.
  • Using it straight without cooking the water out.

Health Benefits

  • Bell pepper is one of the richest food sources of vitamin C.
  • Tomatoes supply lycopene, which becomes more available once cooked.
  • Very low in calories and fat.
  • No added salt, sugar or oil.

Cultural Note

Keeping obe ata in the freezer is ordinary Nigerian household practice and it is the single biggest labour saving in the cuisine. The ratio is the kind of knowledge that passes between people rather than onto pages, which is why one named author writing it down as percentages is more useful than it looks.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 5 days refrigerated, 6 months frozen. Freeze flat in portions.

Reheating: Not applicable — it is a raw base. Cook the water out before frying.

Serve with: Jollof Rice, Boiled white rice, Any Nigerian red stew

  • THE RATIO, verbatim and the reason this entry exists: 'roughly around 50% bell pepper, 30% tomatoes, 15% onions and 5% scotch bonnet'. This is the only quantified tomato-to-pepper ratio found in any source consulted, and every jollof recipe examined dodged the question.
  • LIST/METHOD MISMATCH, flagged: water appears in the method — 'Add into a blender with some water' — but not in the ingredient list, and no quantity is given.
  • COMPARE the three jollof architectures, which are completely different from each other and from this: Food52's party jollof uses 6 tomatoes to 6 red poblano to 1 scotch bonnet, roughly even; Kitchen Butterfly's own site uses 6 tomatoes to half a fresh chili and NO sweet pepper at all; All Nigerian Recipes leaves the ratio entirely unquantified. Three sources, three architectures, and this is the fourth.
  • ON TATASHE, a live dispute among named sources. Abi Olayiwola identifies tatashe as the poblano of Mexican cooking, 'a mildly hot pepper which belongs to the same Capsicum annum group as bell peppers', with bell pepper as the substitute. Food52's jollof calls for '6 fresh red poblano peppers (or 4 large red bell peppers)', consistent with her. But Lola Osinkolu calls tatashe 'dried red bell peppers', and All Nigerian Recipes treats '3 tatashe peppers' as interchangeable with 800g of tomato puree, both implying the bell reading. Two sources say poblano, two imply red bell.
  • Other pepper identifications from the same author, uncontested across sources: ata rodo is scotch bonnet or habanero; shombo, also called bawa, is cayenne. GAP: ata gungun is not identified by any source consulted.
