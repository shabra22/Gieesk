💛 Ncha

Ncha — nkwobi sauce, ngo

The palm-oil-and-alkali emulsion that underlies abacha, nkwobi, isi ewu and ugba — half a cup of oil to two teaspoons of potash dissolved in a quarter cup of water, stirred until it emulsifies.

Ncha is not a dish, it is the technique that several southeastern dishes are built on, and it is worth knowing by name. Palm oil is mixed with ground ehuru, crayfish, seasoning, scotch bonnet and onion; potash is dissolved separately in water and sieved so no sediment goes in; then the potash water is poured slowly into the oil while stirring until the whole thing emulsifies and turns from red liquid to thick yellow paste. That same emulsion dresses African salad, coats cow foot in nkwobi and goat head in isi ewu. One sauce, four dishes.

Ingredients

Equipment: Clean dry bowl or pot, Wooden spatula, Fine sieve, Small bowl for the potash

  • 1/2 cup palm oil
  • 2 tsp potash (akanwu), dissolved in 1/4 cup water and sieved
  • 1 tsp ehuru powder (calabash nutmeg)
  • 1 tsp seasoning powder
  • 2 1/4 tsp crayfish powder
  • Scotch bonnet, to taste
  • 1 tbsp ugba
  • 1 tbsp chopped scent leaf
  • 1 tbsp chopped onions
  • Salt to taste

Method

  1. Combine the palm oil with the ehuru, crayfish, seasoning, scotch bonnet and chopped onions in a clean dry bowl.
  2. Dissolve the potash in the quarter cup of water and sieve it, leaving the sediment behind.
  3. Pour the potash mix slowly into the palm oil mix, stirring continuously.
  4. Keep stirring until your sauce is emulsified — it will go from red and liquid to thick and yellow.
  5. Fold in the ugba and the chopped scent leaf.
  6. Use at once to dress abacha, nkwobi or isi ewu.

Chef Tips

  • A clean dry bowl. Water in the vessel breaks the emulsion before it forms, which is exactly why abacha is mixed off the stove.
  • Sieve the potash water and leave the sediment. The undissolved particles are gritty and taste harsh.
  • Pour slowly, stir continuously. Dumped in, the alkali does not disperse and you get a split sauce with yellow lumps in it.
  • Thick, not fluid, is the target. If it is still pourable you have added too much potash water.
  • The colour change is the cue and it is unmistakable — red oil turns opaque yellow and thickens visibly.
  • Two teaspoons of potash to half a cup of oil is the only clean oil-to-alkali ratio found in any named source. Use it as your starting point.
  • This same emulsion goes by other names: Sisi Jemimah calls hers Ngo sauce, and All Nigerian Recipes describes the identical process without naming it at all.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Mixing in a wet bowl.
  • Pouring the potash water in all at once.
  • Including the potash sediment.
  • Adding too much potash water, so it stays liquid.
  • Warming it, which is unnecessary and can break it.

Health Benefits

  • Red palm oil supplies carotenoids and vitamin E.
  • Ehuru and scent leaf are traditionally used as digestives.
  • No cooking at all, so nothing is degraded by heat.
  • Very low in carbohydrate.

Cultural Note

Learning that ncha is a named thing rather than a step changes how the southeastern repertoire reads. Abacha, nkwobi, isi ewu and dressed ugba are not four unrelated dishes but four things you can put the same emulsion on, and the knowledge that matters is the emulsion. The same logic turns up in the Delta, where owo is made creamy by exactly this move.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Use immediately. The emulsion separates within a few hours.

Reheating: Do not reheat. Make it fresh.

Serve with: Abacha, Nkwobi, Isi Ewu
