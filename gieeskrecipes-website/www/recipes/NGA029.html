🇧🇷 Akara Acarajé

Akara / acarajé

The Atlantic version — one cup of beans soaked no more than two to three hours, ground with onion, fried in three to four centimetres of oil.

This is akara written as a bridge between Lagos and Bahia. The recipe is deliberately small and simple — one cup of beans, an onion, half a cup of water — and it carries one piece of advice the other version does not: soak for two to three hours and no longer, because beans left in water absorb too much of it and come out tasteless. The batter can also be rested several hours or overnight to improve its flavour, or frozen for a month. Served in Nigeria with soft white bread or ogi; in Brazil, with dried shrimp, vatapá and caruru.

Ingredients

Equipment: Blender or grinding mill, Colander, Deep pan, wok or skillet, Slotted spoon

  • 1 cup black-eyed beans, or bean/gram flour as an alternative
  • 1 small onion, peeled and chopped roughly
  • 1/2 cup water, or more as needed
  • Chopped chilli pepper (optional)
  • Salt to taste
  • Chopped onions and dried shrimp (optional)
  • Vegetable oil, for deep frying
  • Bread, to serve

Method

  1. Sort the dried beans and remove any stones and debris.
  2. Rinse under running water, then soak 2 to 3 hours — not longer, because the beans absorb excess water and become tasteless.
  3. Rub the beans between your hands to remove the skins. Repeat rinsing in a colander until they are nearly clean.
  4. Grind the beans with the onions, water and chilli until a smooth thick paste forms.
  5. Optionally rest the batter several hours or overnight to improve the flavour, or freeze it for up to a month.
  6. Heat 3 to 4 cm of oil in a deep pan, wok or skillet.
  7. Stir the batter and season with salt — taste to verify the salt is detectable.
  8. Using a teaspoon, tablespoon or ice cream scoop, lower the batter gently into the hot oil. Do not overcrowd.
  9. Fry until the underside browns, flip, and fry the other side.
  10. Remove with a slotted spoon.

Chef Tips

  • Two to three hours, no more. The reason is given: beans soaked longer absorb excess water and the akara comes out tasteless.
  • Taste the batter for salt before you fry. You cannot correct it afterwards and under-salted akara is flat.
  • Three to four centimetres of oil. Enough to float them, not enough to be a deep-fat fryer.
  • Resting the batter overnight genuinely improves the flavour, and it freezes for a month — useful if you want akara on a weekday morning.
  • European-polished beans do not give up their skins the same way West African varieties do. If the skins will not come off, bean flour or gram flour is the honest substitute.
  • An ice cream scoop gives the most even shape if you are not confident with a spoon.
  • The Brazilian version is fried in dendê — palm oil — which is the same material as Nigerian red palm oil under its Portuguese name.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Soaking overnight, which washes the flavour out.
  • Frying unsalted batter.
  • Crowding the pan.
  • Grinding with too much water.
  • Expecting polished imported beans to peel like West African ones.

Cultural Note

Akara is one of the clearest surviving threads between Yorubaland and the Black Atlantic. In Salvador the baianas de acarajé sell it from street stalls in white lace, and the dish is formally recognised Brazilian heritage; in Lagos the same fritter goes into a split of agege bread for breakfast. The argument in the comments of this very recipe — whether acarajé comes from a corrupted invitation or specifically from akara ijesha — is the kind of question food history leaves open.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Batter 2 days refrigerated or 1 month frozen. Fried akara, eat the same day.

Reheating: Hot oven or air fryer, 5 minutes.

Serve with: Agege Bread, Eko, Ogi or pap
