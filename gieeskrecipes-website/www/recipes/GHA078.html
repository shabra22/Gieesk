🟫 Dawadawa

Dawadawa — dori, koligu, fermented locust bean

An alkaline fermentation — the pH rises to 8.1 or higher and the finished seeds are sticky, pungent and covered with a greyish mucilaginous layer.

Dawadawa is fermented African locust bean and it is the deepest savoury note in West African cooking. Crucially it is an ALKALINE fermentation: where a souring goes acid, this goes basic, with the pH rising from near neutral to 8.1 or higher as protein breaks down to amino acids and ammonia. Seeds are boiled twelve to twenty-four hours to soften the hard testa, de-hulled, boiled again, then wrapped and left two to four days at ambient temperature. The endpoint is visible and smellable: sticky, pungent, covered with a greyish mucilaginous layer. Every Ghanaian source consulted gave a time with no cue; the usable endpoint is Nigerian and labelled as such.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large pot, Log mortar and pestle, Fabric bag or banana leaves, A weight, for pressing

  • African locust bean seeds (Parkia biglobosa)
  • Wood ash, for the pounding stage in the Ghanaian method
  • Water, for repeated boiling and washing

Jute bags or banana leaves, for the fermentation

    No household quantities are published. See the note.

      Method

      1. Dry and sort the locust bean seeds.
      2. Boil them 12 to 24 hours, to soften the hard testa.
      3. Pound with wood ash in a mortar, then wash repeatedly to remove the broken testa.
      4. Boil the kernels again — about an hour in the documented endpoint source, two to four hours in the Ghanaian accounts.
      5. Drain, and place in a fabric bag weighted to press away excess water, or wrap in banana leaves.
      6. Ferment 2 to 4 days at ambient temperature.

      ENDPOINT: the pH rises from near neutral to 8.1 or higher. The seeds become sticky with a strong ammoniacal smell, and the colour changes from light brown to dark brown under a greyish mucilaginous layer.

      1. Press into cakes or balls.

      This endpoint is documented for Nigerian practice; every Ghanaian source consulted gives a time without a cue. See the note.

        Chef Tips

        • It is an ALKALINE fermentation and that is the thing to understand. Proper dawadawa goes basic, to pH 8.1 or above; a souring means something has gone wrong.
        • The endpoint is a smell and a surface: strong ammoniacal, sticky, with a greyish mucilaginous layer, and the colour shifting from light brown to dark brown.
        • Twelve to twenty-four hours of boiling at the start sounds absurd and is correct — the testa on a locust bean seed is extremely hard.
        • Wood ash in the pounding stage is the Ghanaian documented practice. Wood ash is genuinely alkaline, which presumably helps set the fermentation's direction.
        • A consumer survey found that good quality dawadawa has a strong ammonia-like smell and a dark brown colour. That is the market test.
        • Temperature rises during the ferment — from about 30 degrees to a maximum of about 50 in twenty-four hours, by one cited account. That self-heating is a sign it is working.
        • Only women produce dawadawa, by one organisation's account of the Ghanaian practice. Use it by the tablespoon: two tablespoons seasons a whole soup.

        Common Mistakes to Avoid

        • Expecting it to sour rather than go alkaline.
        • Cutting the initial boil short, leaving the testa intact.
        • Skipping the repeated washing after pounding.
        • Fermenting in a sealed container rather than a breathable bag or leaves.
        • Using too much in a dish — it is extremely potent.

        Health Benefits

        • Fermentation raises free amino acids and B vitamins substantially.
        • A significant plant protein source in northern Ghanaian diets.
        • Documented as reducing antinutrients in the raw seed.
        • Used by the tablespoon, so it contributes flavour rather than calories.

        Cultural Note

        Dawadawa is the ingredient that makes northern Ghanaian soups taste like themselves, and the alkaline fermentation behind it is the same chemistry as natto or Japanese fermented soybean — protein broken down to ammonia rather than sugar to acid. That only women produce it, by one account, and that herbs are laid on top in transit to ward off spirits, says how seriously it is taken.

        Storage & Serving

        Storage: Months as dried cakes or balls, kept sealed.

        Reheating: Not applicable. Bloomed in hot oil or added to the pot.

        Serve with: Ayoyo Soup, Bito Soup, Garden Egg Stew
