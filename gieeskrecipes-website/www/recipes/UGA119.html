🥬 Boo with Odii

Boo

Cowpea leaves, fresh or dried, boiled down and finished with odii — the roasted sesame and groundnut paste that makes it northern rather than just greens.

Boo is cowpea leaves, and in Acholi it is not a side dish — it is one of the foods northern Ugandans are most attached to, and it stands alongside dek ngor, malakwang and lakotokoto as a dish that has held its place. What distinguishes it from the same leaf cooked further south is odii: the roasted sesame-and-groundnut paste of the north goes in rather than plain groundnut, and that changes it completely. Dried leaves are used as much as fresh.

Ingredients

Equipment: Saucepan with a lid, Bowl, Ogwec — a stirring stick

  • Cowpea leaves (boo), fresh or dried
  • Water
  • Odii — roasted sesame and groundnut paste
  • Salt, or magadi

Optional

  • Moo yaa — shea butter

No quantities are documented for the odii version

    Method

    1. If using dried leaves, soak them until soft. Wash and chop fresh leaves.
    2. Boil the leaves in salted water until tender. Cowpea leaves want a genuinely long boil — 20 to 30 minutes.
    3. Loosen the odii with a little water to a smooth paste.
    4. Stir it through the leaves.
    5. Simmer a further 15 minutes.
    6. Add shea butter if you want it, and serve with kwon kal.

    Chef Tips

    • Odii, not plain groundnut paste. The roasted sesame-and-groundnut mix in Odii is what makes this a northern dish, and substituting peanut butter gives you the southern version in Cowpea Leaf and Groundnut Soup instead.
    • Twenty to thirty minutes of boiling. Cowpea leaves are tougher than amaranth or collards and they want it.
    • Loosen the odii with water before it goes in. Every paste in Ugandan cooking follows that rule.
    • Dried boo is used as much as fresh and is what a household eats out of season. Soak it first.
    • Shea butter — moo yaa — at the end is the northern finish and a spoonful changes the dish.
    • Magadi in place of salt is traditional and it also softens the leaves.
    • No quantities are documented for the odii version specifically. The documented Slow Food cowpea recipe in Cowpea Leaf and Groundnut Soup gives 300 g of leaves, 300 ml of salted water, 4 to 5 tablespoons of oil, 2 onions, 3 tomatoes and 250 g of groundnut paste, with a 20-to-30-minute boil and a 15-minute simmer, and that is the nearest usable guide — but it is the groundnut version, not this one.
    • Boo stands with dek ngor, malakwang and lakotokoto as one of the four Acholi dishes that people write essays about. It is worth cooking all four to understand the region.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Using plain groundnut paste instead of odii.
    • Treating it as a quick-cooking green.
    • Adding the paste undissolved.
    • Using dried leaves without soaking them.
    • Boiling hard after the paste is in.

    Health Benefits

    • Cowpea leaves are high in protein for a green, plus iron, calcium and vitamins A and C.
    • Odii brings both sesame's calcium and groundnut's protein and vitamin E.
    • The fat in the paste helps absorb the leaf's carotenoids.
    • Contains peanuts and sesame.

    Cultural Note

    The same leaf is a pleasant side dish in central Uganda and a dish people are homesick for in the north, and the difference is one ingredient. Odii is what northern Ugandan food tastes like, and boo is the plainest demonstration of it.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days.

    Reheating: Warm gently with a little water.

    Serve with: Karo, Boiled Sweet Potatoes, Dek Ngor

That recipe appears in this collection as Cowpea Leaf and Groundnut Soup and its timings are used here as a guide, which is stated rather than implied.
    • Odii as the signature northern condiment, magadi as the rock salt used in northern cooking, moo yaa as the shea butter that is the northern cooking fat, and boo's standing alongside dek ngor, malakwang and lakotokoto are all documented in the northern Ugandan sources consulted, including the Daily Monitor's Acholi food coverage.
    • The regional names for cowpea — empindi, mashaza, eggobe — are from the Slow Food booklet, p19.
