Pasta Primavera

Pasta Primavera celebrates spring's brightest vegetables — asparagus, peas, zucchini, and cherry tomatoes — tossed with pasta in a light garlic-and-olive-oil sauce finished with Parmigiano.

Meaning 'spring pasta,' this dish is built to showcase whatever vegetables are freshest and most vibrant at the market, making it naturally adaptable across seasons while keeping its core philosophy of lightness and color.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large pot, Large skillet

  • 400 g (14 oz) penne or fusilli
  • 3 tbsp olive oil
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 bunch asparagus, trimmed and cut into 3cm pieces
  • 1 zucchini, sliced into half-moons
  • 150 g (1 cup) fresh or frozen peas
  • 200 g (7 oz) cherry tomatoes, halved
  • 60 ml (¼ cup) dry white wine
  • 60 g (¾ cup) grated Parmigiano-Reggiano, plus more for serving
  • Handful of fresh basil, torn
  • Salt and pepper, to taste

Method

  1. Bring a large pot of salted water to a boil and cook the pasta until al dente.
  2. Meanwhile, heat the olive oil in a large skillet over medium heat. Add the garlic and cook for 30 seconds until fragrant.
  3. Add the asparagus and zucchini, cooking for 4–5 minutes until just tender-crisp.
  4. Add the peas and cherry tomatoes, cooking for 2–3 minutes more.
  5. Pour in the white wine and simmer for 1–2 minutes.
  6. Reserve some pasta water, then drain the pasta and add directly to the skillet.
  7. Toss everything together, adding pasta water and the Parmigiano, tossing until glossy and well combined.
  8. Stir in the basil, season with salt and pepper, and serve immediately with extra Parmigiano.

Chef Tips

  • Keep the vegetables tender-crisp rather than fully soft — the dish should feel bright and fresh, not stewed.
  • Feel free to swap in whatever seasonal vegetables are freshest — this dish is meant to be flexible.
  • Add heartier vegetables first and quicker-cooking ones later to avoid uneven doneness.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Overcooking the vegetables until mushy, losing the dish's signature freshness and texture contrast.
  • Crowding the pan, which steams rather than sautés the vegetables.
  • Under-seasoning — a dish this light needs careful attention to salt and acidity.

Cultural Note

Though it carries an Italian name, Pasta Primavera as commonly known today was popularized in New York in the 1970s, blending Italian technique with an American emphasis on fresh, colorful vegetables.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Refrigerate for up to 2 days, though the vegetables are best enjoyed fresh.

Reheating: Reheat gently in a skillet to avoid overcooking the vegetables further.

Serve with: A crisp Pinot Grigio, Crusty bread, A simple side salad
