🍢 Steamed Bitter Yams

Kobe — lisankonyo; Balugu — ndaggu

Five kilos of air yam bulbils soaked and then steamed in banana leaves for up to two hours — the soaking and the long cook are what detoxify them, not a preference.

Kobe are the dark brown bulbils that grow at the base of the air yam's leaves, and they need soaking and then boiling or steaming before they can be eaten at all. That is not a texture choice. Balugu, the climbing yam, is the same family and the same rule applies with more force — its tubers can reach eighty kilos and require thorough boiling or sun-drying to remove their toxic constituents. In Mbale, elders give kobe to boys who have just been circumcised, to speed the healing.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large bowl, for soaking, Deep pan with a tight lid, Banana leaf spines, for the steaming rig

  • 5 kg air yams (kobe), or climbing yam (balugu) cut into pieces
  • 2 banana leaves
  • Water, for soaking and steaming

To serve

  • Salt, or groundnut paste

Method

  1. Soak the bulbils in water. This is part of the detoxification and is not optional.

Steaming

  1. Wrap the soaked yams in the banana leaves.
  2. Set them on a bed of banana leaf spines in a deep pan, with water below the spines so it never touches the parcel — the rig described in Ebwanga.
  3. Cover the pan tightly.
  4. Steam for up to 2 hours, until completely tender.
  5. Serve with salt, or in a groundnut sauce.

Chef Tips

  • Soak, then boil or steam. Air yam bulbils must be detoxified this way before eating, and undercooked yam of this family is genuinely unpleasant and not safe. Two hours is the cook time and it is not padding.
  • Climbing yam — balugu — needs thorough boiling or sun-drying for the same reason, and its tubers grow far bigger, which means longer.
  • The banana leaf and spine rig is the same one used for matooke, luwombo and ebwanga. Water below the spines, food above, lid on.
  • Uncooked bulbils keep more than six months, which is why they matter as a food. Cooked, they last about two weeks.
  • Balugu varieties worth knowing: kyetutumula is the most common, then luyiki, nandigoya, mukulu jjuuni, kisebe and kikongo. Ndaggu is a separate species cultivated around Bukunja in Buikwe district.
  • Five kilos is a family or ceremonial quantity. The ratio of leaves to yam scales roughly.
  • In Mbale these are given to boys after imbalu circumcision to accelerate healing — one of the clearest cases of a Ugandan food with a specific medicinal role.
  • Uganda has numerous species and ecotypes of climbing yam under more than ten local names, so what you are sold under one name may be a different plant.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Skipping the soak.
  • Cutting the steaming short.
  • Letting the water touch the parcels.
  • Treating balugu like a potato — the size alone means it needs far longer.
  • Buying cooked bulbils and keeping them as long as raw ones.

Health Benefits

  • High in complex carbohydrate, fibre and potassium.
  • Contains diosgenin and related compounds, which is also why they must be cooked properly.
  • Steamed with nothing added.
  • Exceptional natural storage life, which is why they matter in a food system without refrigeration.

Cultural Note

Yams like these are food-security crops — they keep half a year uncooked in a climate with no cold chain, and they grow with almost no attention. That one of them doubles as a healing food after circumcision is the kind of dual role that gets lost when a cuisine is catalogued as a list of dishes.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Cooked, up to 2 weeks. Uncooked bulbils keep over 6 months.

Reheating: Steam again for a few minutes.

Serve with: Binyebwa, Ttimpa in Groundnut Soup, Salt alone
