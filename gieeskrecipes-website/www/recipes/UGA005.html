🥜 Binyebwa

Binyebwa — Ebinyebwa — Binyewa

Ground raw peanuts soaked to soften, stirred into a base of onion and tomato and simmered until a slick of oil floats to the surface — the national sauce, and the one thing everything else is served with.

Groundnut sauce is what Ugandan food is built on: it goes over matooke, into luwombo, through malakwang and maleewa, and onto every starch there is. Its dull pink comes from the skins, which most cooks leave on. There is no agreed set of quantities because it is a dish people cook by feel — one Ugandan food writer says outright that dishes like this are hard to quantify, and gives only a ratio buried in prose. The numbers here come from a source that did commit to them, and the doneness cue is not a time: it is oil rising to the top.

Ingredients

Equipment: Grinder or mortar and pestle, Large bowl, Saucepan with a lid, Wooden spoon

  • 500 g raw peanuts, finely ground
  • Cool water, to soak
  • 3 medium onions, chopped
  • 1 tin (400 g) tomatoes
  • 15 ml (1 tbsp) salt
  • 2.5 ml (1/2 tsp) ground pepper
  • 5 ml (1 tsp) curry powder
  • 30 ml (2 tbsp) cooking oil
  • Hot water, as needed

Method

  1. Finely grind the raw peanuts, 2 to 3 minutes.
  2. Pour into a large bowl, add cool water, mix well, and cover so the lumpy pieces soften.
  3. Chop the onions.
  4. Heat the oil in a saucepan and add the onions. Fry 2 to 3 minutes until lightly golden.
  5. Add the curry powder, mix, then add the tomatoes immediately so the spice does not burn.
  6. Mix well and cover the pan.
  7. Cook the tomatoes at least 10 minutes, checking often, until thickened.
  8. Add the soaked groundnut mixture and mix well.
  9. Cover until it begins to bubble.
  10. Check every 5 to 6 minutes so it does not stick or burn, adding hot water if it gets too thick.
  11. After about 30 minutes, oil floating on the surface tells you it is ready.
  12. Serve with matooke, sweet potatoes, chapati, cassava or rice.

Chef Tips

  • Oil on the surface is the doneness cue. Not a colour, not a clock — when a slick rises, it is done.
  • Soak the ground nuts in cool water before they go anywhere near heat. That is what dissolves the lumps, and skipping it gives you a gritty sauce.
  • Leave the skins on. The pink colour is the skins and most cooks prefer it that way.
  • Watch it constantly. Groundnut sauce catches and burns faster than anything else in the kitchen, and a burnt batch cannot be saved.
  • It is a sensitive sauce that spoils easily, so keep the added vegetables and seasonings light and do not let it boil over.
  • Roasted nuts instead of raw give a sweeter, toastier sauce. Both are done; it is a real choice.
  • A big batch can take four hours. Thirty to forty minutes is right for this quantity, and longer is not wrong.
  • Another Ugandan cook gives the only other published ratio: about 250 g of groundnuts to 4 or 5 cups of boiled water, and nothing else but salt. That is the plainest version of this sauce.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Adding dry ground nuts straight to the pan.
  • Walking away from it.
  • Adding the curry powder and then pausing before the tomatoes, which scorches it.
  • Loading it with vegetables and spices — it is a sensitive sauce and does not want them.
  • Calling it done at a time rather than waiting for the oil.

Health Benefits

  • Groundnuts are high in plant protein, monounsaturated fat, niacin, folate and vitamin E.
  • The fat in the sauce helps the body absorb the carotenoids in whatever greens go alongside.
  • No dairy and no gluten.
  • Contains peanuts — not suitable for anyone with a nut allergy.

Cultural Note

The groundnut runs through the entire Ugandan repertoire and changes name as it crosses the country — binyebwa in Luganda, emaido in Ateso, odii in Acholi when it is blended with sesame. It is the thing that makes a plate of starch into a meal, and the sauce a household is judged on.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Refrigerate up to 2 days. It spoils faster than most sauces — do not push it.

Reheating: Warm gently with a splash of hot water, stirring constantly.

Serve with: Matooke, Sweet potatoes, Posho
