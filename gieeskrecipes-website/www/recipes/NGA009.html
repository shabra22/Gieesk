🥬 Efo Riro with Shoko Leaf

Efo riro

The fully weighed efo riro — a whole chicken simmered forty-five minutes for its stock, shoko leaf blanched six minutes and squeezed dry, and five plum tomatoes in the base.

This is the most precisely measured Yoruba recipe in the collection, and it is also the one that breaks the rule. Where the other efo riro refuses tomatoes outright, this one puts five plum tomatoes in the blend alongside eight red bell peppers. It is built on a proper chicken stock, strained and cooled, and the greens are treated like greens in a French kitchen — blanched separately for six minutes, drained, squeezed out and seasoned before they ever meet the stew. Shoko leaf is named as the traditional green, with spinach as the substitute.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large pot, Blender, Fine mesh sieve, Colander

    For the chicken

    • One 3 1/4 lb whole chicken, cut in 10 pieces
    • 2 medium yellow onions, coarsely chopped
    • 3-4 scotch bonnet chiles
    • 1 smoked and dried fish, such as catfish
    • One 1 1/2 in piece fresh ginger, coarsely chopped
    • 3 medium garlic cloves, coarsely chopped
    • 3 chicken bouillon cubes
    • 2 1/2 tsp kosher salt
    • 3/4 tsp curry powder
    • 3/4 tsp freshly ground white pepper
    • 3/4 tsp dried thyme

    For the stew

    • 8 large red bell peppers, stemmed, seeded and coarsely chopped
    • 5 medium plum tomatoes, coarsely chopped
    • 2 medium yellow onions, coarsely chopped
    • 5 scotch bonnet chiles, stemmed, seeded and coarsely chopped
    • 1/2 cup water, for blending
    • 2 1/4 lb shoko leaf (or substitute fresh spinach), cleaned
    • 3 1/2 oz ponmo (dried cow skin)
    • 3 1/2 oz stockfish
    • 1/4 cup plus 2 tbsp ground crayfish
    • 3 tbsp iru (fermented locust beans)
    • 2 tbsp palm oil
    • 2 tbsp vegetable oil
    • 2 chicken bouillon cubes
    • 1 oz smoked and dried fish, such as catfish
    • Kosher salt

    Method

    1. Put the chicken, onion, chiles, dried fish, ginger, garlic, bouillon, salt, curry powder, white pepper and thyme in a large pot with water to cover.
    2. Boil over medium-high, reduce to a simmer and cook until the chicken is tender and cooked through, about 45 minutes.
    3. Transfer the chicken out. Strain the liquid through a fine mesh sieve and discard the solids. Cool both at room temperature.
    4. Blend the bell peppers, tomatoes, onions and chiles with 1/2 cup water on high until smooth, about 1 minute.
    5. Bring a large pot of water halfway full to a boil. Working in batches, blanch the shoko leaves until wilted and tender, about 6 minutes.
    6. Drain in a colander and discard the cooking water. Once cool, squeeze out the excess water and season lightly with salt.
    7. Set a pot to medium, add the puree and cook until slightly darkened and thickened, about 5 minutes.
    8. Stir in the ponmo, stockfish, crayfish, iru, palm oil, vegetable oil, bouillon, dried fish and the reserved chicken.
    9. Bring to a simmer and reduce to low.
    10. Stir in the greens and cook, stirring frequently, until the flavours meld and everything is heated through, about 10 minutes.
    11. Ladle into bowls and serve with pounded yam, white rice or whole grains.

    Chef Tips

    • The tomatoes are the controversy. Five plum tomatoes against eight red bell peppers — and another named Nigerian authority calls tomatoes in efo riro an absolute no. Cook both and decide.
    • Blanch and squeeze. Six minutes, drained, cooled, wrung out, lightly salted. It is the opposite of the add-them-raw-at-the-end approach, and it gives a cleaner-tasting, less watery soup.
    • Discard the blanching water. The greens' bitterness and grit go with it.
    • Strain the stock through fine mesh and throw the solids away. The chicken comes back; nothing else does.
    • Two oils, deliberately — two tablespoons of palm for flavour and colour, two of vegetable to carry it. That is a restaurant move, not a household one.
    • Only five minutes on the pepper puree here, against fifteen to twenty in Efo Riro. The difference is that this base has already been diluted by blending with water and will cook on in the stew.
    • Shoko leaf is named as the traditional green. If you are using spinach you are substituting, and the author says so.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Skipping the blanch and adding the shoko raw.
    • Not squeezing the blanched greens, which waters the stew.
    • Leaving the stock unstrained.
    • Blending the pepper base without the half cup of water, which stalls the blender.
    • Using all palm oil, which overwhelms a base this tomato-forward.

    Health Benefits

    • Blanching and squeezing reduces the oxalate load of amaranth-family greens.
    • Very high in protein from chicken, stockfish, ponmo and crayfish.
    • Red bell pepper is an exceptional source of vitamin C.
    • Low in carbohydrate.

    Cultural Note

    Published in an American magazine by a Nigerian restaurateur and cookbook author, this recipe shows what happens when a buka dish is written down for a reader who has never seen it made: everything gets weighed, the stock gets strained, and the greens get blanched. Whether the tomatoes are a concession or simply how her family makes it, no source settles — and that is exactly the kind of question the written record cannot answer.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: 4 days refrigerated, 3 months frozen.

    Reheating: Low heat on the stove, stirring, until heated through.

    Serve with: Iyan, Amala, White rice
