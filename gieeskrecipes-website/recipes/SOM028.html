Malaay la Shiilay — Mpaampa

Shark steaks soaked, rubbed with garlic, dusted with flour and fried, then buried under deeply caramelised onions — a Bravanese dish, known as mpaampa in the town's own language.

Coastal Somalis have eaten shark for centuries, caught from small boats close to shore and sold at the seashore market the same day. It is not a fish that tolerates waiting: it spoils faster than anything else on the slab and the first job in any recipe is to deal with its smell. Soak it, fry it hard, and cover it in onions cooked down until they are almost jam, and it becomes one of the best things on the Brava table.

Ingredients

Equipment: Wok or deep frying pan, Bowl for soaking, Slotted spoon, Paper towels

  • 4 shark steaks, about 2.5 cm thick, skin removed
  • Brine or milk, enough to cover the fish for soaking
  • 2 cloves garlic, minced
  • 1 tbsp oil
  • 1 tsp salt
  • 1/4 tsp ground black pepper
  • 2 tbsp plain flour
  • 1 large onion, thinly sliced
  • Oil, for frying

Method

  1. Soak the shark steaks in brine or milk for 30 minutes. This is not optional — it is how the strong ammonia smell of shark is drawn out.
  2. Meanwhile, fry the sliced onion slowly in oil until deeply caramelised and sweet. Drain it on paper towels and set aside.
  3. Drain and pat the fish dry. Cut each steak lengthwise and remove the central bone.
  4. Mix the 1 tbsp oil with the minced garlic, salt and pepper, and rub the paste over the fish.
  5. Dust the pieces lightly with flour — a thin coating only, not a batter.
  6. Fry in a wok or deep pan over medium heat for about 6 minutes, turning once, until golden and cooked through.
  7. Drain on paper towels.
  8. Arrange on a platter, heap the caramelised onions over the top and serve warm with maanda.

Chef Tips

  • Smaller sharks are always better — the meat is more tender, and the older the animal the stronger the flavour.
  • Buy shark the day you cook it. It spoils markedly faster than other fish and yesterday's shark is not worth the trouble.
  • The soak is the whole technique. Brine or milk both work; milk is gentler and leaves the flesh slightly sweeter.
  • Take the onions further than feels comfortable. Pale fried onions add nothing here; the dish needs them dark and sweet.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Skipping the 30-minute soak, which leaves the finished dish smelling of ammonia no matter how well it is seasoned.
  • Coating the fish in a thick batter, which steams the flesh instead of frying it.
  • Frying in oil that is not hot enough, so the flour turns pasty and grey.
  • Rushing the onions — they need slow heat and patience, and they are half the dish.

Health Benefits

  • A very lean, high-protein fish.
  • Provides selenium and vitamin B12.
  • Onions cooked long and slow contribute prebiotic fibre.

Cultural Note

In Brava the fishermen went out twice a day, morning and late afternoon, which is how a small seashore market kept a town supplied with fish fresh enough to eat the same day. The artisanal fishery that this dish belongs to is a subsistence tradition rather than a commercial one, worked from small boats within sight of the beach.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Refrigerate up to 2 days, fish and onions separately.

Reheating: Warm the fish in a dry pan and the onions alongside. Do not microwave.

Serve with: Muufo Baraawe, Bariis Iskukaris, Basbaas
