🐪 Muqmad Geel

Muqmad Geel — Oodkac Geel

Camel meat cut into strips and sun-dried for days, chopped into tiny cubes, fried hard and then packed under browned butter and oil with cardamom — the original form of the preserve, before beef replaced it.

Muqmad is camel first and beef second. The beef version most people now make is a diaspora substitution, and the recipes that carry it say so: oodkac is traditionally prepared from camel meat, and traditionally with pure ghee rather than ghee cut with oil. The camel original adds a stage the modern version drops entirely — the meat is cut into strips and sun-dried for days before it is cubed and fried, which is what gave it a shelf life measured in years rather than months. In Somaliland this is what fills the xeedho, the decorated wedding vessel the groom's family must untie.

Ingredients

Equipment: Sharp knife, Drying racks, and perforated cloth or fine mesh, Heavy frying pan or wok, Slotted spoon, Sterilised jars, or a wooden container

    The meat

    • 1 kg camel meat — brisket, rib or loin

    For frying

    • 480 ml (2 cups) canola oil
    • 5 ml (1 tsp) salt

    For packing

    • 240 ml (1 cup) butter, or subag
    • 240 ml (1 cup) canola oil
    • 1.25 ml (1/4 tsp) ground cardamom

    Method

      Drying — the stage the beef version leaves out

      1. Cut the camel meat into thin strips along the grain.
      2. Lay the strips on racks in the shade with good airflow, covered with perforated cloth or fine mesh to keep insects off. Do not dry them in direct sun.
      3. Leave them to dry for 7 to 15 days depending on the weather, until they are hard and leathery right through with no soft centre.

      Cubing and frying

      1. Chop the dried strips into very small cubes.
      2. Cook the cubes in a dry pan over medium heat. After about 5 minutes, pour off and discard any moisture the meat releases — this step is what makes it keep.
      3. Add the 2 cups of frying oil and the salt, and fry continuously for about 11 minutes, until the cubes are thoroughly dry and crisp at the edges.
      4. Drain off the oil. It can be kept and reused for cooking.

      Packing

      1. Melt the butter in a clean pan and let it brown. Once the foam subsides, add the second cup of oil.
      2. Stir in the cardamom.
      3. Combine the fried meat with the butter-and-oil mixture so the cubes are fully submerged.
      4. Pack into sterilised jars, keeping the meat covered by the fat, and store.

      Chef Tips

      • The quantities here are the published ones for the beef version and the sun-drying stage is restored from the documented camel practice, where dried strips are packed in clarified butter fat. No source gives both together, and you should know that seam is there.
      • Fry it properly dry or it will not keep. This is the whole safety mechanism: a hard fry plus full submersion in fat. A softer, less-fried batch has a markedly shorter life and is the commonest way this goes wrong.
      • Pour off the moisture the meat releases in the first five minutes. Leaving it in is the other commonest way this goes wrong.
      • Half butter and half oil is deliberate — it stops the fat setting rock solid in the jar. Pure ghee is the traditional choice and it does set hard.
      • Dry in the shade with airflow, never in direct sun, and always covered against insects. The preservation works because low water activity plus mild acidity stops microbes; heat and flies are the enemies of both.
      • Cardamom is the only spice in it. Muqmad is not xawaash-spiced, which surprises people.
      • The meat must stay under the fat. Any cube poking out above the surface is the one that spoils.
      • Camel is the leaner, tougher meat and it dries well for exactly that reason. The hump is the prized part of the animal but it is fat, not lean, so it is not what goes into this.

      Common Mistakes to Avoid

      • Skipping the drying stage and expecting camel to keep like the fried beef version.
      • Under-frying, which leaves moisture in and shortens the life to weeks.
      • Leaving the released moisture in the pan.
      • Drying it in full sun or uncovered.
      • Packing it so some cubes sit above the fat.
      • Using a lean cut with no fat at all and wondering why it tastes of nothing.

      Health Benefits

      • Camel meat is leaner than beef and high in protein, iron and B vitamins.
      • An enormously calorie-dense, shelf-stable protein — which is exactly what it was designed to be.
      • Very high in fat from the packing medium. A spoonful is a serving.

      Cultural Note

      Muqmad is the food of movement: dried, fried, sealed in fat and carried. The name shifts with the region — muqmad in Djibouti, Ethiopia and Somaliland, oodkac in central and southern Somalia and in Kenya. Its ceremonial home is the xeedho, a wood-and-leather vessel decorated with shells and beads by the bride's family and given to the groom's; a week after the wedding his representative has to untie the knots, and the muqmad is what is inside.

      Storage & Serving

      Storage: Fully submerged in fat in a sterilised jar: several months, and traditional wooden-container versions are credited with over a year. Keep it cool and dark.

      Reheating: Eat it as it is, or warm a spoonful in its own fat.

      Serve with: Canjeero, Malawax, Shaah Bigays
