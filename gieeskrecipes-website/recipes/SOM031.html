🍰 Toorte Basbuusa

Toorte Basbuusa — Doolshe Basbuusa

A semolina and coconut cake split in two, both halves soaked in rose-water syrup with pastry cream between them, chilled and finished with pistachio and coconut — the layered take on basbuusa served at Somali celebrations.

Basbuusa itself is an Arab sweet, semolina soaked in syrup, that reached Somalia along the same trade routes as the spices and settled in as a wedding and Eid cake. This is the layered version: rather than baking cream between two dense slabs, the cake is baked light, cut, soaked and filled cold. It is not traditional basbuusa and does not pretend to be — Somali cooks who make it this way call it a toorte, a torte, for exactly that reason.

Ingredients

Equipment: 20 cm (8 inch) springform tin, buttered, Electric mixer or whisk, Serrated knife and a long slicing knife, Small saucepan

    For the cake

    • 93 g (1/2 cup) fine semolina
    • 70 g (1/2 cup) plain flour
    • 45 g (1/2 cup) shredded desiccated coconut
    • 1 tsp baking powder
    • 1/4 tsp bicarbonate of soda
    • 1/4 tsp salt
    • 100 g (1/2 cup) granulated sugar
    • 2 large eggs
    • 120 ml (1/2 cup) melted unsalted butter
    • 80 ml (1/3 cup) milk, lukewarm
    • 1 tsp vanilla extract

    For the syrup

    • 200 g (1 cup) granulated sugar
    • 240 ml (1 cup) water
    • 1 tsp lemon juice
    • 1 tsp rose water

    To fill and finish

    • 1 batch white pastry cream, sweetened with only 1 tbsp sugar
    • Ground pistachios, to decorate
    • Shredded desiccated coconut, to decorate

    Method

    1. Whisk the semolina, flour, coconut, baking powder, bicarbonate of soda and salt together in a bowl.
    2. Beat the eggs and sugar for a full 5 minutes, until pale and thick — this is where the lift comes from.
    3. Fold the dry ingredients in gradually, in three stages.
    4. Mix the melted butter, milk and vanilla together, add to the batter and fold gently until just combined.
    5. Pour into the buttered springform tin and bake at 193C / 380F for 20 minutes. Let it cool completely in the tin.
    6. For the syrup, bring the sugar and water to a simmer, add the lemon juice, simmer until slightly thickened, then stir in the rose water and take it off the heat. Let it cool.
    7. Trace a line round the middle of the cooled cake with a serrated knife, then cut along it with a long knife. Return the bottom half to the tin and soak it with 80 ml (1/3 cup) of the syrup.
    8. Spread the pastry cream over the bottom layer and level it.
    9. Set the top layer back on and soak it with 120 ml (1/2 cup) of syrup.
    10. Refrigerate for 2 hours, then decorate with ground pistachios and shredded coconut.

    Chef Tips

    • Use fine semolina, and check it really is semolina — the commonest failure here is corn grits bought by mistake, which gives a gritty, crunchy cake. Semolina is milled from wheat.
    • If your semolina is coarse, put it through a coffee or spice grinder. The longer you grind, the finer it gets.
    • To move it closer to real basbuusa, increase the semolina and reduce the flour by the same weight — the cake gets denser with every gram you shift.
    • Sweeten the pastry cream with only a tablespoon of sugar. There is a cup of sugar in the syrup and the cake will be cloying otherwise.
    • Rose water or orange blossom water is what makes it taste authentically of basbuusa. Vanilla or cardamom will work but taste like a different cake.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Cutting the cake before it is completely cold, which tears it and leaves the layers uneven.
    • Soaking both halves with equal syrup — the top layer takes more because gravity carries it down into the cream.
    • Beating the eggs and sugar for a minute instead of five, which gives a flat, heavy cake.
    • Letting the pastry cream set hard in the fridge before spreading it. Spread it while still warm from blending, straight onto the cake.

    Health Benefits

    • Semolina is a durum wheat product with more protein than plain flour.
    • Pistachios add a little healthy fat and potassium.

    Cultural Note

    Basbuusa arrived in Somalia with traders and is generally traced to Egypt by way of the Ottoman period. It has been thoroughly naturalised: it turns up at Somali weddings and at Eid, and the layered toorte version is what many diaspora households now make instead of the dense original.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Refrigerate, covered, up to 3 days. The syrup keeps it moist rather than drying out.

    Reheating: Serve cold from the fridge; warming it collapses the cream layer.

    Serve with: Shaah Cadays, Somali coffee, Extra syrup, for anyone wanting it sweeter
