🍌 Matooke

Matooke — Amatooke

Green cooking bananas peeled with oiled hands, wrapped in their own leaves and steamed on a bed of leaf spines above the water until they turn deep yellow, then mashed inside the parcel.

Matooke is not a banana dish so much as the centre of Ugandan eating, and the cultivar is nothing like a dessert banana — there are more than fifty of them, grown to be cooked. The technique is the whole thing: the peeled fingers go into a parcel of banana leaves, the parcel sits on a raft of hard leaf spines above water it must never touch, and the pan is covered so the bananas steam rather than boil. Done properly they turn from white to a deep yellow, and they are mashed in the parcel, still wrapped, before being turned out.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large heavy pan with a tight lid, Sharp knife, Banana leaves and their spines, Bowl of water, for the peeled fingers

  • 15 to 20 green cooking bananas (matooke)
  • 1/2 tsp cooking oil, for your hands and the knife

The steaming rig

  • Young, tender banana leaves, hard spines removed
  • The hard spines themselves, cut an inch shorter than the pan is wide
  • Water, to sit below the spines

To serve

  • Salt, at the table
  • Ghee

Method

  1. Rub the 1/2 tsp of oil over your hands and the blade of the knife. This stops the sap staining and sticking.
  2. Peel the bananas and drop each one into a bowl of water as you go, so they do not oxidise and darken.

Build the rig

  1. Cut the hard spines from the banana leaves and cut them an inch shorter than the diameter of the pan.
  2. Lay the spines across the base of the pan so they fill it about halfway up.
  3. Pour in water. It must stay below the spines so it never touches the parcel.

Wrap and steam

  1. Line the spines with the tender leaves, spines removed, overlapping them generously.
  2. Pile the drained bananas into the middle and fold the leaves over into a closed parcel.
  3. Cover the pan tightly and steam over medium heat for about an hour and a half, checking the water now and then and topping it up with boiling water if it runs low.
  4. The bananas are ready when they have turned a deep yellow all through and give completely under a spoon.

Mash

  1. Without unwrapping, mash the bananas inside the parcel until smooth.
  2. Turn the parcel out, open it at the table, and serve with ghee, groundnut sauce or a stew.

Chef Tips

  • Oil your hands and the knife before you peel. This is the first thing any Ugandan cook will tell you and it saves you black-stained fingers for two days.
  • The water must never touch the parcel. If it does you are boiling, not steaming, and the matooke goes watery and pale instead of yellow.
  • Yellow is the doneness cue, not a clock. White or streaky means it needs longer.
  • Mash it while it is still wrapped and still very hot. Out of the parcel it cools fast and turns lumpy.
  • No source I could find publishes a quantified recipe for steamed matooke, and the reason is honest rather than accidental — the most reliable Ugandan food writer there is says she is still trying to perfect it, and lists it among dishes that are hard to quantify. The rig described here is from her ebwanga method, where she says outright that it is how Ugandan matooke is made. Treat the timing as a guide and the colour as the truth.
  • Buy firm bananas that are nowhere near ripening. A finger that has started to turn will go sweet and ruin the pot.
  • Keep the spines. They are the raft, and a pan without them will scorch the leaves.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Peeling with dry hands.
  • Letting the water reach the parcel.
  • Lifting the lid repeatedly, which lets out the steam doing the work.
  • Pulling it off while it is still pale.
  • Mashing it after it has been unwrapped and cooled.
  • Using ripe or ripening bananas.

Health Benefits

  • Cooking bananas are high in complex carbohydrate, potassium and vitamin B6.
  • Steamed in leaves with nothing added, so no fat at all until the ghee goes on.
  • Naturally gluten-free and a good source of resistant starch.
  • Lower on the glycaemic index than most refined staples.

Cultural Note

In Buganda matooke is not a side dish, it is the meal, and the word for food and the word for matooke shade into one another. The plant is used entire — the fruit steamed, the leaves as the wrapper and the plate, the spines as the rack, the fibres as the string. Uganda grows more cooking banana than anywhere else on earth, and the Ugandan treatment is distinctive within the region: steamed in its own leaves until yellow and mashed in the parcel, where neighbours more often boil and serve the pieces whole.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days, wrapped. It firms up considerably when cold.

Reheating: Steam again briefly, or warm in a pan with a splash of water.

Serve with: Binyebwa, Luwombo, Ghee and salt
