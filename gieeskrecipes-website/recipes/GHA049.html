🍌 King of Kelewele

Kelewele

Hwentia, anise seed, cloves and a great deal of ginger blended with water, the fibres squeezed out, and only the liquid used to coat the plantain.

Kelewele is spiced fried plantain and it is the best street snack in Ghana. This version is the most heavily spiced documented anywhere: two to four ounces of fresh ginger, a teaspoon of anise seed, a teaspoon of whole cloves, three or four sticks of hwentia and a couple of tablespoons of onion, blended with two or three tablespoons of water. The trick she arrived at on her third attempt is the useful part — squeeze out the coarse fibres and use only the liquid to coat the plantain. Four slightly over-ripe plantains, cubed, rested a few minutes, fried at about 350 degrees Fahrenheit.

Ingredients

Equipment: Blender, Sieve or cloth, for squeezing out the fibres, Deep pot or fryer, Thermometer, Slotted spoon

  • 4 slightly over-ripe plantains
  • 2-4 oz fresh ginger
  • 3 or 4 sticks hwentia (grains of selim)
  • 1 tsp anise seed
  • 1 tsp whole cloves
  • A couple of tbsp onion
  • Fresh habanero or red pepper, to taste — start with about 1/2 tsp
  • About 1/4 tsp salt, or more to taste
  • About 1/4 tsp nutmeg (optional)
  • 2-3 tbsp water, for blending
  • Oil, for deep frying

Method

  1. Blend the onion, ginger, pepper and hwentia with 2 or 3 tablespoons of water.
  2. Squeeze out the fibrous strings and keep mostly the liquid.
  3. Stir in the anise seed, cloves, salt and the optional nutmeg.
  4. Cube the plantains.
  5. Coat them with a few tablespoons of the seasoning liquid.
  6. Rest several minutes.
  7. Heat the oil to about 350 degrees Fahrenheit.
  8. Fry in batches until nicely browned on all sides.
  9. Remove and drain on paper towels. Best eaten immediately or soon after cooking.

Chef Tips

  • Squeeze out the fibres and use the liquid. Her own account is that she tried it three times and this worked best — the coarse ginger and hwentia strings do not want to be in the finished dish.
  • Her ripeness note is the key to kelewele: part of the secret is having the plantains ripe enough, so the sweetness comes through and they caramelise on the outside.
  • Slightly over-ripe, not merely ripe. Too firm and it will not caramelise; too soft and it absorbs oil.
  • Hwentia is grains of selim, the smoky West African pepper pod — three or four sticks is a lot and it is what makes this version taste of itself.
  • 350 degrees Fahrenheit, which is one of only two documented frying temperatures for this dish with a stated unit. The other named sources give 180 degrees Celsius, which is the same thing.
  • Fry in batches and do not crowd the pan. Crowded plantain steams rather than fries.
  • Traditionally served with bean stew, though she prefers dry-roasted peanuts. Groundnuts are the usual street pairing.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Leaving the fibres in the marinade.
  • Under-ripe plantain, which will not caramelise.
  • Crowding the pan.
  • Skipping the hwentia, which is the whole character.
  • Letting it stand long after frying — it goes soft fast.

Health Benefits

  • Ripe plantain is high in potassium, vitamin A and vitamin B6.
  • Ginger, cloves and selim all carry documented antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory compounds.
  • Vegan and gluten-free.
  • Served with groundnuts it becomes a reasonably complete snack.

Cultural Note

Kelewele is sold in paper cones from braziers after dark in Accra, with a twist of roasted groundnuts on top, and it is the thing people miss most when they leave Ghana. That the 1968 government record describes it seasoned with ginger alone, while modern recipes pile in cloves, anise, nutmeg and selim, is a nice record of a street food getting more elaborate over sixty years.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Eat at once. Kelewele does not keep.

Reheating: A hot oven briefly, at a push. Never the microwave.

Serve with: Dry-roasted peanuts, Red Red, Groundnuts
