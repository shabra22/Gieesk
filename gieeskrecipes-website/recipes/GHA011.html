🍲 Goat Light Soup

Nkrakra — light soup

The tomatoes and onions are cooked, pulled out, blended and returned — which is what makes light soup light rather than a stew.

Light soup is the broth that fufu is eaten with, and the technique that defines it is the removal and return: the tomatoes, onions and garden egg are cooked in the soup, lifted out, blended smooth, and stirred back in. Nothing is fried, so the soup stays thin and clean-tasting and red rather than orange. Five hundred grams of goat is marinated ten minutes in a blended paste of ginger, garlic, chilli, onion and tomato, cooked gently, given tomato puree and then half a litre of water, and simmered until the meat is tender.

Ingredients

Equipment: Heavy pot, Blender, Slotted spoon, Knife

  • 500 g diced goat, or half a chicken, diced
  • 6 whole tomatoes
  • 1/2 kpakpo shito, or other hot pepper
  • 10 g ginger
  • 10 g garlic
  • 2 onions
  • 50 g tomato puree
  • 1 aubergine — garden egg in Ghana
  • 500 ml water
  • Salt

To serve

  • Fufu, banku or rice

Method

  1. Blend the ginger, garlic, chilli, one onion and three of the tomatoes to a paste.
  2. Marinate the meat in the paste for 10 minutes.
  3. Cook the meat gently for 10 minutes.
  4. Add the tomato puree and cook a further 10 minutes.
  5. Add 500 ml of water and simmer until the meat is tender.
  6. Add the remaining three tomatoes, diced, the diced aubergine and the last onion, and cook 30 minutes.
  7. Remove the onions and tomatoes, blend them, and return them to the soup.
  8. Bring back to a simmer, season, and serve with fufu.

Chef Tips

  • Nothing is fried. That is the whole distinction between light soup and a Ghanaian stew, and it is why the soup reads as clean and thin.
  • Cook the vegetables, remove them, blend them, return them. The removal and return is the technique — blending them raw into the pot at the start gives you a different, duller soup.
  • Goat is the first choice and chicken the alternative, in that order, which matches how the dish is eaten in Ghana.
  • Kpakpo shito is the small round Ghanaian chilli and it is aromatic rather than merely hot. Half of one is the printed amount; a scotch bonnet is hotter and less fragrant.
  • Garden egg is the Ghanaian aubergine — small, pale and slightly bitter. It is cooked into the soup and blended back in, so it thickens very slightly while adding that bitter edge.
  • Until the meat is tender is the only cue for the long simmer, and goat can take well over an hour. Do not rush it to the thirty-minute vegetable stage.
  • Published figures for this soup vary enormously — one lab-standardised version uses 317 g of tomato and 15 g of salt per litre, another gives most amounts as 'a handful' or 'a small tin'. Use this one as the quantified household version.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Frying the aromatics first, which makes it a stew.
  • Blending the vegetables in rather than cooking, removing and returning them.
  • Moving to the vegetable stage before the goat is tender.
  • Substituting scotch bonnet for kpakpo shito without expecting a different flavour.
  • Leaving out the garden egg, which carries the bitterness the soup is built on.

Health Benefits

  • No oil is added at any stage, which makes this one of the leanest West African soups.
  • Goat is lower in fat and cholesterol than beef or lamb.
  • High in lycopene from the long-cooked tomato.
  • Ginger and garlic both have documented anti-inflammatory activity.

Cultural Note

Light soup is what a Ghanaian household makes when someone is unwell, and also what gets cooked on a Sunday with goat in it — a broth that stretches from convalescent food to celebration depending on what goes in the pot. Its name is literal: light, as against the heavy groundnut and palm nut soups that are the other things fufu is eaten with.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 4 days refrigerated, 3 months frozen.

Reheating: Simmer gently. It takes reheating better than most soups.

Serve with: Fufu, Omo Tuo, Banku
