🥛 Nono

Nono — nunu, fermented milk

Raw milk soured at room temperature with a little of the previous batch — and the endpoint is measurable: pH about 4.3 and lactic acid around 2.5 per cent.

Nono is the Fulani fermented milk that fura is served in, and it is one of the few Nigerian ferments with a published, measurable endpoint. Traditional practice is straightforward: raw milk is collected in a calabash, a little of the previous batch is kept back as a starter, and it is left to ferment at room temperature for about twenty-four hours. What makes it publishable is the laboratory work — the pH falls from about 6.78 to 4.30 and the lactic acid rises from about 0.32 per cent to 2.50 per cent at 27±2°C, which gives anyone with pH strips an unambiguous test.

Ingredients

Equipment: Calabash bowl, or a clean glass or ceramic vessel, Cloth cover, pH test strips, Sieve

  • Fresh raw cow's milk
  • A little of the previous batch of nono, as a starter

Equipment that is part of the tradition

  • A calabash bowl

Method

  1. Collect the fresh milk, traditionally early in the morning, in a clean calabash.
  2. Stir in a little of the leftover nono from the previous batch as a starter.
  3. Cover with a cloth and leave at room temperature, around 27°C, about 24 hours.
  4. Check it with a pH strip. It is soured when the pH has fallen to roughly 4.3 — down from about 6.8 at the start.
  5. It should be tangy, thickened and pleasantly sour, with no off smells.
  6. Keep a little back as the starter for your next batch.
  7. Serve cold, with mashed fura balls and sugar to taste.

Chef Tips

  • pH 4.3 is the endpoint, and it is the reason this can be published with any confidence. A strip costs almost nothing and it removes the guesswork.
  • Backslopping — keeping a little of the last batch — is the traditional starter, and it makes each batch more reliable than the one before.
  • Twenty-four hours at room temperature is the traditional figure, given by two academic sources. Note that a 1988 study ran its fermentation for 120 hours, but that was to chart the full acidification curve rather than to make drinkable nono.
  • Room temperature here means around 27°C. Cooler and it will take longer; much warmer and you risk the wrong organisms.
  • A SAFETY NOTE WORTH TAKING SERIOUSLY: nono is traditionally made from raw, unpasteurised milk and distributed in open calabashes, and one published study states plainly that this practice contravenes food safety principles. If you are making it, use milk you trust, keep everything scrupulously clean, and consider pasteurising and cooling the milk before adding your starter.
  • Nono is drunk cold and it is the liquid half of fura da nono.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Judging it by smell alone when a pH strip would tell you.
  • Fermenting somewhere cold, which stalls it.
  • Not keeping a starter back for the next batch.
  • Leaving it at room temperature long past souring.
  • Using an open vessel in a dusty place.

Health Benefits

  • Live lactic acid bacteria, as in any traditionally soured milk.
  • Fermentation reduces lactose, which makes it more digestible than fresh milk.
  • High in protein and calcium.
  • No added sugar unless you add it.

Cultural Note

Nono is Fulani food, carried by pastoralists and sold from calabashes, and the backslopping method means a household's nono carries its own continuous culture. It is the liquid that fura is mashed into, and the pairing — fura da nono — is one of the most recognisable things in northern Nigeria.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 3 days refrigerated. It continues to sour.

Reheating: Not applicable — drunk cold.

Serve with: Fura, Fura da Nono, Sugar to taste

Adapted From

  • THE MEASURABLE ENDPOINT, and the reason this fermentation is published at all: 'Changes in the acidity and lactic acid content of "Nono", a Nigerian cultured milk product', authors O.O. Atanda and M.J. Ikenebomeh, Letters in Applied Microbiology, 1988, Volume 6, Issue 6, pp. 137–138. Verbatim figures: 'The pH decreased from 6·78 ± 0·19 to 4·30 ± 0·11' and 'the lactic acid content increased from 0·32 ± 0·04% to 2·50 ± 0·02% (w/v)', 'during incubation at room temperature (27°± 2°C) for 120 h'. So the publishable cue is: nono is soured when the pH reaches approximately 4.3 and lactic acid approximately 2.5 per cent w/v, at 27±2°C. That is a measurable endpoint from a peer-reviewed source with a stated temperature.
  • THE TIME DISAGREEMENT, reported and NOT reconciled. Egwim, Evans, Amanabo Musa, Yahaya Abubakar and Bello Mainuna, 'Nigerian Indigenous Fermented Foods: Processes and Prospects', IntechOpen: milk is 'allowed to ferment for twenty four hours at room temperature', with 'a little of the leftover as starter'. Okonkwo, Ogbonna Innocent, 'Microbiological Analyses and Safety Evaluation of Nono: A Fermented Milk Product Consumed in Most Parts of Northern Nigeria', International Journal of Dairy Science, 2011, 6(3): 'collected in calabash and allowed to ferment naturally for 24h'. Atanda & Ikenebomeh (1988): incubation for 120 h at 27±2°C. Twenty-four hours against 120 is a fivefold discrepancy. The likeliest reading is that traditional 24 h produces drinkable nono while the 1988 study ran fermentation to completion to chart the full acidification curve — but THAT IS AN INFERENCE AND NO SOURCE STATES IT, so it is not published as sourced. What is published safely: traditional practice is 24 h at room temperature with backslopping from the previous batch, and the measurable acidification endpoint is pH 4.3.
  • A DISAGREEMENT ABOUT WHAT NONO EVEN IS, unreconciled and worth recording. Dobby's Signature states that 'Nunu "Pronounced No-no" is the Hausa word for fresh cow milk & it's usually collected early in the morning in calabash bowls' — and yet the SAME PAGE describes the dish as 'a combination of fermented cow milk (Locally prepared yogurt) with ground millet'. That is an internal contradiction within one source. Okonkwo (2011) defines nono as 'an opaque white to milky coloured liquid food drink got from fermented raw milk.' Egwim et al. describe it as 'sour milk.' The academic consensus is fermented; the blog says fresh. The academic reading is followed here and the conflict is recorded.
  • THE SAFETY NOTE, from the published literature rather than invented: Okonkwo (2011) states that 'the practice of preparation and distribution of nono in open calabash contravenes' food safety principles. Nono is traditionally made from raw unpasteurised milk. The caution in the tips follows from that published finding.
  • No quantities are given for nono in any source consulted — it is milk, a starter and time. The serving figure here is a serving suggestion.
  • Nono is the liquid half of fura da nono. For the millet balls see Fura and for the assembled drink Fura da Nono.
