Qaxwo — Qahwa — Bun Cadays

Coffee brewed with cardamom, ginger and cinnamon and a pinch of salt, simmered rather than boiled hard — the afternoon cup of the casariyo, and the one drink Somali cooks say nobody measures.

Qaxwo is the afternoon coffee, drunk during casariyo, the stretch of the day when the heat breaks and people sit. At a Somali coffee stall the beans are roasted to order in a pan over charcoal until they crackle, pounded by hand with a pestle called an itan, ground in a carved wooden grinder called a mogotar, and brewed slowly in a clay pot whose long neck is shaped to hold heat without letting it boil hard. The spices often go into the roast rather than into the water. Somalis drink it hot in the heat of the day and see no contradiction in that.

Ingredients

Equipment: Saucepan, or a long-necked clay coffee pot, Fine strainer, or a metal mesh filter or French press, Mortar and pestle

    Per 500 ml of water

    • 2 to 3 tbsp ground coffee, medium-coarse
    • 5 to 6 green cardamom pods, lightly crushed
    • 1 slice fresh ginger, about 5 mm thick
    • 1 cinnamon stick
    • A pinch of salt
    • 500 ml water, freshly boiled

    Optional, to serve

    • Dates
    • Milk or camel milk

    Method

    1. Lightly crush the cardamom pods and the cinnamon stick in a mortar so they open up.

    Either brew it

    1. Put the coffee, cardamom, ginger, cinnamon and salt in a mesh filter or French press, pour over the boiling water and steep for 4 to 5 minutes.

    Or simmer it, which is the traditional way

    1. Put the water, cardamom, ginger, cinnamon and salt in the pot and bring it to a bare simmer.
    2. Add the coffee and hold it at a simmer for 5 minutes. Do not let it come to a hard, rolling boil at any point.
    3. Take it off the heat and let the grounds settle for a minute.
    4. Strain as you pour it into cups. Serve with dates, and with milk or camel milk if you want it.

    Chef Tips

    • Never let it boil hard. The traditional clay pot is shaped the way it is precisely to hold heat without boiling, and a hard boil turns the coffee bitter and flattens the cardamom.
    • The quantities here are the only published ones I could find and they come from a single unnamed personal blog, so treat them as a sound starting ratio rather than a canon. Somali coffee sellers put it plainly: no one measures, it is all intuition and feel.
    • If you roast your own beans, put the cardamom, cloves or cinnamon bark in with them and roast the spices together with the coffee. That is the documented stall method and it gives a rounder result than spicing the water.
    • The pinch of salt is unusual and appears in only one source. It takes the edge off the bitterness rather than making it salty. Leave it out if you would rather.
    • Grind coarse. Fine grounds are misery to strain out of a simmered pot.
    • There is also a milk version — no water at all, 2 cups milk with 2 tbsp coffee, 7 crushed cardamom seeds, 2 cinnamon sticks and 2 tbsp sugar, simmered 3 to 4 minutes. It is a genuinely different drink under the same name.
    • Do not confuse this with bun iyo toor, which is whole coffee beans fried in ghee and eaten rather than drunk.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Boiling it hard, which is the one thing every account warns against.
    • Grinding the coffee too fine for a simmered pot.
    • Using ground cardamom instead of crushed pods, which goes muddy and cloudy.
    • Adding sugar to the pot when you meant to make the water-based version — most accounts sweeten in the cup, if at all.
    • Leaving the grounds in the pot between cups, so the second cup is bitter.

    Health Benefits

    • Cardamom and ginger are both traditionally taken for digestion.
    • Almost no calories unless you add milk or sugar.
    • Caffeine content is comparable to any other brewed coffee.

    Cultural Note

    Coffee is Horn of Africa in origin and Somalis are on the trade routes that carried it everywhere else, so qaxwo sits inside a much older story than the cup suggests. In the Somali quarters of Nairobi the beans are still roasted at the stall, pounded in an itan and ground in a mogotar while you wait — and the pot used there is the long-necked clay jebena, a borrowing from Ethiopian and Eritrean practice rather than a Mogadishu tradition.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Drink it fresh. It does not keep.

    Reheating: Not recommended — reheated Somali coffee turns bitter.

    Serve with: Dates, Buskud, Xalwo
