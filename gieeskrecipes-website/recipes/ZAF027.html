🍮 Malvapoeding

Malvapoeding

An apricot-jam sponge with vinegar in the batter, drenched the instant it leaves the oven in a hot sauce of cream, butter, sugar and brandy so it soaks all the way through.

Malva pudding is not as old as most South Africans assume. A similarly named pudding existed around 1924, but it was boiled rather than baked and had no apricot jam in it. The version everyone now knows traces to 1978, when the food writer Michael Olivier asked his friend Maggie Pepler to stand in for the head chef at Boschendal; her malva pudding went down so well with the patrons that it stayed on the menu for thirty-four years. The vinegar in the batter is not a mistake — it reacts with the bicarbonate of soda.

Ingredients

Equipment: Electric mixer or whisk, 3 or 4 shallow 15 cm dishes, or small skillet pans, greased, Small saucepan

    The sponge

    • 220 g caster sugar
    • 2 large free-range eggs
    • 1 tbsp apricot jam
    • 2 tbsp butter, melted
    • 1 tbsp vinegar
    • 120 ml (1/2 cup) milk
    • 175 g cake flour
    • 1/2 tsp bicarbonate of soda
    • A pinch of salt

    The sauce

    • 240 ml (1 cup) cream
    • 115 g butter
    • 60 g sugar
    • 120 ml (1/2 cup) brandy

    To serve

    • Vanilla custard

    Method

    1. Heat the oven to 180C. Beat the caster sugar and eggs until pale and creamy, then beat in the apricot jam.
    2. Melt the butter and combine it with the vinegar and the milk.
    3. Combine the flour, bicarbonate of soda and salt in a separate bowl.
    4. Add the milk mixture to the egg mixture a little at a time, alternating with the dry ingredients, beating well between additions.
    5. Pour into the greased dishes and bake for 20 to 25 minutes, until golden brown and spongy in the middle.
    6. While they bake, heat the cream, butter, sugar and brandy together and simmer for 2 minutes.
    7. Pour the hot sauce over the puddings the moment they come out of the oven.
    8. Let them cool slightly so the sauce sinks right into the sponge, then serve with custard.

    Chef Tips

    • The sauce goes on straight out of the oven. A pudding that has cooled will not absorb it, and you end up with sauce sitting in the dish.
    • The vinegar and the bicarbonate of soda are a pair. Leaving the vinegar out gives a flatter, denser sponge.
    • The brandy is optional. Plenty of households make the sauce with cream, butter and sugar alone, and some use hot water in place of the cream for a thinner, more syrupy finish.
    • Individual dishes make the soaking more even than one large pudding, where the middle stays drier than the edges.
    • It wants more sauce than looks reasonable. That is the dish.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Letting the sponge cool before saucing it.
    • Baking it until dry, when it needs to be just spongy so it has somewhere to put the sauce.
    • Omitting the vinegar.
    • Making the sauce ahead and pouring it over cold, which defeats the whole mechanism.

    Health Benefits

    • Eggs and cream contribute protein and calcium.
    • Portioned into four individual puddings rather than served from a dish, which is its own restraint.
    • There is no getting around it: this is a rich dessert and it is meant to be.

    Cultural Note

    Nobody is certain where the name comes from. The most repeated suggestion is the Afrikaans for marshmallow, malva, and another points at the geranium leaves once used to flavour it. What is documented is the 1978 Boschendal version, which is the reason the dish is on every South African restaurant menu today.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days, sauce and all.

    Reheating: Warm gently in a low oven or briefly in a microwave. It is good cold too.

    Serve with: Vanilla custard, Cream, Vanilla ice cream
