🥘 Thiebou Yaap

Thiebou yaap — thiep, riz à la viande

The meat thieb her author calls badly underrated and much simpler than thiéboudieune — the one you make when you cannot be bothered but still want to eat well.

1h 45mTotal time
25mPrep
1h 20mCook
6Servings
650Calories
MediumDifficulty

A rice-and-meat dish built on a browned-onion base with no tomato colouring and no bouillon. Three onions, fifty millilitres of oil, a bay leaf, five cloves of garlic, half a bell pepper, a tablespoon of vinegar, two dried bird's eye chillies, a carrot, a turnip and pitted green olives. The meat browns first, then an onion and the other half-pepper are caramelised with it, about a litre and a half of water goes in, and it simmers an hour on low heat. The onion sauce is finished separately until there is almost no water left. Her colour comes entirely from browning the onions.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large heavy pot, Cocotte or second pan, for the onion sauce, Knife

    For the sauce, as printed

    • 3 onions
    • 50 ml vegetable oil
    • 1 bay leaf
    • 5 cloves garlic
    • 0.5 bell pepper — red, green or yellow
    • 1 tbsp vinegar
    • 2 dried bird's eye chillies
    • Salt and pepper
    • 1 carrot
    • 1 turnip
    • Pitted green olives

    From the method

    • About 1.5 litres water

    No quantity is printed for the meat or the rice. See the note.

    • Meat, cut into pieces
    • Rice

    Optional: 1/2 sugar, unit not printed in the source

      Method

      1. Heat the oil. Once hot, brown the meat pieces.
      2. When well browned, add one sliced onion and the other half of the pepper, diced.
      3. Keep caramelising, stirring occasionally. The onions must be well browned, even brown, to get a good rice colour.
      4. Add about 1.5 litres of water and bring to the boil.
      5. Salt, add a fresh chilli and a bay leaf, then leave it to simmer 1 hour on low heat.

      The onion sauce

      1. Dice or slice the remaining onions. Put a little oil in a cocotte and add the onions once the oil is somewhat hot.
      2. Meanwhile chop the garlic, pepper and chilli into a small farce.
      3. Add the bay leaf and stir so it does not stick.
      4. When the onions turn translucent, add the farce, salt and pepper.
      5. Add a little water, then the vinegar.
      6. Peel and dice the turnip and carrot, add them, mix well and leave to cook a few minutes on low heat.
      7. Adjust the salt near the end. The sauce is ready when there is almost no water left.
      8. Finish with the green olives, and serve with the rice cooked in the meat broth.

      Chef Tips

      • Her colour rule is the whole dish: the onions must be well browned, even brown, to get a good rice colour. No tomato, no bouillon, no soy sauce — just properly caramelised onions.
      • The sauce is ready when there is almost no water left. That is the endpoint, not a time.
      • Brown the meat before anything else goes in the pot, and get it well browned rather than merely sealed.
      • Vinegar in the onion sauce is what keeps an hour of caramelising from turning cloying.
      • Her own framing: much simpler than thiéboudieune, and the thieb you make when you are feeling lazy but still want to eat properly. She is right.
      • Note her seasoning tip calls for half a sugar to lift the taste, but the source never prints the unit — half a sugar cube is implied and never stated.
      • Pitted olives at the end are not a garnish; they are part of the seasoning and they belong in the sauce.

      Common Mistakes to Avoid

      • Under-browning the onions and then reaching for tomato paste to fake the colour.
      • Stopping the sauce while liquid remains.
      • Adding water before the meat has coloured.
      • Skipping the vinegar.
      • Boiling rather than simmering the hour.

      Health Benefits

      • High in protein and iron from the meat.
      • No bouillon cubes, so markedly lower in sodium than most published Senegalese rice dishes.
      • Carrot and turnip add fibre and beta-carotene.
      • Gluten-free and dairy-free.

      Cultural Note

      Every cuisine has the dish that gets overlooked because it is easy, and in Senegal it is thiebou yaap — the meat thieb that takes an hour instead of three and asks nothing of you but patience with a pan of onions. Getting its colour from browning rather than from a bouillon cube is a point of pride worth keeping.

      Storage & Serving

      Storage: 4 days refrigerated, 3 months frozen.

      Reheating: Steam the rice; warm the meat and sauce gently.

      Serve with: Green olives, Kaani, Attaya
