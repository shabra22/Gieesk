🥗 Edikang Ikong with Six Bunches of Ugwu

Edikang ikong — ikong ubong

Six medium bunches of ugwu and no waterleaf at all — because, as the author says, the ugwu was very tender and did not need it.

This is the version that explains the whole argument about edikang ikong. The author lists one bunch of waterleaf and then notes that she did not use it, because the ugwu was very tender. That single remark reframes the famous ratio: waterleaf is there to supply moisture and to help soften tougher ugwu, so the right amount depends on the leaves in front of you rather than on a fixed rule. She adds three cups of water outright, which also puts the no-water rule in perspective. The leaves take five to seven minutes depending on how tender they are.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large pot with a lid, Colander, Sharp knife

  • 8 medium pieces beef
  • 8 small pieces thick ikpa (cow skin, pomo)
  • 8 medium pieces shaki (optional)
  • A handful of shredded stockfish
  • 1 medium smoked catfish or shine nose fish
  • 6 medium bunches ikong ubong (ugwu leaves)
  • 1 bunch waterleaf — see the note; the author did not use hers
  • 4 snails (optional)
  • 1 bulb onion, chopped
  • 3-4 pieces yellow pepper
  • 2 cooking spoons palm oil
  • 3 tbsp crayfish
  • 1 beef seasoning cube
  • 2 crayfish seasoning tablets
  • 3 cups water
  • Salt to taste

Method

  1. Season the beef, ikpa and shaki with the beef seasoning, chopped onion and salt, and steam till the beef juice dries up.
  2. Add 2 cups of water and continue cooking until the meats are tender.
  3. Add the shredded stockfish, the smoked fish and the snails if using.
  4. Add the yellow pepper, the crayfish and the crayfish seasoning tablets.
  5. Add the palm oil and the remaining cup of water, and let it cook through.
  6. Add the ugwu leaves and cook about 5 to 7 minutes, depending on how tender the leaves are.
  7. If your ugwu is tough rather than tender, add the sliced waterleaf before it to supply moisture.
  8. Taste, adjust the salt, and serve.

Chef Tips

  • The author's own note is the key to this whole dish: she skipped the waterleaf because the ugwu was very tender. Judge the leaves, not the recipe.
  • Five to seven minutes depending on how tender the leaves are — again, a condition rather than a clock.
  • Till the beef juice dries up is the first-stage cue, and it is clearer than a time. You want the pot nearly dry before any water goes in.
  • Three cups of water in total, added in two stages. The famous no-water rule is about the first stage only.
  • Six medium bunches of ugwu is a great deal of leaf, and that is the character of the dish — it should be dense with greens.
  • Ikong ubong is the Efik name for fluted pumpkin leaf, and it is the leaf the soup is named after.
  • Two crayfish seasoning tablets plus three tablespoons of actual crayfish is a lot of crayfish, and it is what gives this version its depth.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Adding waterleaf regardless of how tender the ugwu is.
  • Adding water before the beef juice has dried.
  • Timing the leaves rather than watching them.
  • Skimping on the ugwu, which makes it a thin soup.
  • Boiling hard once the greens are in.

Health Benefits

  • An exceptional quantity of fluted pumpkin leaf, high in iron, calcium and vitamins A and C.
  • Very low in carbohydrate.
  • High in protein and collagen from ikpa, shaki and stockfish.
  • Snails are very lean and rich in iron and magnesium.

Cultural Note

That one cook writes down why she left an ingredient out is worth more than a dozen recipes that simply list it. The edikang ikong ratio has been argued about for as long as it has been written down, and the answer turns out not to be a number at all — it is a judgement about the leaves you have, which is the kind of thing a recipe is bad at carrying and a cook learns by eye.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 4 days refrigerated, 3 months frozen.

Reheating: Low heat, briefly.

Serve with: Iyan, Eba or garri, Akpu or fufu
