🥛 Eshabwe

Eshabwe — Kitaama

Aged ghee whipped in one direction with a wooden masher while salted cold water goes in a spoonful at a time, until it doubles, thickens and turns pure white — no heat anywhere near it.

Eshabwe is the ceremonial sauce of Ankole, made from the ghee of long-horned cattle and nothing else but salt and water. It is an emulsion built by hand: the ghee is beaten and the brine added drop by drop until the yellow fat turns white and doubles in volume. The rule that matters most is that no heat is involved at any stage — an Ankole account puts it flatly, never boil the ghee, no hot utensils. Best results come from ghee aged around two months in traditional gourds.

Ingredients

Equipment: Small clean bowl, Small wooden masher, or a wooden spoon, Two fine sieves, Measuring spoon

  • 250 g ghee, clean and mature — aged about 2 months if you can
  • 150 ml (10 tbsp) cold water, previously boiled and cooled
  • 5 ml (1 tsp) powdered rock salt (rwabarire)
  • 2.5 ml (1/2 tsp) table salt

Method

  1. Dissolve both the rock salt and the table salt in 75 ml — half — of the cold boiled water.
  2. Sieve the solution through two fine sieves to trap any impurities in the salt.
  3. Put the ghee in a small clean bowl and stir it briskly with a small wooden masher, always in one direction, until smooth.
  4. Add the salt solution a tablespoonful at a time, stirring briskly after each addition.
  5. The ghee will begin to thicken and turn white.
  6. Keep adding a little of the solution and stirring briskly until the ghee increases in volume and is smooth, thick and pure white.
  7. If it curdles and looks like spoilt milk, add a little more salt solution along with some of the remaining water, gradually, a little at a time.
  8. When it has doubled in volume and is very thick, creamy and pure white, taste it and add a pinch of table salt if it needs it.
  9. Thin it if you like with the remaining water, a tablespoonful at a time, stirring well after each — the finished sauce should be thick, not runny.
  10. Sieve into a clean bowl and serve with hot matooke, millet bread, cassava or sweet potatoes.

Chef Tips

  • No heat, ever. An Ankole account is unambiguous: never boil the ghee, and no heat or hot utensils are used in the process. Warm ghee will not emulsify, it will melt.
  • One direction only. Reversing the stroke breaks the emulsion you are building.
  • A tablespoon at a time. This is the single most common failure — pour the brine in and you get curdled ghee and salty water.
  • If it curdles, it is recoverable: more solution plus a little plain water, added gradually, brings it back.
  • Doubled in volume and pure white is the doneness cue. There is no time given in any source because it depends entirely on the ghee.
  • Aged ghee is better than fresh — around two months in a gourd, traditionally, and ideally from Ankole cattle for the high butterfat.
  • On the water temperature the sources genuinely disagree. Two say cold, including the Ankole report that forbids heat outright; one 2017 newspaper version says warm and not hot, and that is the only source with a usable ratio. The quantities here are from the warm-water version and the cold water is from the traditional accounts, which is the honest combination.
  • The rock salt is named rwabarire, though whether that word means the salt itself or the salt-and-water brine is contested between sources.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Warming or melting the ghee.
  • Adding the water all at once.
  • Stirring in both directions.
  • Giving up when it curdles instead of adding more solution gradually.
  • Using fresh, unaged ghee and wondering why it tastes flat.
  • Thinning it to a pourable sauce — it should stay thick.

Health Benefits

  • Ghee supplies fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K, and clarifying removes almost all the lactose and casein.
  • No cooking at all, so nothing is degraded by heat.
  • It is essentially whipped fat with salt. A spoonful is a serving and it is celebration food.

Cultural Note

Ankole is cattle country and the long-horned cattle are close to sacred, so a sauce made of nothing but their ghee, salt and water carries a particular weight. Eshabwe goes over millet bread or matooke at weddings and in front of guests you want to honour, and a cook who can bring it to pure white without curdling it has a skill people talk about.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days in a covered bowl. It stiffens when cold — bring it back to room temperature and stir.

Reheating: Never. Heat destroys it.

Serve with: Matooke, Posho, Millet bread
