🌸 Waterblommetjiebredie

Waterblommetjiebredie

Mutton browned and stewed with onion and nutmeg for an hour, then the Cape pondweed flower spikes and potatoes go in for another — soaked in salted water first, because they come out of a dam.

Waterblommetjies are the flower spikes of Aponogeton distachyos, Cape pondweed, which grows wild in the dams and marshes of the Boland and is now farmed as well. They eat like the leaves of a small artichoke, taste somewhere near green beans, and carry a floral, faintly lemony note. Nothing else in South African cooking is like this and nothing outside the Western Cape can really substitute for it — which makes this the most genuinely regional dish in the collection.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large bowl, for soaking, Colander, Large heavy-based saucepan with a lid

    The waterblommetjies

    • 500 g waterblommetjies
    • 1.4 L (6 cups) cold water
    • 1 tbsp salt

    The stew

    • 3 tbsp olive or vegetable oil
    • 1.5 kg mutton, lamb knuckles, neck or rib cutlets
    • Salt and pepper, to taste
    • 1 large onion, chopped
    • 240 ml (1 cup) hot water
    • 1/4 tsp ground nutmeg
    • 4 large potatoes, peeled and cubed
    • 1 lemon, juiced — or a few surings (Cape sorrel), if you can find them

    To serve

    • Cooked rice

    Method

    1. Put the waterblommetjies in a large bowl and cover them with the cold water.
    2. Add the tablespoon of salt, stir gently, and leave them to soak for 30 to 60 minutes. This is how the sand comes out — they are picked out of dams.
    3. Drain them in a colander, rinse well under cold running water and set aside.
    4. Heat the oil in a large heavy-based saucepan over moderately high heat and fry the meat in batches until lightly browned on some sides, seasoning as you go. Remove and set aside.
    5. Reduce the heat to medium and fry the onion until soft and translucent.
    6. Return the meat, add the hot water and the nutmeg, bring to a simmer with the lid on, and cook over low heat for about an hour.
    7. Add the potatoes and the drained waterblommetjies and cook a further hour, or until the potatoes are soft and the meat is very tender.
    8. Stir the lemon juice, or the surings, through at the end and check the seasoning. Serve warm with rice.

    Chef Tips

    • The salted soak is not optional. Wild-picked waterblommetjies carry dam sand and there is no other way to get it out.
    • The source recipe lists the lemon juice or surings and then never says when to add them. At the end is where they belong — the acid lifts the whole pot and cooking it for an hour would waste it.
    • Waterblommetjies are a winter and early spring crop in the Western Cape, which is when this is made. Tinned ones exist and are a reasonable stand-in out of season.
    • Suring, Cape sorrel, is the traditional souring agent and lemon is the everyday substitute. If you can find surings, use them.
    • Lightly browned on some sides is the instruction, not a hard sear. The flowers are delicate and a heavily caramelised base overwhelms them.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Skipping the soak and serving a gritty stew.
    • Adding the waterblommetjies at the start, where two hours reduces them to threads.
    • Cooking the acid in from the beginning.
    • Over-browning the meat, which buries the floral note the dish exists for.

    Cultural Note

    This is the one dish in South African cooking that genuinely cannot be exported, because the plant grows where it grows. Waterblommetjie picking is a Boland and Overberg winter activity, and it is one of the clearer cases of Khoi and San plant knowledge surviving into Afrikaans kitchens through the names and the practices rather than through any written record.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days.

    Reheating: Reheat gently on the hob. The flowers soften further, so stop as soon as it is hot.

    Serve with: Cooked white rice, Blatjang, Fresh bread
