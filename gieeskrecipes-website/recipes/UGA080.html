🥜 Spiced Groundnut Sauce

Binyebwa

Tomatoes cooked down with paste, a Royco slurry built into a thick sauce, and only then three hundred grams of groundnut paste blended in off the heat before half a litre of hot water goes back.

The argument behind this recipe is that groundnut sauce does not have to be boiled paste with salt and a few vegetables — that you can build a proper sauce underneath it. So the tomato and bouillon base is made first and thickened, and the groundnut paste goes in last, blended off the heat if it is stiff, and only then lengthened with half a litre of hot water. The greens go in for the final five minutes. It is a more constructed sauce than the traditional one and it is very good.

Ingredients

Equipment: Pan with a lid, Wooden spoon

  • 300 g groundnut paste
  • 1 large onion, sliced
  • 1 clove garlic, finely grated
  • 2 large tomatoes, finely chopped
  • 30 ml (2 tbsp) tomato paste
  • 30 ml (2 tbsp) Royco
  • 1 bunch green leafy vegetables, washed and finely chopped
  • 15 ml (1 tbsp) oil
  • 5 ml (1 tsp) salt
  • 500 ml hot water, plus a little more as needed

Listed in the source but never used in its method

  • 1 chicken stock cube

Method

  1. Finely grate the garlic, slice the onion and finely chop the tomatoes. Wash the greens as many times as needed to remove all dust and dirt, and chop them finely.
  2. Heat the oil in a pan and fry the onions until they just begin to colour. Add the garlic and fry, stirring, until golden.
  3. Add the chopped tomatoes, stir well, and cook covered over medium heat until the tomatoes are cooked, adding a little water as you go to stop them burning.
  4. Add the tomato paste and cook, stirring, a few minutes. Add a little water to make a thick paste and continue a few minutes more.
  5. Dissolve the Royco in a little water and add it to the tomato mixture. Cook, stirring, a few minutes, adding a little more water to make a thick sauce. Reduce the heat and simmer a few minutes.

The groundnut paste

  1. Add the groundnut paste and blend it in well by stirring while gradually adding a little hot water. You may need to take the pan off the heat and blend the paste first, depending on how thick it is.
  2. When the paste is well blended with no lumps, return it to the heat, add about half a litre of hot water and bring to the boil, stirring.
  3. Reduce the heat, add a little more water and simmer until the groundnuts are cooked.
  4. Add the greens and salt and simmer about 5 minutes, stirring occasionally.
  5. Serve with your staple and a glass of chilled fruit juice.

Chef Tips

  • Take the pan off the heat to blend the paste if it is stiff. That is the instruction that stops lumps, and it is the whole difficulty of every groundnut sauce.
  • Build the base first. Onion, garlic, tomato, tomato paste and bouillon all cooked to a thick sauce before the groundnut goes anywhere near it.
  • Hot water for the groundnut paste here, gradually — which is the opposite of the cold-water rule used elsewhere. It works because you are adding it a little at a time with constant stirring into an already hot sauce.
  • Simmer until the groundnuts are cooked, not just hot. Raw groundnut paste tastes chalky.
  • The greens get five minutes only.
  • Wash the greens as many times as necessary. That is the printed phrasing and it is realistic about Ugandan market greens.
  • A chicken stock cube is in the ingredient list and never used — the method uses only Royco. This is the second Monitor house recipe with an orphaned bouillon line, so it looks like a template rather than an oversight.
  • This is the modern constructed version. The plainer traditional sauce is Binyebwa and the dried-fish one is Groundnut Sauce with Dried Fish.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Adding the groundnut paste to a thin, unbuilt base.
  • Trying to stir a stiff paste in over the heat.
  • Adding all the water at once.
  • Taking it off before the groundnuts have cooked.
  • Cooking the greens for as long as the sauce.

Health Benefits

  • Groundnut paste is high in plant protein, niacin, folate and vitamin E.
  • Leafy greens add iron, calcium and vitamins A, C and K.
  • One tablespoon of oil across five servings.
  • Contains peanuts, and the bouillon makes it high in sodium.

Cultural Note

Groundnut sauce is the dish Ugandans are most willing to argue about, and this recipe is a position in that argument: that the sauce deserves a built base rather than being a paste loosened with water. It is worth trying alongside the plainer version to see what the extra work buys.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days.

Reheating: Warm gently with a splash of water.

Serve with: Matooke, Posho, Boiled Sweet Potatoes
