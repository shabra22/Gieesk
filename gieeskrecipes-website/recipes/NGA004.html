🍲 Gbegiri

Gbegiri — obe gbegiri

Peeled beans cooked to collapse with no salt at all, mashed with the ijabe or blended, then pushed through a sieve — the second de-skinning that most people skip.

Gbegiri is the yellow bean half of abula, and the whole dish is about getting the skins out. They come off twice: once from the soaked raw bean, by hand or by pulsing it in a blender until each bean splits, and again after cooking, when the puree goes through a sieve and the chaff is discarded. Salt is withheld until the beans are soft, which the author says makes them cook faster. It thickens very fast once it is done, so expect to loosen it.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large pot, Blender, Ijabe (ewedu broom) or whisk, Fine sieve

  • 2 cups black-eyed or brown beans
  • 2 cups water to start, more as needed
  • About 1/2 cup water, for blending
  • 3 tbsp palm oil
  • 2 tbsp crayfish powder
  • 1 tbsp locust beans (iru)
  • 1-2 tbsp ground dried pepper (atagungun)
  • 1 bouillon cube
  • Salt to taste

Method

  1. Soak the beans, then peel — by hand, or pulse a few times in a jug blender so each bean splits and the skin falls off, and finish by hand.
  2. Cook the peeled beans with water, starting with 2 cups and adding more as needed, until very soft and mushy. Do not add salt at this stage.
  3. Mash with an ijabe or a whisk; or cool slightly and blend smooth with about half a cup of water.
  4. If you mashed by hand, pass the puree through a sieve to remove the clumps.
  5. Return it to low heat and add water to a semi-fluid consistency.
  6. Add the palm oil, iru, crayfish powder, bouillon cube, ground pepper and salt.
  7. Cook 5 to 7 minutes, stirring, until it has thickened and the palm oil is well combined.

Chef Tips

  • No salt until the beans are soft. Salt early and they take much longer.
  • Pulse, do not blend, to peel. You want each bean to split, not to puree raw beans.
  • The sieve is the step people drop, and it is the difference between velvet and grit. Push it through and throw the chaff away.
  • The palm oil is not bleached for gbegiri. No gbegiri source consulted bleaches it — that is an ayamase technique, not a general palm-oil step.
  • It thickens quickly and will keep thickening as it cools. The author's own note: do not be alarmed, just add a bit of water and reheat.
  • Done is when it has thickened and the oil has stopped sitting separate on top.
  • Unpeeled beans will work at a push — one named source says so outright — but you will not get the smooth yellow texture the dish is known for.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Salting the beans at the start.
  • Blending the raw soaked beans instead of pulsing them.
  • Skipping the sieve.
  • Bleaching the palm oil.
  • Judging the consistency hot — it will be much thicker cold.

Cultural Note

Gbegiri is buka food — the roadside eating houses of the southwest, where it arrives beside ewedu with a ladle of stew over both. The same bean soup turns up in the north as miyan wake, which 9jafoodie glosses directly as gbegiri, making it one of the few dishes with a settled name on both sides of the country.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 4 days refrigerated, 3 months frozen.

Reheating: Low heat with water to loosen. It sets firm when cold.

Serve with: Amala, Ewedu, Abula
