🥘 Plantain Eto

Eto — mashed plantain with palm oil

Slightly ripe plantain mashed with red palm oil, ground groundnuts, onion and pepper — the dish given to brides on the morning of a wedding.

Eto is mashed starch enriched with palm oil, and it is ceremonial food before it is everyday food. Three slightly ripe plantains and two eggs boil together for half an hour; onions, chillies and roasted groundnuts are ground, with smoked herring and koobi if you have them; sliced onions are heated in palm oil, and the oil goes into the mashed plantain until the whole thing is a deep red-orange. It is served to brides the morning before weddings with eggs, and is described by its author as the local version of a birthday cake.

Ingredients

Equipment: Pot, Earthenware grinding bowl (asanka) and grinder, or a mortar, Masher or wooden spoon, Small frying pan

  • 3 slightly ripe plantains
  • 2 eggs
  • 3 chili peppers
  • 1 medium onion
  • 7 tbsp palm oil — see the note on the quantity
  • Roasted groundnuts
  • Salt, to taste

To serve

  • 1 avocado
  • Dried smoked herrings and koobi (optional)

Method

  1. Boil the plantains and the eggs together with salt for 30 minutes.
  2. Grind the onions, peppers and roasted groundnuts.
  3. Add the smoked herring and koobi, if using, and grind into the mixture in an earthenware bowl.
  4. Mash the plantains separately until smooth.
  5. Heat the sliced onions in 5 to 6 tablespoons of the palm oil.
  6. Mix the palm oil into the mashed plantain.
  7. Combine the mash with the ground mixture.
  8. Garnish with the boiled eggs and sliced avocado, and serve.

Chef Tips

  • Slightly ripe, not ripe. Fully ripe plantain goes sweet and sticky and will not take the oil.
  • The palm oil quantity does not add up as printed — the ingredient list says seven tablespoons, the method uses five to six for the onions and then adds more to the plantain. Treat seven as the total and start with five.
  • Boil the eggs with the plantains. One pot, and the eggs are part of the dish rather than a garnish.
  • Grind in an earthenware asanka if you have one. The rough interior does something a blender cannot, and the source specifies it.
  • Red palm oil, not refined. The colour is the dish — eto without it is just mashed plantain.
  • The avocado is not optional in practice. Its fat and coolness are what the dish is built against.
  • Served without salt or pepper when it is being used ceremonially — Osseo-Asare records that the sacred form of the dish is given plain.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Using fully ripe plantain.
  • All seven tablespoons of oil into the onions.
  • Blending rather than grinding, which gives a smooth paste instead of texture.
  • Refined oil in place of red palm oil.
  • Skipping the eggs, which are structural rather than decorative.

Health Benefits

  • Red palm oil is one of the richest known food sources of provitamin-A carotenoids and vitamin E tocotrienols.
  • Groundnuts and eggs together make this a complete-protein dish.
  • Plantain supplies potassium and resistant starch.
  • Avocado adds monounsaturated fat and folate.

Cultural Note

Eto is what you are given at the thresholds of a life — the morning of a wedding, a birthday, the naming of a child — and the eggs on top are the marker that this is an occasion rather than a meal. Its author's framing is that Ghanaians have abandoned it, which is itself worth recording: this is a dish being written about because it is slipping away.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 2 days refrigerated. The palm oil will solidify and the colour deepens.

Reheating: Gentle heat, or let it come to room temperature. Do not microwave hard.

Serve with: Sliced avocado, Boiled eggs, Dried smoked herring and koobi
