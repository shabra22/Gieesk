🧵 Dambu Nama

Dambu nama — dambun saniya, beef floss

Two kilos of beef boiled dry, pounded to shreds, then shallow-fried at 165–170°C — and the cue is that the meat absorbs oil first and releases it when cooked.

Dambu nama is shredded beef floss and it is the only recipe in the northern set with a stated frying temperature. Two kilos of beef are seasoned with yaji, a stock cube, onion and ginger, covered with water and boiled until the meat softens and the liquid has evaporated. It is then pounded in batches in a mortar until fully shredded, more yaji is worked in, and it is shallow-fried five to ten minutes at 165 to 170°C. The doneness cue is excellent and worth memorising: the meat absorbs oil at first, then releases it when it is cooked.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large pot, Mortar and pestle, or a mallet or rolling pin, Large frying pan, Thermometer, Strainer and kitchen towels

  • 2 kg beef, trimmed of fat and sinew and cut into chunks
  • 1 tbsp yaji — used at two stages, see the note
  • 1 stock cube
  • 1 large fresh onion, chopped
  • 1 knob of fresh ginger, pounded
  • Peanut oil (or vegetable oil), for frying
  • Salt

Method

  1. Season the beef chunks with the yaji, stock cube, onions, ginger and salt. Cover with water and boil until the meat softens and the liquid evaporates. Check the seasoning.
  2. Pound the meat in batches in a mortar until shredded, raking through to make sure the shredding is complete. A mallet, or a rolling pin on a chopping board, will also do it.
  3. Add more of the yaji and work it into the meat.
  4. Heat the peanut oil in a large pan and shallow-fry in batches, 5 to 10 minutes, keeping the oil at 165 to 170°C for a soft and chewy texture rather than dry or greasy.
  5. Stir continuously. The meat initially absorbs oil, then releases it when cooked.
  6. Drain in a strainer lined with kitchen towels and press gently.
  7. Refrigerate for several weeks, or freeze for longer.

Chef Tips

  • Absorbs oil, then releases it. That is the doneness cue and it is one of the best in this whole collection — watch the pan rather than the clock.
  • 165 to 170°C. Hotter and it goes dry and brittle; cooler and it stays greasy. This is the only stated oil temperature in the northern set.
  • Boil until the liquid has evaporated, not just until tender. Wet meat will not shred and will not fry.
  • Pound in batches and rake through between them. Partially shredded dambu has lumps that never fry properly.
  • Stir continuously throughout the frying. Nothing about this stage is passive.
  • The yaji goes in at two stages — with the boiling and again after shredding — but the ingredient list gives one tablespoon in total and does not say how to split it. Half and half is a reasonable reading.
  • Trim the fat and sinew properly before you start. Sinew will not shred and fat will not fry out.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Boiling only until tender and leaving liquid in the pot.
  • Frying too hot, which gives dry brittle floss.
  • Shredding incompletely.
  • Leaving the pan unattended during frying.
  • Not draining and pressing, which leaves it greasy.

Health Benefits

  • Very high in protein and very low in carbohydrate.
  • Trimmed of fat before cooking, and the frying fat is drained off.
  • Lean beef supplies iron, zinc and B12.
  • Keeps for weeks without refrigeration issues.

Cultural Note

Dambu belongs to the northern category of specialty meats — the same family as suya, kilishi and balangu — and it is defined by the pounding rather than the cooking. The naming system is systematic in a way few Nigerian food vocabularies are: dambun nama for beef, dambun kazaa for chicken, dambun rakumi for camel. The animal names the dish.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Several weeks refrigerated, per the source; months frozen.

Reheating: Eat as is, or refresh briefly in a dry pan.

Serve with: Yaji, Gurasa or bread, Hot tea
