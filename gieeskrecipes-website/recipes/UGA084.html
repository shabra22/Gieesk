Lakotokoto

Unroasted sesame ground coarse, pressed with hot water to draw the oil out, rolled into balls and dropped into boiling water — named, people think, for the sound it makes as it cooks.

Lakotokoto is made from ground unroasted simsim, which makes it quite different from odii and from everything else in the northern repertoire. It stands alongside dek ngor, boo and malakwang as a dish that has stood the test of time in Acholi. The processing is the craft: grind coarse but not to a paste, pour hot water on and press to release the oil, then roll the pressed simsim into balls. The name is thought to come from the sound it makes in the pot.

Ingredients

Equipment: Mortar and pestle, grinding stone, or a grinder, Winnowing basket, Bowl and press, Saucepan with a lid

  • Unroasted simsim (sesame) — no quantities are given in the source
  • Hot water, for pressing
  • Water, for boiling
  • Onions
  • Tomatoes
  • Salt

Optional

  • Soda ash or magadi
  • Boiled dry fish, or beef or offal cooked separately

If frying rather than boiling

  • The lakotokoto oil, or any cooking oil

Method

    Choosing the simsim

    1. Look for fresh clean simsim, or wash it clean to remove the bitter taste.
    2. Some cooks pound it slightly and thresh it to remove the outer shells by winnowing, which is credited for a whitish look in the finished dish rather than a blackish one.

    Grinding

    1. Grind it, on a grinding machine or a traditional grinding stone.
    2. Grind it only slightly, so it does not turn completely into a paste, but enough to remove the visible tiny particles of seed.

    Pressing out the oil

    1. Boil water for pressing the ground simsim.
    2. Pour the hot water onto the ground simsim, carefully and in balanced proportion, so the lump is not overwhelmed by water. Hot water rather than cold is used because it eases the pressing.
    3. Press the lump until it is clear of oil, and pour the oil off into a separate container.

    Shaping

    1. Roll the pressed simsim into small balls and spread them in a container, ready for cooking.
    2. Some cooks mix the ground simsim with soda ash or magadi to keep the taste and keep it fresh.

    Cooking

    1. Boil water, then add your chosen ingredients — onions and tomatoes for boiled lakotokoto.
    2. Or, to fry it, fry the onions and tomatoes in the lakotokoto oil or any cooking oil.
    3. Pour the lump of ground lakotokoto slowly into the boiling water.
    4. After about 20 minutes, add salt.
    5. Keep the water just sufficient so it does not burn.
    6. Leave it cooking at least an hour, covered so it steams and keeps its aroma.
    7. Serve with kwon kal or kalo, sweet potatoes, or cassava flour dough.

    Chef Tips

    • Grind it only slightly. Ground to a paste you have sesame butter, not lakotokoto — the coarseness is the dish.
    • Hot water for the pressing, not cold. It eases the process of pressing the oil out, which is the stated reason.
    • Taste the simsim before you buy it. Bitterness in the seed carries straight through, and the people who cook this professionally say so.
    • There are two incompatible techniques in the one good source and neither is wrong. One cook presses the oil out and separates it off completely; another presses only slightly and does not separate it at all, arguing that keeping the oil prevents the dish becoming bitter. Both are recorded.
    • Twenty minutes before the salt, then at least an hour of cooking. It is a slow dish.
    • Keep the water sufficient but no more. Too much and it will not thicken; too little and it burns.
    • Cover it as it steams, to retain the aroma.
    • The name is thought to come from the sound the food makes as it cooks — la-ko-to-ko-to. Nobody has established that, but everybody says it.
    • No quantities are given anywhere in the source. Nothing here is estimated to cover that.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Grinding it to a paste.
    • Using cold water to press.
    • Buying bitter simsim without tasting it.
    • Adding the salt at the start.
    • Adding too much water so it never comes together.

    Health Benefits

    • Sesame is one of the richest plant sources of calcium, plus magnesium, iron and zinc.
    • High in plant protein and in lignans.
    • Pressing the oil out reduces the fat considerably, which is one reason the two techniques give different dishes.
    • Naturally free of gluten and dairy.

    Cultural Note

    Unroasted sesame is the unusual thing here — northern Uganda roasts simsim for almost everything else, and odii, the signature condiment, is roasted sesame and groundnut pounded together. That lakotokoto goes the other way, and presses the oil out rather than keeping it in, makes it the most technically distinctive dish in the region.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days.

    Reheating: Warm gently with a little water.

    Serve with: Kalo, Boiled Sweet Potatoes, Malakwang
