🥚 Ayamase with Boiled Eggs

Ayamase

A tighter, measured ayamase — half a cup of palm oil bleached ten to twelve minutes with the lid shut, four green peppers, and four to six boiled eggs actually in the ingredient list.

This is the most manageable of the ayamase versions: half a cup of palm oil rather than an unstated amount, four green bell peppers rather than six, and a stated yield. It is also the one that puts the eggs where they belong — on the ingredient list, four to six of them, rather than appearing unannounced in the method. The bleaching instruction here is the strictest: ten to twelve minutes on medium heat with the lid closed throughout, which keeps the smoke in but means you cannot watch the oil, so the endpoint she describes becomes something you have to take on trust.

Ingredients

Equipment: Heavy pot with a tight lid, Blender, Open windows

  • 4 green bell peppers, sliced into 2 halves
  • 1 large onion, cut into quarters
  • 2 green chilies
  • 2 habanero (rodo) or scotch bonnet
  • 1/2 cup palm oil
  • 1 medium onion, diced
  • 2 tbsp locust beans (iru)
  • 2 tbsp crayfish, divided
  • Beef stock, as needed
  • 1 1/2 lb beef and tripe, or any mix of protein of choice
  • 4-6 boiled eggs
  • Salt to taste

Method

  1. Open your windows and doors. Bleaching palm oil can be smoky.
  2. Blend the green bell peppers, quartered onion, green chilies and habaneros.
  3. In a well-ventilated space, heat the palm oil in a heavy pot over medium heat for about 10 to 12 minutes, until it darkens slightly and loses its raw smell. Do not open the lid while it bleaches.
  4. Add the diced onion and fry until fragrant.
  5. Add the locust beans and half the crayfish, and fry until aromatic.
  6. Add the blended pepper mixture and fry, stirring, until the water has cooked off and the oil returns to the surface.
  7. Add the beef and tripe, the remaining crayfish and salt.
  8. Add beef stock as needed to loosen.
  9. Add the boiled eggs whole and simmer until everything is hot through and the oil floats clear on top.

Chef Tips

  • Half a cup of oil is the measurement to use if you want a quantity rather than a judgement call. The other versions either decline to give one or ask you to fry meat in it first.
  • The lid stays shut while the oil bleaches. That controls the smoke — but it also means you cannot see the colour change, so time it.
  • 'Darkens slightly and loses its raw smell' is her endpoint. Note that another named source describes the finished oil as going clear like vegetable oil. These are not the same state, and both are published by careful cooks.
  • The crayfish is divided — half early with the iru, half late with the meat. Early for depth, late for aroma.
  • Eggs go in whole and late. They are there to absorb the stew, not to cook in it.
  • If you want a verifiable endpoint for the oil, Ajoke's white-paper test is the only falsifiable one any source offers: dip a white paper in the oil, and if it comes out clean and translucent the oil is ready.
  • Four green bell peppers and two habaneros is a mild-to-medium ayamase. For the fierce version see Ofada Stew.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Opening the lid during bleaching, which fills the kitchen with smoke.
  • Bleaching with no ventilation at all.
  • Adding the eggs early so they break up.
  • Using all the crayfish at once.
  • Scaling the oil down below half a cup, which stops the stew frying.

Cultural Note

Boiled eggs in ayamase are not a garnish — they go in whole and come out stained green, saturated with the stew, and they are often the first thing taken off a party tray. The dish's nickname, designer stew, says most of what needs saying about where it sits socially: it is expensive to make, it takes real work, and it is served when someone is being honoured.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 1 week refrigerated. Remove the eggs before freezing.

Reheating: Gentle heat. Add the eggs back at the end if they were stored separately.

Serve with: Ofada rice, Boiled white rice, Eko
