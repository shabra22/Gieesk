🦗 Roasted Grasshoppers

Nsenene — nssenene, isanani

Grasshoppers dipped in wood ash so the wings and legs come away, washed clean of it, then dry-roasted fifteen minutes before salt, onion and tomato go in for ten more.

Nsenene are long-horned grasshoppers and in central Uganda they are not a curiosity — they represent community relationships, honesty and sharing. The main season is November, museene; May is the light season, biswamugosi. The cleaning is the work, and the traditional trick is wood ash: dipping the insects in it makes the wings, legs and hairlike tail fin come away far more easily. Then you wash the ash off completely and roast them dry.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large bowl, Heavy saucepan, Wooden spoon, Colander

  • 2 kg grasshoppers (nsenene)
  • 15 ml (3 tsp) salt
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • Tomatoes, chopped — no quantity is given in the source

For cleaning

  • Wood ash

Method

    Cleaning

    1. Clean the grasshoppers thoroughly, removing all wings, legs and the hairlike tail fin.
    2. Dip the insects into wood ash to make that removal easier.
    3. Remove them one by one and wash until clean and completely free of ash.

    Roasting

    1. Heat a saucepan, add the grasshoppers and stir continuously for about 15 minutes.
    2. Sprinkle with the salt, add the chopped tomatoes and onions.
    3. Stir constantly for about 10 minutes, until ready.

    Chef Tips

    • The wood ash is the technique. It dries the insects enough that wings and legs release cleanly, and without it the cleaning takes several times as long.
    • Wash the ash off completely. Every trace — ash left on tastes of ash.
    • Stir continuously for the whole fifteen minutes. Nothing is added to the pan at that stage, so they will catch the moment you stop.
    • The source lists salt and onion but its method also calls for tomatoes, with no quantity. They are listed here for that reason and you should judge the amount.
    • The three types are worth knowing at market: kulunkalu is brown and looks like a dry banana leaf; kulumbisi is green like a fresh one; kibazzi is the rarest, green with a purple stripe on the wings.
    • November is the main season and May the light one, so fresh nsenene are genuinely seasonal.
    • Traditionally a community call, wuuli, went up when a swarm was spotted, and the insects were collected into troughs called nkanga. Commercial harvesting now uses LED lights shone into the sky at night.
    • Two kilos is a market quantity. Scale down freely — the ratios hold.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Skipping the ash and trying to pull wings off wet insects.
    • Leaving ash residue behind.
    • Adding the salt and vegetables at the start.
    • Leaving the pan unattended during the dry roast.
    • Roasting them without cleaning off the tail fin.

    Health Benefits

    • Exceptionally high in protein — comparable to or better than most meat by weight.
    • A good source of iron, zinc and B vitamins.
    • Dry-roasted with no added fat at all.
    • Insect protein carries a far smaller environmental cost than livestock.

    Cultural Note

    Nsenene are among the clearest examples of a Ugandan food that outsiders treat as exotic and Ugandans treat as ordinary and a little sentimental. The community call when a swarm was spotted, and the sharing that followed, is why the booklet frames them as being about honesty and relationships rather than about protein.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days. Traditionally they are also sun-dried for longer keeping.

    Reheating: A few minutes in a dry pan.

    Serve with: Matooke, Posho, A cold drink
