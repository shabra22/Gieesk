🐚 Yeet, Fermented Dried Sea Snail

Yeet / yett / yété (Cymbium)

Cymbium whelk flesh removed from the shell, split in four, buried in sand or under old nets for a day (FAO: 2 to 4 days), washed and sun-dried two days until the whole treatment has taken 64% of the weight off — a semi-dry light brown product with a strong smell.

Yeet, the ‘Senegalese camembert’, is a fermented, dried sea-snail seasoning used in ceebu jën and soupou kandia. The shell and viscera are removed and the foot split in four lengthwise. At the landing site the pieces are placed in sand or under used nets for 24 hours to ferment, cleaned, and sun-dried for two days; the whole treatment results in a weight loss of 64% — the one measurable endpoint anywhere in the literature. FAO describes the finished product as a semi-dry light brown product with a strong smell, which redried becomes hard and dark brown. FAO gives longer times (2 to 4 days of fermentation in tanks, jute bags or polysacks, then 2 to 4 days of drying on raised platforms) — the two sources disagree and are not reconciled. No salt is mentioned in either. No pH, salt level, water activity or microbiology has ever been published for yeet.

Ingredients

Equipment: Sand pit or fermenting container, Raised drying platforms

  • Cymbium (yeet) whelks — quantity not printed
  • Sand or used nets for the ferment (CRODT), or fermentation tanks, jute bags or polysacks (FAO)
  • Clean water to wash

Method

    Prepare

    1. Remove the shell and the viscera. Split the foot in four lengthwise (FAO: into two or four parts).

    Ferment

    1. Place the pieces in sand, or under used nets, for 24 hours (CRODT), or in tanks, jute bags or polysacks for 2 to 4 days (FAO). No salting is mentioned.

    Clean and dry

    1. Clean the pieces (a non-peer-reviewed source adds washing in four to five waters) and dry them in the sun for 2 days (CRODT) or on raised platforms for 2 to 4 days (FAO).

    Judge the finished product

    1. Weigh in and weigh out: the whole treatment takes the pieces down by 64% of their starting weight (CRODT). The finished yeet is semi-dry, light brown, with a strong smell; re-drying makes it hard and dark brown.
    2. Rinse before use; a non-peer-reviewed source blanches it 5 minutes in boiling water to firm it.

    Chef Tips

    • The one hard number is the 64% weight loss across fermentation and drying.
    • Finished: ‘a semi-dry light brown product with a strong smell’ (FAO 329).
    • It is susceptible to a lot of mould growth and keeps about two months (FAO).

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Judging by days alone: the two published fermentation times differ by up to four-fold.
    • Trusting a home ferment: no safety limits have been established for yeet.
    • Re-drying hard — FAO notes redrying makes it hard and dark brown.

    Cultural Note

    CRODT’s Morinière records that the two operations give yeet ‘son goût particulier’ and its nickname ‘camembert sénégalais’, and make it a condiment of choice for perfuming dishes; the work is done by fishermen’s wives at the landing site. A further source (ICSF/CREDETIP, 2002) says traditional yeet is buried in the ground 3 to 4 days and that its drying was extended from three days to almost a month for Asian buyers who wanted a hygienic, reddish product — conflicting with the 24-hour and 2-day figures.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: About two months (FAO).

    Reheating: Not applicable — a seasoning.

    Serve with: Ceebu jën, Soupou kandia, Nokoss
