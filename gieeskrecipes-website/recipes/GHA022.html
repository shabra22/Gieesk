🥬 Ebunuebunu

Abunuabunu — ebunu ebunu, green green

Green green — cocoyam leaves blended with a teaspoon of groundnut paste, six cups of water, smoked fish and prekese.

Ebunuebunu means green green, and it is the Akan soup that fufu is eaten with when neither groundnut nor palm nut is wanted. Four cups of spinach or cocoyam leaves are cooked five minutes until soft, drained and blended with a single teaspoon of groundnut paste — just enough to bind, not enough to make it a groundnut soup. The meat is steamed, the blended aromatics and tomatoes cooked ten minutes, then the greens and six cups of water go in with the blended tomato and onion returned. Smoked fish for twenty minutes, prekese for five.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large pot, Blender, Colander

  • 4 cups spinach or cocoyam leaves
  • Choice of meat
  • Dried or smoked fish
  • Dried or smoked mackerel
  • 2 medium tomatoes
  • 2 medium onions
  • 3 peppers
  • 2 cloves garlic
  • Ginger
  • 1 tsp peanut butter or groundnut paste
  • 6 cups water
  • Prekese
  • Salt
  • Seasoning

Method

  1. Cook the greens 5 minutes, until soft.
  2. Steam the meat.
  3. Blend the ginger, garlic and peppers; add with the tomatoes and onions and cook 10 minutes.
  4. Drain the greens and blend them with the peanut butter.
  5. Add the blended greens with 6 cups of water.
  6. Blend the cooked onions and tomatoes and return them to the pot.
  7. Add the smoked fish and cook 20 minutes.
  8. Add the prekese and cook 5 minutes more, then taste and serve.

Chef Tips

  • One teaspoon of groundnut paste for the whole pot. It binds the blended greens so they do not separate — it is structural, not a flavour, and more would make this a different soup.
  • Five minutes on the greens and no longer. The name is green green, and overcooked cocoyam leaves go olive-grey.
  • Blend the greens AFTER draining them. Blending with the cooking water gives a thin, watery soup.
  • Prekese goes in last, for five minutes. It is the aromatic finish and prolonged boiling flattens it.
  • Cocoyam leaves are the authentic green and spinach is the accepted substitute — the author offers both. Cocoyam leaves need longer than spinach, so judge by softness.
  • The remove-blend-return move for the onions and tomatoes is the same technique the light soups use, and it is what keeps the soup smooth without frying anything.
  • Only the greens, tomatoes, onions, garlic, peppers and groundnut paste carry quantities in the source. The meat, fish, ginger and prekese do not, and no figures have been invented for them.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Overcooking the greens so the colour dulls.
  • Blending the greens in their cooking water.
  • Using a tablespoon of groundnut paste instead of a teaspoon.
  • Boiling the prekese for a long time.
  • Adding the smoked fish at the very end rather than giving it twenty minutes.

Health Benefits

  • Cocoyam leaves are very high in vitamin A, vitamin C, iron and calcium.
  • Low in carbohydrate and fat for a West African soup.
  • Smoked fish supplies omega-3 fats, protein and iodine.
  • Prekese has documented antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory activity.

Cultural Note

Green green is one of the better dish names in West Africa and it is also a precise instruction: if the soup is not vividly green you have cooked the leaves too long. It sits alongside groundnut and palm nut soup as the third of the three things fufu is properly eaten with, and it is the lightest of them.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 3 days refrigerated. The colour darkens.

Reheating: Gentle heat. Do not boil, or the green dulls further.

Serve with: Fufu, Omo Tuo, Banku
