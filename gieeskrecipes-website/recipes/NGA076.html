🟡 Onunu

Onunu

Yam and ripe plantain pounded together, with the palm oil added while the mash is still hot to stop it curdling — worked until the whole thing turns yellow.

Onunu is the Kalabari swallow: yam and ripe plantain boiled and pounded into one mass, enriched with palm oil. Two instructions in it are genuinely mechanistic and worth having. The yam is boiled halfway before the plantain joins it, because they soften at different rates. And the palm oil must go in while the mash is still hot, specifically to prevent curdling — then it is worked until the oil combines fully and the mixture turns yellow. No quantities are given anywhere, which is recorded rather than invented.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large pot, Mortar and pestle, or a food processor with a dough hook, or a stand mixer with a paddle

  • Yam
  • Ripe plantain
  • Palm oil
  • Fresh pepper (optional)

Method

  1. Peel and cut the yam, and boil it until halfway cooked.
  2. Add the ripe plantains and continue until both are soft throughout.
  3. Pound the yams first, because of their texture, then incorporate the plantains.
  4. A food processor with a dough hook or a stand mixer with a paddle may be used instead.
  5. Once it is smooth, add the palm oil while the mash is still hot, to prevent curdling.
  6. Continue pounding until the oil combines fully and the mixture turns yellow.
  7. Serve with a spicy fish pepper soup.

Chef Tips

  • Palm oil into hot mash. The reason is given outright — it prevents curdling — and it is the single most useful instruction in the recipe.
  • Yam halfway first, then the plantain. They do not soften at the same rate and adding them together gives you overcooked yam or undercooked plantain.
  • Pound the yam before incorporating the plantain. The yam needs the work; the plantain gives way easily.
  • Turning yellow is the endpoint, and it is the same visual cue as the ncha emulsion in the southeast — palm oil fully worked into a base reads as yellow rather than orange streaks.
  • No quantities are published anywhere for this dish. A rough starting point is equal volumes of yam and plantain with palm oil to taste, but that is a suggestion rather than a sourced ratio.
  • Ripe plantain, not unripe. Onunu is meant to be faintly sweet, which is the opposite of kekefiyai.
  • It is served with fish pepper soup, and that pairing is fixed rather than optional in Kalabari practice.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Adding the palm oil once the mash has cooled, which curdles it.
  • Boiling the yam and plantain together from the start.
  • Incorporating the plantain before the yam is properly pounded.
  • Stopping while the oil is still visible in streaks.
  • Using unripe plantain.

Health Benefits

  • Ripe plantain supplies potassium, vitamin A and vitamin B6.
  • Yam contributes resistant starch and potassium.
  • Red palm oil provides carotenoids and vitamin E.
  • Vegan and gluten-free.

Cultural Note

Onunu is the Kalabari answer to pounded yam, and the plantain in it changes the whole character — softer, faintly sweet, and yellow from the palm oil rather than white. It is eaten with fish pepper soup, which is the pairing the Rivers coast is known for, and the combination is as fixed there as amala with gbegiri is in Ibadan.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Eat the same day. It firms and dries quickly.

Reheating: Steam or microwave wrapped in damp cling film.

Serve with: Catfish Pepper Soup, Fresh fish pepper soup, Banga Soup
