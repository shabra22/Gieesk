🧂 A'itipa

A'itipa

Salty leaves gathered from the valleys, left to wither in the sun, then burnt dry — a plant salt that stands in for the mineral kind.

A'itipa is salt made from plants. Women collect various salty leaves, including from the valley, leave them in the sun to wither, and burn them while they are dry. The resulting ash is used as salt. It belongs to a set of three Lugbara alkaline preparations alongside ofuta, the ash cooking liquid, and magadi, which is a mineral salt got from saline water at places like Kibiro on the northern shores of Lake Albert. In a region far from any salt trade, this is how a kitchen gets seasoning.

Ingredients

Equipment: Collecting basket, Drying area in full sun, A fire or brazier, Storage container

  • Various salty leaves, including ones gathered from the valley

No quantities and no species are documented

    Method

    1. Collect various salty leaves, including from the valley.
    2. Leave the leaves to wither in the sun for some time.
    3. Burn them while they are dry.
    4. Use the ash as salt, or add it early to greens to keep their colour.

    Chef Tips

    • Wither first, then burn. Green leaves smoulder rather than burn clean, and the source is specific that they are burnt while dry.
    • It is a salt substitute and it is also alkaline, so like ofuta it can go in early to hold the colour of boiled greens.
    • No species are named in the source. 'Various salty leaves' is the whole botanical guidance, which is a real limit on reproducing this outside West Nile.
    • Magadi is the related mineral salt, obtained from saline water at places like Kibiro on the northern shores of Lake Albert, and it probably takes its name from the salty Lake Magadi. It can replace salt and is used the same way.
    • Use it sparingly and taste as you go. Plant ash salts are alkaline as well as salty and they do not behave like sodium chloride.
    • It is women's work in the Lugbara account, from the collecting through to the burning, and that is worth recording as stated.
    • No quantities are documented. Nothing has been estimated.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Burning the leaves green.
    • Using it measure-for-measure in place of table salt.
    • Collecting leaves you cannot identify as edible.
    • Storing it damp, which cakes it solid.
    • Adding it at the end of cooking greens rather than the start.

    Health Benefits

    • Supplies potassium and trace minerals alongside its salinity.
    • Lower in sodium than mined or sea salt, depending on the plants used.
    • Alkaline, so it also helps greens keep their colour and some of their vitamins.
    • Made from gathered wild material with no processing inputs.

    Cultural Note

    Plant salts exist wherever people live far from a salt source, and West Nile is about as far inland as Uganda goes. That the Lugbara maintain both a plant salt and a trade route to mineral salt from Lake Albert says something about how much effort seasoning has historically cost.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Indefinitely, in a dry airtight container.

    Reheating: Not applicable.

    Serve with: Cowpea Leaf and Groundnut Soup, Kila Kila, Enya

That article prints 'wiver' where it means wither.
    • The same article describes magadi as a salty substance obtained from water such as at Kibiro on the northern shores of Lake Albert, notes that it probably got its name from the salty Lake Magadi, and states that it can replace salt.
