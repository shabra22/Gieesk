🥣 Millet Porridge with Tamarind

Bushera y'obulo

Tamarind boiled and hand-squeezed for its paste, the juice sieved and mixed with millet flour, then strained twice to leave the grit behind before it ever reaches the heat.

Millet porridge is a Ugandan breakfast and tamarind is what makes this version northern: sour, dark and sharp rather than sweet. The preparation is long relative to the cooking, and most of it is extraction and straining. The straining matters more than it looks — millet flour carries soil, and the two-stage strain that leaves the gritty residue in the pan is real traditional knowledge that almost never gets written down. A spoonful of peanut and sesame butter goes in at the end.

Ingredients

Equipment: Saucepan, Wooden spoon, Ladle, Sieve, Large jar, Kettle of hot water

  • 90 ml (6 tbsp) millet flour
  • 12 pieces tamarind
  • 1440 ml (6 cups) water, cold and hot
  • 15 ml (1 tbsp) peanut and sesame butter mix
  • Sugar or honey, to taste

Method

    Extracting the tamarind

    1. Wash your hands. Unshell the tamarind and rinse the shelled pods under cold water.
    2. Put them in the saucepan, add 1 cup of hot water and boil for 5 minutes.
    3. Turn off the heat and drain the hot tamarind juice into the jar.
    4. Add 1 cup of cold water onto the tamarind in the pan.
    5. Wash your hands again, then squash the boiled tamarind by hand to extract the paste.
    6. Pour that juice into the jar.
    7. Add another half cup of cold water, mix, and pour the juice into the jar.
    8. Discard the seeds and wash the saucepan clean.

    Removing the grit — do not skip this

    1. Sieve the juice back into the pan and add the millet flour.
    2. Mix well to a smooth consistency.
    3. Strain it carefully into the jar, leaving some residue behind in the pan.
    4. Add half a cup of cold water to the pan and drain it gently, again leaving the gritty bits behind.
    5. Wash the pan out and pour the jar contents back into the clean pan.

    Cooking

    1. Put the pan on the heat and start stirring immediately and continuously with the wooden spoon.
    2. Keep hot water in the kettle and add it little by little if it thickens too fast.
    3. Once it starts bubbling you can relax, and add sugar or honey to taste.
    4. Add the peanut and sesame butter and mix well.
    5. Turn off the heat and serve.

    Chef Tips

    • The double strain is the point. Millet flour carries soil, and leaving the residue in the pan twice — with a wash in between — is what gets it out. Almost nobody writes this down and it is why homemade millet porridge is sometimes gritty.
    • Boil the tamarind first, then squeeze it by hand in cold water. Hot pods do not give up their paste and unboiled ones will not break down.
    • Stir immediately and continuously from the moment it goes on the heat. Millet porridge lumps the instant you stop.
    • Keep the kettle hot. Adding cold water to a thickening porridge makes it seize.
    • No sugar quantity is given in the source. Sweeten at the bubbling stage and taste as you go.
    • The peanut and sesame butter goes in last and off the boil. It is what makes it a meal rather than a drink.
    • Wash your hands, twice, as the recipe says — you are squeezing the fruit by hand into something you will drink unheated at that stage.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Skipping the strains and getting grit.
    • Not stirring from the start.
    • Adding cold water to thin it.
    • Squeezing the tamarind before boiling it.
    • Adding the nut butter while it is still boiling hard.

    Health Benefits

    • Millet is high in iron, magnesium and B vitamins, and is naturally gluten-free.
    • Tamarind supplies vitamin C, potassium and organic acids.
    • Peanut and sesame butter add protein, calcium and healthy fats.
    • Contains peanuts and sesame.

    Cultural Note

    Millet porridge is a Ugandan breakfast across the whole country, but tamarind ties this one north, where the sour fruit is common and much used. It is also a weaning food and a food for the unwell, which is why the nut butter matters — it puts protein into something easy to swallow.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Refrigerate up to 2 days. It thickens as it sits.

    Reheating: Warm gently with a little hot water, stirring constantly.

    Serve with: Roasted Simsim, Mandazi, Chapati
