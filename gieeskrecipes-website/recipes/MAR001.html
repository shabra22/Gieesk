🍖 Mrouzia

Mrouzia — lamb confit with raisins, almonds and honey (مروزية)

Lamb rubbed with a ras el hanout blend, simmered until tender, then glazed in a thick sauce of plumped raisins and honey and crowned with golden almonds. A sweet-and-spiced Eid dish.

Mrouzia is the Moroccan meat dish that tastes like a sweet shop and a spice market at once. Lamb on the bone is rubbed with ras el hanout, pepper and oil and left in the fridge for 6 to 12 hours, then browned gently with onion, ginger, smen, a cinnamon stick and saffron and simmered for about two hours until tender. Soaked raisins, a little more ras el hanout and honey go in at the end and the sauce reduces to a glossy coat. Whole blanched almonds, roasted or fried until lightly golden, go over the top. The Taste of Maroc page ties it to Eid al-Adha and says the Fassi tradition made it with the leftover bones.

Ingredients

Equipment: Heavy pot or tagine, Baking sheet or frying pan for the almonds, Bowl for soaking the raisins

    Meat and marinade

    • 2.2 lbs trimmed lamb with bones (shanks, neck or shoulder), cut into large pieces
    • 1 1/2 tbsp ras el hanout for mrouzia, preferably freshly ground
    • 1/2 tsp ground pepper, a mix of black and white
    • 2 tbsp vegetable or olive oil

    For cooking the meat

    • 4 tbsp olive and vegetable oils, mixed
    • 2 tsp ground ginger
    • 1 medium yellow onion, finely chopped (optional)
    • 1 tsp smen
    • 1 tsp salt
    • 1 cinnamon stick
    • 1/2 tsp saffron threads

    For the raisins

    • 1.1 lbs dark raisins, preferably not too sweet
    • 1 tsp ras el hanout (mrouzia blend), freshly ground preferably
    • 4 tbsp clear honey, or icing sugar
    • 1 tsp ground cinnamon

    Garnish

    • 5.2 oz whole blanched almonds

    Method

    1. Rub the ras el hanout, pepper and the 2 tbsp oil into the lamb. Cover and refrigerate for 6 to 12 hours.
    2. Put the meat in the pot with the 4 tbsp mixed oils, ginger, onion if using, smen, salt, cinnamon stick, saffron and about 1/4 cup water. Set over medium heat for 10 minutes, stirring a couple of times to turn the meat.
    3. Add enough water to cover, then cover the pot and simmer over medium-low heat for about two hours, or until the meat is tender.
    4. While the meat cooks, cover the raisins with water and leave them to soak for at least an hour, then drain.
    5. Stir the drained raisins, the second teaspoon of ras el hanout, the honey and the ground cinnamon into the pot. Cook until the sauce is reduced and thickened, 20 to 30 minutes or so. No watery liquid should remain.
    6. For the almonds, either roast them spread on a baking sheet in a 325°F (160°C) oven for about 25 minutes, tossing a couple of times, or fry them and fish them out once they turn lightly golden.
    7. Serve the lamb with the raisin sauce spooned over and the almonds on top.

    Chef Tips

    • The page says everything in mrouzia is about time. Do not rush the two-hour simmer or the final reduction; both change the texture of the sauce.
    • Buy raisins that are not too sweet. The honey is doing the sweetening and sweet raisins push the dish into jam.
    • Fresh ras el hanout matters here. The source asks for a dedicated mrouzia blend, preferably freshly ground.
    • Doneness test from the source: the meat is tender and no watery liquid remains in the pot.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Serving it while the sauce is still thin. The raisin-honey sauce should be reduced and thick.
    • Leaving out the rest in the fridge. The 6 to 12 hours is when the rub works into the meat.
    • Burning the almonds. Take them out when lightly golden; they keep darkening in the hot oil.

    Health Benefits

    • Almonds and raisins add fibre, vitamin E and potassium alongside the protein from the lamb.
    • The dish is rich and sweet, so it suits a small festive portion more than daily eating.

    Cultural Note

    Taste of Maroc ties mrouzia to Eid al-Adha and says the Fassi tradition used the leftover bones. Before refrigeration, the page says, it was cooked confit-style until only oil remained and stored in clay jars. The page also describes a test for dryness: dip a cloth or braided cotton in the dish and light it; if it holds a flame, the water is gone. Its origin claim, which the page makes and we do not assert, runs through Andalusia, citing the 13th-century Anonymous Andalusian Cookbook and Ibn Razin in the 14th.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: The source says it keeps a couple of weeks in the fridge or months frozen.

    Reheating: Warm gently in a covered pot with a spoonful of water; keep the almonds aside and add them at the end so they stay crunchy.

    Serve with: Khobz, Mint tea
