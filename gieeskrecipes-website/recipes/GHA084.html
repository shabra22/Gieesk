🧈 Wagashi

Wagashi — wagashie, Fulani cheese

Milk warmed to fifty or seventy degrees and set with the juice of Calotropis leaves, the curd boiled about twenty minutes before draining.

Wagashi is the Fulani cheese of northern Ghana and the coagulant is what makes it extraordinary: not rennet but the juice of Calotropis procera, the apple of Sodom. Fresh milk is filtered and gently heated to between fifty and seventy degrees, the leaf or stem extract is stirred in, and heating continues until coagulation occurs. The curd is then boiled about twenty minutes before draining in a raffia basket or mesh strainer. It is shaped into balls or cakes, sometimes stained red outside with an extract of guinea-corn leaves, and it is usually sliced and fried before eating.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large pot, Thermometer, Raffia or plastic basket, or a mesh strainer, Frying pan, for serving

  • Fresh milk, filtered
  • Extract of Calotropis procera leaves or stems — the coagulant
  • Salt

Optional, for the traditional red exterior

  • Extract of guinea-corn leaves

No volumes or extract quantities are published. See the note.

    Method

    1. Filter the fresh milk.
    2. Heat it gently to between 50 and 70 degrees.
    3. Add the extract from Calotropis procera leaves or stems and stir thoroughly.
    4. Continue heating at the same or a slightly higher temperature until coagulation occurs.

    ENDPOINT: coagulation — the curd separating visibly from the whey.

    1. Boil the curd about 20 minutes.
    2. Drain in a raffia or plastic basket, or a mesh strainer.
    3. Shape into balls or rounded cakes.
    4. Optionally stain the outside red with an extract of guinea-corn leaves.
    5. Slice and fry before eating, with pepper.

    Chef Tips

    • Calotropis procera is the coagulant and it is a plant, not an animal enzyme — which makes wagashi one of the few traditional vegetarian cheeses anywhere.
    • Fifty to seventy degrees is the documented range before the extract goes in. Too hot and the proteins set badly.
    • Boil the curd about twenty minutes AFTER coagulation. That is not a European cheesemaking step and it is what gives wagashi its squeaky, frying-stable texture.
    • The red exterior comes from guinea-corn leaf extract and is cosmetic — it also makes the cheese recognisable in a market.
    • It is almost always sliced and fried before eating. Fresh wagashi is bland; fried, it is excellent.
    • Shelf life is short — one study gives three days — which is why it is fried and salted rather than aged.
    • Sources disagree completely on the coagulant: Calotropis extract in the two traditional accounts, 'plant extract or commercial rennet' in an optimisation study, and plain white vinegar in a modern adaptation. Vinegar will give you a cheese; it will not give you wagashi.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Using rennet or vinegar and calling it wagashi.
    • Overheating the milk before adding the extract.
    • Skipping the twenty-minute curd boil.
    • Expecting it to keep — it is a three-day cheese.
    • Eating it unfried.

    Cultural Note

    Wagashi is Fulani cheese, made by herding communities across the Sahel and brought into Ghana from the north, and its plant coagulant is a genuinely distinct cheesemaking tradition rather than a variation on a European one. Sliced, fried and dusted with pepper, it is sold at roadsides and is one of the few West African foods with no southern equivalent at all.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: 3 days, per the published study. Fry it or salt it heavily to extend that.

    Reheating: Slice and fry.

    Serve with: Fried and served with pepper, Waakye, Jollof Rice
