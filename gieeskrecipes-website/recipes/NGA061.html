🌰 Ukwa

Ukwa — African breadfruit porridge

Seven grams of edible potash to seven hundred grams of breadfruit — exactly one per cent — and the test is whether the seeds melt when pressed.

Ukwa is African breadfruit cooked to a porridge and it is notorious for how long it takes. The useful thing this recipe gives you is not a time, because it does not state one, but a tactile test that is better than a clock: the ukwa is done when the seeds melt when pressed. Breadfruit varies by age and dryness, so a timing would mislead. What is precisely given is the alkali — seven grams of edible potash to seven hundred grams of breadfruit, exactly one per cent by weight, which tenderises the seeds. A pressure cooker does it in thirty minutes.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large heavy pot, or a pressure cooker, Wooden spoon, Small bowl for the potash, Sieve

  • 700 g African breadfruit (ukwa)
  • 2 medium dry fish
  • 2 small stock cubes
  • 7 g edible potash
  • Palm oil, enough to colour
  • 5-6 fresh bitter leaves
  • Pepper and salt, to taste
  • Water, topped up as needed

Method

  1. Pick over the breadfruit and wash it well.
  2. Dissolve the 7 g of potash in a little water and sieve it, leaving the sediment behind.
  3. Put the breadfruit in a pot with water to cover and the sieved potash water.
  4. Cook till well done, topping up the water as needed. The ukwa is done when the seeds melt when pressed.
  5. Add the dry fish, the stock cubes, pepper and salt.
  6. Add palm oil, enough to colour it, and cook about 5 minutes.
  7. Add the fresh bitter leaves and simmer till the leaves wilt but are still green.
  8. Serve hot. It thickens considerably as it cools.

Chef Tips

  • Seeds melting when pressed is the test. Press one against the side of the pot with a spoon — if it gives way completely, you are there.
  • Seven grams to seven hundred is one per cent by weight. That is a precise figure from a named source and it is worth weighing rather than guessing.
  • Sieve the potash water and leave the sediment, as with every other alkali step in this collection.
  • No total cooking time is given anywhere on the source page, and that is honest rather than careless — breadfruit varies enormously with age and dryness.
  • A pressure cooker does it in 30 minutes, at pressure setting 1 and heat level 7 on the source's equipment.
  • Leaves wilted but still green is the finishing cue. Overcooked bitter leaf goes olive and tastes flat.
  • It gets thicker as it cools. Serve it a little looser than you want to eat it.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Cooking to a clock instead of to the press test.
  • Guessing the potash rather than weighing it.
  • Letting the pot run dry during the long cook.
  • Adding the bitter leaf early.
  • Judging the final consistency while it is piping hot.

Health Benefits

  • African breadfruit is high in protein, fibre, iron and potassium.
  • Very filling for its calorie count.
  • Bitter leaf is traditionally used for digestion.
  • Gluten-free.

Cultural Note

Ukwa is a seasonal Igbo dish and a well-loved one, and its reputation rests partly on how much patience it takes. The seed comes from Treculia africana, the African breadfruit tree, and it is sold both fresh and dried — dried takes considerably longer. That the best instruction anyone gives is press a seed and see whether it melts says something true about cooking an ingredient that varies this much.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 4 days refrigerated. It sets very firm.

Reheating: Low heat with plenty of water to loosen it back to a porridge.

Serve with: On its own, as a porridge, Boiled yam, Cold palm wine
