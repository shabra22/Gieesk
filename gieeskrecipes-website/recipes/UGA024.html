🍃 Sombe

Sombe — Isombe

Cassava leaves plucked young, pounded in a mortar and boiled two and a half hours with a great deal of green pepper, garlic and a cup and a half of palm oil until the oil darkens.

Sombe is pounded cassava leaves, and it belongs to the Bakonzo of the Rwenzori as much as to eastern Congo, Rwanda and Burundi — calling it simply Ugandan flattens a genuinely cross-border dish. It takes a long time and it takes a lot of palm oil, and both of those are the dish rather than indulgences. The leaves have to be the top tender ones whose stalks snap when you break them, the pounding is done in a mortar, and it cooks for hours until the oil has gone dark.

Ingredients

Equipment: Mortar and pestle, or a blender, Large pan, Wooden spoon

    No fixed weights — this is cooked by eye

    • A large quantity of young cassava leaves — the top tender ones
    • About 4 cloves garlic, or more for a bigger pot
    • A lot of green pepper, grated or finely diced
    • Tomatoes, chopped
    • Onions, chopped
    • 355 ml (about 1 1/2 cups) palm oil
    • Water, to cover
    • Salt

    Method

    1. Pluck young cassava leaves — the top tender ones, whose stalks snap when you break them. Take only two or three from each plant and move on, rather than stripping one.
    2. Strip the leaves from their stalks one by one and put them in a mortar.
    3. Pound the leaves. A blender works too.
    4. Put the pounded leaves in a large pan with enough water and salt, and put it on the heat.
    5. Grate or finely dice the green pepper, crush and chop the garlic, and chop the onions and tomatoes.
    6. While the sombe boils, add the vegetables one by one. There is no order that matters, as long as they all go in.
    7. Add the palm oil — about a cup and a half, and a generous amount is the point.
    8. Keep it boiling. It should take two and a half hours or more to cook.
    9. Keep stirring to work the flavours through.
    10. Once all the vegetables are tender, let it simmer 30 minutes or more, until the water has reduced and the palm oil has turned a darkish colour.
    11. Take it off the heat, let it cool a little, and serve.

    Chef Tips

    • The top tender leaves whose stalks snap are the ones you want. Older leaves stay fibrous through hours of cooking.
    • Pluck two or three leaves from a plant and move to the next. Stripping one plant kills it.
    • Green pepper, garlic and onion in quantity is the secret, and a lot of green pepper specifically. This is not a lightly seasoned dish.
    • Sombe is not sombe without palm oil, and a cup and a half for a pot this size is correct. Less makes it thin; you can reduce it, but you are making something else.
    • Two and a half hours minimum, and the darkening of the palm oil is the doneness cue rather than a clock.
    • The published version is honest that it is not a detailed recipe but a rough estimate from knowledge passed down, so treat the quantities as a starting point.
    • It is good on its own as well as with kalo, matooke or sweet potatoes.
    • Cassava leaves must be cooked thoroughly. Raw and undercooked cassava leaf is not safe, and this is one reason the long boil is not negotiable.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Using older, tougher leaves.
    • Under-pounding them.
    • Being sparing with the palm oil.
    • Cutting the cooking time — both for taste and for safety.
    • Taking it off before the oil has darkened.

    Health Benefits

    • Cassava leaves are unusually high in protein for a leaf, and rich in iron, calcium and vitamins A and C.
    • Palm oil is high in vitamin E and carotenoids, and it is also most of the calories here.
    • Very high in fibre.
    • Cassava leaves must be thoroughly cooked to be safe to eat, which the long boil takes care of.

    Cultural Note

    Pounding the leaves was a childhood chore in households that ate this, and the labour is part of why it reads as substantial food rather than a vegetable side. It crosses four national borders in the Great Lakes region under variations of the same name, which is a better description of that part of Africa than any map.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days. It is better the next day.

    Reheating: Warm gently — it is already well cooked.

    Serve with: Matooke, Posho, Sweet potatoes
