🫖 Shaah Bigays

Shaah Bigays — Shaah Rinji

Black tea boiled with a great deal of cardamom, cloves, cinnamon and smashed fresh ginger, sweetened in the pot and steeped off the heat — the tea that comes after a heavy meal, with no milk in it.

Somali tea comes in two forms and the names say which is which: shaah cadays is the milk one, from a word meaning to whiten, and shaah bigays — also called shaah rinji, depending on where in the country you are from — is the black one. This is the black one, and it is what gets poured after a heavy meal rather than at breakfast. Cardamom is the whole point: by one Somali standard the tea should carry so much of it that it makes you sneeze. Black pepper, which Indian chai leans on, is usually left out.

Ingredients

Equipment: Medium pot, Mortar and pestle, Fine mesh strainer, Thermos or teapot

  • 1.4 litres (6 cups) water
  • 4 to 14 green cardamom pods, roughly ground — see the tips
  • 2 whole cloves
  • 1 cinnamon stick, 8 to 10 cm
  • 90 g fresh ginger, unpeeled, sliced 5 mm thick and smashed
  • 80 g (1/3 cup) granulated sugar, or to taste
  • 1 tbsp loose leaf black tea, or 2 tea bags

Method

  1. Bring the water to a boil in a medium pot over medium-high heat.
  2. Grind the cloves, cardamom and cinnamon to a rough grind and add them to the boiling water.
  3. Slice the ginger into 5 mm pieces and smash each piece, then add it with the sugar and the tea.
  4. Bring back to the boil.
  5. Turn off the heat and let it steep on the burner for 10 to 15 minutes.
  6. Strain through a fine mesh sieve into a thermos or teapot and serve.

Chef Tips

  • The cardamom quantity is where the sources genuinely part company: 4 pods to 6 cups at one end, 14 pods to 4 1/2 cups at the other — a roughly fivefold difference between two good Somali sources. Start at 6 to 8 pods for 6 cups and push it up if you want the sneeze.
  • Steeping off the heat rather than simmering keeps the spice bright. Boiled hard and long, cardamom goes soapy.
  • Leave the black pepper out. Somali shaah usually omits it, and that is one of the things separating it from Indian chai.
  • Smash the ginger rather than mincing it — you want it to give up flavour and then come out cleanly in the strainer.
  • Unpeeled ginger is correct. The skin carries flavour and it is all getting strained out anyway.
  • For shaah cadays instead, the milk version, the milk goes in after the spices have done their work rather than being boiled with them from the start.
  • Many elders drink it black by preference, and serving the milk separately in a jug lets everyone choose.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Being timid with the cardamom. This is a tea defined by it.
  • Boiling it for a long time instead of steeping it off the heat.
  • Adding black pepper.
  • Adding milk to the pot and calling it bigays — with milk in it, it is cadays.
  • Leaving the tea leaves in while it sits, which turns it tannic and grey.

Health Benefits

  • Cardamom, ginger, cinnamon and cloves are all traditionally taken for digestion, which is why this comes after a heavy meal.
  • Black tea supplies flavonoids.
  • No dairy at all. The sugar is the only real calorie in it, and it is adjustable.

Cultural Note

The naming is worth getting right because Somalis do: cadays comes from whitening, so shaah cadays is the tea with milk, and bigays and rinji are the two regional names for the black one. Somali tea is a hospitality obligation more than a beverage — it arrives whether you asked for it or not, and every household's version tastes slightly different from every other's.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Keeps hot in a thermos most of a day. Refrigerate up to 2 days and drink it cold.

Reheating: Warm gently. Do not re-boil.

Serve with: Buskud, Xalwo, Sambuusa
