🍚 Iwuk Edesi

Iwuk edesi — native jollof, palm oil rice

Native jollof — two cups of rice cooked in a litre of palm-oil stock with smoked shrimp and dried fish, and a bunch of chopped ugwu stirred through for the last five minutes.

Iwuk edesi is palm oil rice, and the author describes it as the way our grandmothers cooked one-pot rice dishes back in the day. It has nothing in common with party jollof except that it is rice in a sauce: no tomato paste, no curry powder, no foil lid. A cooking spoon of palm oil, ground tomatoes and pepper, half a cup of smoked shrimp, a cup of dried fish and the beef stock make the cooking liquid, the washed rice goes in to absorb it, and a medium bunch of chopped ikong or ugwu goes through at the end.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large pot with a lid, Wooden spoon, Colander

  • 2 cups rice
  • 1 medium bunch ikong or ugwu, chopped
  • 6 pieces of beef
  • 1/2 cup smoked shrimp
  • 1 cup dried fish
  • 2 cooking spoons tomatoes and pepper, ground
  • 1/2 tsp dried pepper
  • 1 cooking spoon palm oil
  • 1 tbsp ground crayfish
  • 1 crayfish seasoning tablet
  • 1 beef seasoning cube
  • 1 litre water
  • Salt to taste

Used in the method but not listed in the original

  • 1 onion, half for the beef and half for frying

Method

  1. Season and boil the beef with the beef seasoning, half an onion, salt and the half teaspoon of dried pepper. Keep the stock.
  2. Wash the rice and set it aside.
  3. Heat the palm oil and fry the remaining onion.
  4. Add the ground tomatoes and pepper, the shrimps, the dried fish, the beef, the ground crayfish, the remaining seasoning and salt to taste.
  5. Pour in the water and the available meat stock, and boil 10 minutes.
  6. Add the washed rice and cook until it is almost soft and dry.
  7. Add the chopped vegetable and cook about 5 minutes.
  8. Serve hot.

Chef Tips

  • Almost soft and dry is the cue for the rice, and the 'almost' matters — it finishes in the five minutes with the greens.
  • A litre of water plus the beef stock to two cups of rice. That is generous, because the dried fish and shrimp absorb a good deal.
  • One cooking spoon of palm oil only. This is not a heavy dish despite being called palm oil rice — the oil is for flavour and colour.
  • Fry the onion in the palm oil before anything else. It is the base of the whole sauce.
  • Ugwu or ikong goes in at the very end, chopped. Five minutes and no more.
  • No tomato paste, no curry powder, no thyme. Those belong to party jollof and would make this a different dish entirely.
  • Onion is used twice in the method and appears nowhere in the ingredient list. One bulb, split in half, is what the method implies.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Cooking the rice fully before the greens go in.
  • Using more palm oil, which makes it greasy rather than flavoured.
  • Adding jollof seasonings.
  • Skipping the fried onion base.
  • Overcooking the ugwu to a grey mush.

Health Benefits

  • Red palm oil supplies carotenoids and vitamin E.
  • Ugwu adds iron, calcium and fibre to a rice dish.
  • Smoked shrimp and dried fish give lean protein, iodine and calcium.
  • A complete one-pot meal.

Cultural Note

Native jollof is the honest name for this: rice cooked in palm oil with whatever smoked and dried seafood the household had, which is what rice dishes looked like before tomato paste and curry powder arrived. It is called iwuk edesi among the Efik, and versions of the same idea — concoction rice, palm oil rice — turn up across southern Nigeria under local names.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 3 days refrigerated.

Reheating: Steam or microwave with a splash of water.

Serve with: Dodo (fried plantain), Moin Moin, Fried fish
