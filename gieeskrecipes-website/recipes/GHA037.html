🌽 Ga Kenkey

Komi — Ga kenkey, dokono

Three cups of cornmeal soured three days, half of it cooked into aflata and recombined, wrapped in corn husks and steamed an hour.

Kenkey is Ghana's great fermented staple and this is the Ga version — salted, wrapped in dried corn husks, shaped into balls. Three cups of white stone-ground cornmeal are mixed with about two and a half cups of warm water and left in a warm place, loosely covered, stirred well every day. Half the soured dough is then cooked with two cups of water and a teaspoon of salt for about ten minutes into a thick paste called aflata, the raw half is stirred back in, and the balls are wrapped in softened husks and steamed about an hour. The fermentation endpoint comes from the research literature, not from the recipe.

Ingredients

Equipment: Nonreactive glass or ceramic container, Heavy wooden spoon or paddle, Steamer insert and a tall pot, Bowl of water, for wetting the hands

  • 3 cups white stone-ground cornmeal — finely ground, not degerminated, not masa harina
  • About 2 1/2 cups warm water, plus more if the dough seems very dry
  • 1 tbsp corn starch (optional, for a smoother dough)

For the aflata

  • 2 cups water
  • 1 tsp salt, or less

For wrapping

  • Dried corn husks, softened in water

Method

  1. Mix the cornmeal and the optional corn starch with the warm water in a nonreactive glass or ceramic container.
  2. Keep it in a warm place, loosely covered, and stir well every day, removing any mould that forms on top or on the sides.
  3. Ferment about 3 days in a cool kitchen — longer means more sour.

ENDPOINT, from the research literature rather than this recipe: the dough is ready when it smells distinctly sour. Processors also watch for a change in colour. Dough fermented only 24 hours does not give a good-textured product, and three days is about the maximum.

  1. Make the aflata: cook 2 cups of water, the salt and HALF the dough over medium heat about 10 minutes, stirring constantly with a heavy wooden spoon. It thickens in about 5 minutes and is quite thick by 10.
  2. Remove from the heat and stir in the uncooked half.
  3. Wet your hands and shape into 3 or 4 good-sized balls, tossing and turning each in one hand.
  4. Wrap each ball in softened corn husks, overlapping at least a quarter inch over the previous husk, then twist the narrow ends together tightly and push the twisted end into the ball.
  5. Steam on a steamer insert about an hour.
  6. Let the balls cool slightly before unwrapping.

Chef Tips

  • The fermentation cue is NOT in this recipe and has to come from elsewhere. A survey of 138 kenkey processors found the readiness indicator was a SOUR SMELL for 63.8 per cent of them, and a change in colour for 34.1 per cent. That is the test to use.
  • Three days is about the maximum. The Ghanaian literature is explicit that dough fermented only 24 hours does not give a good-textured product, and that three days is the ceiling.
  • Stir every day and scrape off any mould. If you stir daily there usually is none — the stirring is what prevents it.
  • Corn husks, not foil or plastic. Her reasoning is worth having: foil does not let the balls steam properly, you lose the delicate corn husk flavour, and she worries about material transferring into the dough.
  • The aflata step — cooking half and folding in the raw half — is what gives kenkey its particular texture. Cooking all of it would give you banku.
  • White stone-ground cornmeal, finely ground, not degerminated, and definitely not masa harina. The source rules masa harina out explicitly.
  • She names and rejects the shortcut: adding a teaspoon of vinegar to unfermented dough to skip the wait. Her verdict is that it is cheating, and she is right — the flavour is not the same.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Using masa harina.
  • Stopping at 24 hours, which gives poor texture.
  • Going well past three days.
  • Cooking all the dough instead of half.
  • Wrapping in foil instead of corn husks.

Cultural Note

Kenkey is sold wrapped in its husks from basins all over Accra and eaten with fried fish and shito in the hand, standing up. The Ga and Fante versions are different foods that share a name — salted and husk-wrapped against unsalted and plantain-leaf-wrapped — and people have firm views about which is correct depending on where they are from.

Storage & Serving

Storage: 3 to 4 days, per the literature on Ga kenkey. Refrigerate or freeze and thaw — her own note is that they are best warm.

Reheating: Re-steam in the husks, or microwave wrapped in damp paper.

Serve with: Shito, Fried fish, Fante Fante
