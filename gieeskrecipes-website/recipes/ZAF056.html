🥜 Tshidzimba

Tshidzimba

Samp and groundnuts boiled an hour and a half, whole peanuts in for another hour, and ground peanuts stirred through at the end — the Venda dish where the nut is not a garnish but the body of the thing.

Tshidzimba is Vhembe food, and it is built on the groundnut in a way that almost nothing else in South African cooking is. The staging is what makes it: dried groundnuts and samp from the start, whole peanuts added when the samp is already halfway soft, and pounded peanuts last, where they break down into a creamy body that binds everything. That three-stage handling comes from Venda practice, and it is the detail the modern cookbook versions leave out.

Ingredients

Equipment: Two bowls, for the overnight soaks, Large heavy pot, Mortar and pestle, or a food processor, for pounding the peanuts

    Soak overnight, separately

    • 250 ml (1 cup) samp (thuvhule)
    • 125 ml (1/2 cup) dried sugar beans or groundnuts (phonda)

    Added during cooking

    • 1.5 litres water, plus more as needed
    • 125 ml (1/2 cup) raw whole peanuts (nduhu)
    • 125 ml (1/2 cup) raw peanuts, pounded to a coarse powder
    • 5 ml (1 tsp) salt, or to taste

    Method

    1. Rinse the samp and the beans separately and soak them overnight in separate bowls. Drain and discard the water.
    2. Put the drained samp and beans in a large pot with the 1.5 litres of water. Bring to the boil, then reduce the heat and simmer for 1 1/2 hours, topping up with water so it does not stick.
    3. Add the whole raw peanuts and continue simmering for another hour. The samp should be softening and the nuts should still have some bite.
    4. Pound the remaining peanuts to a coarse powder in a mortar, or pulse them in a processor — not to a paste.
    5. Stir in the pounded peanuts and the salt.
    6. Stir well and simmer a further 30 to 45 minutes, until the ground nuts have thickened the dish into something creamy.
    7. Serve hot.

    Chef Tips

    • The quantities here are scaled from published cookbook versions; the three-stage nut method — dried groundnuts from the start, whole peanuts at the halfway mark, pounded peanuts last — comes from a Venda source that gives no amounts. It is the better method and worth following.
    • Raw peanuts, not roasted. Roasting them gives a nuttier, drier dish; boiled raw, they soften and cream into the body of it, which is what tshidzimba is meant to do.
    • Pound, do not blend to butter. You want a coarse powder that thickens the pot, not a paste that turns it into peanut soup.
    • Traditionally this is made with whole maize kernels rather than samp, and with mbambara nuts as well as peanuts. Samp is the practical modern version and is what every published recipe uses.
    • Three hours plus. This is not a dish you start at six for supper at seven.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Adding all the peanuts at once, which loses the whole point of the staging.
    • Using roasted or salted peanuts.
    • Blending the ground peanuts to a smooth butter.
    • Letting it catch on the base — top up the water and stir.
    • Skipping the overnight soak.

    Health Benefits

    • Samp, beans and groundnuts together give a complete plant protein.
    • Groundnuts add healthy monounsaturated fat, niacin and vitamin E.
    • Very high in fibre and satisfying enough to be a meal on its own.
    • Contains peanuts — not suitable for anyone with a nut allergy.

    Cultural Note

    Venda food is barely documented in writing, and tshidzimba is one of the few dishes that has made it into a published cookbook. The groundnut runs through the whole of Limpopo cooking — in the greens, in the porridges, in this — and it is the clearest marker separating the food of the north from the maize-and-meat cooking further south.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days.

    Reheating: Warm with a splash of water. The peanuts thicken further overnight.

    Serve with: Beef stew, Mokhuse, Delele (okra)
