🍆 Fried Ntula with Mukene Powder

Ntula

Fourteen bitter garden eggs cooked down with tomato and ginger, finished with a cup of ground dried silverfish and a slurry of soy sauce and maize flour to bind it.

Ntula are the small African garden eggs, and calling them eggplants would be wrong — an eggplant in Luganda is bilinganya, which is a different vegetable. There are two kinds of ntula: the green, slightly bitter ones and the white, bland ones. The green are the ones to use here, because the bitterness is what gives the dish character. The mukene powder — dried Lake Victoria silverfish, ground — brings a salty depth that does the work anchovy does elsewhere.

Ingredients

Equipment: Cooking pan with a lid, Small bowl, Grinder, for the mukene

  • 14 ntula (green bitter garden eggs), chopped
  • 5 tomatoes, chopped
  • 240 ml (1 cup) ground mukene (dried silverfish)
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 5 ml (1 tsp) fresh ginger, finely chopped
  • 30 ml (2 tbsp) cooking oil

The binder

  • 30 ml (2 tbsp) maize flour
  • 45 ml (3 tbsp) soy sauce
  • 45 ml (3 tbsp) water
  • Salt

Method

  1. Pour the cooking oil into a pan. Add salt and the ginger and stir for one minute.
  2. Add the onions and tomatoes and stir for a minute.
  3. Cover the pan and let the tomatoes cook for five to eight minutes, until soft and tender.
  4. Add the ntula, cover, and cook for ten minutes, stirring occasionally.
  5. Stir in the mukene powder.
  6. In a separate bowl, dilute the soy sauce with the 3 tbsp of water, add the maize flour and mix to a smooth slurry.
  7. Pour the slurry into the pan while stirring.
  8. Keep stirring for a minute, then take it off the heat and serve.

Chef Tips

  • Use the green bitter ntula, not the white bland ones. The bitterness adds character, which is the reason to cook them at all — the white ones are for stews where you want a blank canvas.
  • Check the skins for holes before you cook. A hole means something has been inside, and you will not know until you bite it.
  • The maize flour slurry is what binds the dish. Add it as a slurry and never dry, or it will sit in lumps.
  • Ntula are not eggplants. The Luganda word for eggplant is bilinganya and it is a different vegetable — worth knowing if you are shopping.
  • If you have no mukene powder, fish sauce does the same job.
  • The dish can be made without the mukene, but it is thinner. It was a last-minute addition in the original and it improved it.
  • Let the tomatoes get properly soft before the ntula go in.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Using the white bland variety and wondering where the flavour went.
  • Not checking for holes in the skins.
  • Adding the maize flour dry.
  • Adding the ntula to underdone tomatoes.
  • Overcooking so they collapse entirely.

Health Benefits

  • Garden eggs are high in fibre and low in calories, with useful amounts of potassium and folate.
  • Dried mukene is an excellent source of calcium — the whole fish is eaten, bones and all — plus protein and omega-3.
  • Two tablespoons of oil for four servings.
  • Contains fish.

Cultural Note

Ntula are common in central Uganda and go into soups and stews, and the bitter variety is one of those foods that divides people along generational lines. Mukene powder is a good example of Ugandan resourcefulness with a cheap lake fish that had a poor reputation for years and turns out to be one of the most nutritious things in the market.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Refrigerate up to 2 days.

Reheating: Warm gently in a pan.

Serve with: Posho, Matooke, Rice
