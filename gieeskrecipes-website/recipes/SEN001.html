Ceebu jën — thiéboudienne, riz au poisson

Senegal's national dish and, in its author's words, the one dish that unites all Senegalese — the rice finishes in the fish broth and the crust from the pot bottom is served in its own bowl.

200 minTotal time
45 minPrep
125 minCook
10Servings
640Calories
HardDifficulty

A whole five-pound thioff or grouper is cut into seven steaks, each slit twice and stuffed with rof — a pounded paste of parsley, garlic, onion and scallion. The stew base is onions, green pepper and two cups of tomato paste cooked down on low until the oil separates and rises to the surface and the paste has turned a dark orange. Fish, hibiscus and scotch bonnets poach in it; then guedj and yeet, the fermented fish and shellfish, go in for ten minutes. The vegetables follow, then two cups of broken rice are steamed over the broth in a cheesecloth-lined colander before being stirred into it to finish. Serves ten.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large heavy pot, Mortar and pestle, or a blender, Colander and cheesecloth, Large serving platter and a separate bowl

    Fish and marinade

    • 1 large whole thioff or grouper, about 5 lb, scaled and gutted, cut into 7 steaks about 1 1/2 in thick, head and tail reserved
    • 1 cup rof

    Stew base

    • 1/2 cup vegetable oil
    • Salt
    • 2 white onions, chopped
    • 1 green bell pepper, seeded and chopped
    • 2 cups tomato paste
    • 5 cups water
    • 1 cup dried white hibiscus flowers (optional)
    • 2 whole scotch bonnet peppers
    • Freshly ground black pepper
    • 2 palm-sized pieces guedj — dried, salted, smoked fish
    • 2 pieces yeet, rinsed — dried, fermented shellfish

    or substitute 1/4 to 1/2 cup Vietnamese or Thai fish sauce, to taste

      Vegetables

      • 1/2 head green cabbage, cut into 3 wedges
      • 1 turnip, peeled and cut into thick wedges
      • 1 globe eggplant, halved lengthwise
      • 1 small butternut squash, peeled, seeded and cut into large chunks
      • 1 yuca, 4 to 5 in, peeled and cut into large chunks
      • 2 carrots, peeled and cut into large chunks
      • 2 bitter eggplants (optional)
      • 1/4 lb small whole okra, trimmed

      To finish

      • 1 handful shelled tamarind pods, or 1 tbsp tamarind paste
      • 2 cups broken white rice, or jasmine or basmati, washed and drained
      • 2 limes, cut into wedges

      Method

      1. Blend or pound the rof coarse.
      2. Cut two 2-inch slits in each fish steak and fill each with about 1 teaspoon of rof. Coat the fish, including the head and tail, and refrigerate.
      3. Heat the oil over medium-high. Add two pinches of salt, the onions, the green pepper and the tomato paste.
      4. Lower the heat to low and cook 10 to 15 minutes, stirring often and adding 1 to 2 tablespoons of water if needed — until the oil separates and rises to the surface and the tomato paste has turned a dark orange.
      5. Add the 5 cups of water, bring to the boil, reduce, and simmer about 30 minutes.
      6. Add the fish, head and tail, the hibiscus, the scotch bonnets and a pinch of black pepper. Cook uncovered over medium about 15 minutes, until the fish is cooked through.
      7. Remove the fish to a bowl, cover and keep warm.
      8. Add the guedj and yeet, or the fish sauce. Partially cover and simmer 10 minutes.
      9. Add the cabbage, turnip, eggplant, squash, yuca, carrots and bitter eggplants. Return to the boil, season, reduce and simmer 20 minutes, until tender.
      10. Add the okra and cook 10 minutes more.
      11. Move the vegetables to the fish bowl with a few ladles of broth and the tamarind.
      12. Set a cheesecloth-lined colander of rice over the simmering broth, cover, and steam 10 to 15 minutes.
      13. Stir the rice into the broth, bring to the boil, lower to low, skim the excess oil and adjust the broth so it just covers the rice. Cover tightly and cook about 20 minutes, until the rice is tender and the liquid is absorbed.
      14. Mound the rice on a platter with the fish and vegetables in the centre and lime wedges around.
      15. Scrape the crust from the bottom of the pot and serve it in a separate bowl.

      Chef Tips

      • The tomato paste stage is the dish. Two cups of paste cooked on low until the oil separates and rises and the colour turns dark orange is what makes this red rather than merely tomatoey — rush it and you get a raw, sharp broth.
      • Steam the rice over the broth before stirring it in. That two-stage treatment is why the grains stay separate in a dish with this much liquid.
      • Adjust the broth so it JUST covers the rice before the final twenty minutes. More than that and it goes to porridge.
      • The crust from the pot bottom — the khoukhou — is served in its own bowl and is the prized part. Scrape it; do not soak the pot.
      • Guedj and yeet are the fermented backbone. If you cannot get them, the author's own substitution is a quarter to half a cup of Vietnamese or Thai fish sauce, which is a fair swap for the fermented note.
      • Broken rice, not whole grain, is traditional and it matters: the broken grains absorb the broth faster and bind the dish.
      • The author notes this version is more traditional than the one in his earlier book, so where his two printings disagree, this is the one he stands behind.

      Common Mistakes to Avoid

      • Under-cooking the tomato paste, so the oil never separates.
      • Adding raw rice straight to the broth without the steaming stage.
      • Too much broth at the final covered cook.
      • Leaving the fish in while the vegetables cook, which shreds it.
      • Washing the crust off the pot bottom.

      Health Benefits

      • Grouper is high in protein, selenium and vitamin B12 and low in saturated fat.
      • Eight or nine vegetables in one pot, which is unusual for any single dish.
      • Cooked tomato paste at this concentration is very high in lycopene.
      • Gluten-free and dairy-free.

      Cultural Note

      Ceebu jen was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2021, and the nomination file traces it to the fishing communities of Saint-Louis island. It is eaten from a shared bowl, by hand, with the left hand holding the bowl and the right doing the work — and there are rules: no raised knee, no dropped grains. Its author calls it the one dish that unites all Senegalese, which is a large claim he is entitled to make.

      Storage & Serving

      Storage: 3 days refrigerated, components separate. The rice dries out faster than the stew.

      Reheating: Steam the rice; warm the fish and vegetables gently in a little broth.

      Serve with: Lime wedges, Kaani, Attaya
