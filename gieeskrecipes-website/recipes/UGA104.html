🫘 Operete

Operete — ajira, kaiko

Beans with the skins slipped off and cooked down, with or without groundnut paste — the West Nile counterpart to firinda, and the same idea arrived at separately.

Operete, called ajira, and kaiko in the Terego dialect, is beans with the skin removed, cooked with or without groundnut paste. It is the same dish in kind as the western Ugandan firinda and the Batooro version that appears at every cultural function — skinned beans, no skins, a smooth result. Angarawa is the closely related Terego dish in which skinless beans are lightly fried or cooked with groundnut paste, and kila kila is the version that keeps the skins on.

Ingredients

Equipment: Bowl, for soaking, Saucepan, Masher

  • Dried beans
  • Water
  • Salt

Optional

  • Groundnut paste
  • Mundrokole

No quantities are documented

    Method

    1. Soak the dried beans overnight, or scald them in hot water and leave them at least two hours.
    2. Slip the skins off and discard them.
    3. Boil the skinned beans until soft.
    4. Cook them down, with groundnut paste stirred in or without it.
    5. Season and serve.

    Chef Tips

    • Soak overnight in cold water, or scald in hot water and leave two hours. Either works and the second is the shortcut.
    • The skins are the whole point of the dish. Slipping them off is tedious and it is what gives the smooth texture these beans are eaten for.
    • With or without groundnut paste are both correct — the source gives them as equal options rather than one as a variant.
    • Three related Lugbara bean dishes are worth distinguishing: operete or ajira has the skins off; angarawa or angaraba is skinless beans lightly fried or cooked with groundnut paste, in the Terego dialect; kila kila keeps the skins on and is cooked with groundnut paste, with its own variants osu olungulungu using uncrushed beans and angunduru.
    • The western Ugandan firinda, Firinda, is the same idea from a different community, and the Batooro use yellow kisombwa beans specifically because the skins come off more easily. That is a useful tip here too.
    • Firinda contains no oil at all, which is credited for its popularity, and operete can be made the same way.
    • No quantities are documented. Nothing here is estimated.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Skipping the skinning.
    • Cooking the beans before removing the skins, which makes them harder to slip.
    • Under-soaking.
    • Adding groundnut paste dry.
    • Mashing it while the beans are still firm.

    Health Benefits

    • Beans are high in plant protein, fibre, folate, iron and magnesium.
    • Removing the skins makes them considerably easier to digest.
    • Can be made with no oil at all.
    • Contains peanuts if the groundnut paste is used.

    Cultural Note

    Skinned beans turn up under a different name in nearly every Ugandan region — firinda in the west, operete in West Nile, madakwa in Bunyoro — and the labour of slipping the skins off is why they read as special-occasion food. In Tooro, receiving a pet name requires a function where firinda and millet are served for it to count.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days.

    Reheating: Warm gently with a little water.

    Serve with: Enya, Karo, Mutere
