🐟 Sauteed Mukene

Mukene — Lake Victoria silverfish

Dried silverfish soaked twenty minutes, then fried in a single tablespoon of oil until crisp and browned before five tomatoes and two onions go in to soften around them.

Mukene is the tiny dried silverfish of Lake Victoria — the Lake Victoria sardine — and it spent years with a bad reputation: the smell, the little bones, the bitter heads. It is also one of the cheapest and most nutritious things in a Ugandan market, eaten whole with the bones, and it needs almost nothing done to it. A tablespoon of oil for three cups of fish is not a typo. The order matters: crisp the fish first, then the vegetables.

Ingredients

Equipment: Bowl, for soaking, Saucepan with a lid, Colander

  • 720 ml (3 cups) dried mukene
  • 5 large ripe tomatoes, chopped
  • 2 small onions, chopped
  • 15 ml (1 tbsp) oil
  • Salt and pepper
  • Spring onions, thinly sliced, to garnish

Method

  1. Soak the dried mukene in water for about 20 minutes.
  2. Cut the tomatoes and onions and set them aside.
  3. Drain the mukene and wash it thoroughly.
  4. Put a saucepan on high heat and add the oil.
  5. Once the oil is hot, add the mukene. Let it cook until crisp, stirring constantly.
  6. Once it is crispy and browned but not burnt, add the onions and tomatoes.
  7. Mix well, cover the pan, add salt and pepper, and stir occasionally.
  8. Once the tomatoes have softened and mixed through the fish, take it off the heat.
  9. Garnish with thinly sliced spring onions and serve.

Chef Tips

  • Crisp the fish before the vegetables go in. Add the tomatoes early and the mukene steams instead of frying, which is where the fishy smell people complain about actually comes from.
  • Soak for twenty minutes and wash thoroughly. This is what takes the edge off, and it is not a step to skip.
  • One tablespoon of oil is correct. Mukene is full of flavour on its own and does not need much doing to it.
  • Browned but not burnt. The line is narrow and it is easy to cross while you are chopping.
  • Five tomatoes to three cups of fish looks like a lot. It is what makes this a dish rather than a garnish.
  • The bitter heads are part of it. If you strongly dislike them, they can be picked off before cooking, though nobody in Uganda bothers.
  • It is also excellent stirred through greens or a groundnut stew rather than served on its own.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Adding the tomatoes before the fish is crisp.
  • Cooking it dry without soaking first.
  • Using too much oil.
  • Leaving it to catch while you chop the onions.
  • Buying mukene that smells strongly ammoniac — that is old stock, not the normal smell.

Health Benefits

  • Eaten whole with the bones, so exceptionally high in calcium.
  • Very high in protein and in omega-3 fatty acids for the price.
  • A significant source of iron, zinc and vitamin B12 in Ugandan diets.
  • Cooked in a single tablespoon of oil.

Cultural Note

Mukene has been food for people with little money and, for years, food for livestock, which is where the stigma came from. Nutritionally it is one of the best things on a Ugandan market stall, and the reputational rescue of it has been a real project among Ugandan food writers over the last decade.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Refrigerate up to 3 days.

Reheating: Warm in a dry pan to bring back some crispness.

Serve with: Posho, Matooke, Sauteed Garlic Dodo
