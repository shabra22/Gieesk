Xalwo Muus — Halwa ya Mazu

Over-ripe bananas mashed into a pan and cooked down for three quarters of an hour with sugar, butter and cardamom until they turn dark and sticky — the Bravanese halwa, made from fruit too far gone to eat.

Known in Brava as halwa ya mazu, this is a thrift dish that became a delicacy. Twelve bananas nobody would eat raw go into a dry pan and reduce for half an hour before any sugar is added, by which point they have gone from pale mash to something dark and jam-like. Traditionally it was not portioned at all — it set as one large piece in a dish and everyone tore off what they wanted.

Ingredients

Equipment: Large non-stick pan, Wooden spoon

  • 12 very ripe bananas
  • 100 g (1/2 cup) granulated sugar
  • 60 ml (1/4 cup) melted unsalted butter
  • 1/4 tsp ground cardamom

To shape and coat, if you are not serving it from the dish

  • A handful of pecans or other nuts, for stuffing
  • Shredded desiccated coconut, sesame seeds, crushed nuts or sweetened cocoa powder, for rolling

Method

  1. Peel the bananas into a non-stick pan and mash them roughly.
  2. Cook over medium heat for 30 minutes, stirring every 5 minutes. The mash will darken and thicken considerably.
  3. Add the sugar and the melted butter.
  4. Cook for a further 10 minutes, stirring continuously now — with the sugar in, it will catch if you leave it.
  5. Add the cardamom and cook for 2 minutes more.
  6. Either spoon it into a dish and serve it as one piece, or let it cool enough to handle and roll it into balls.
  7. If shaping, press a nut into the centre of each ball, then roll them in coconut, sesame, crushed nuts or cocoa powder.

Chef Tips

  • The bananas must be genuinely over-ripe — black-skinned, the ones you would otherwise throw out. Firm bananas will not reduce to the right texture and you will end up adding sugar to compensate.
  • Thirty minutes before the sugar goes in is not negotiable. That is where the water cooks off and the flavour concentrates.
  • Once the sugar is in, stir constantly. Sugar plus a hot dry pan means it will scorch the moment your back is turned.
  • The balls-and-coating presentation is a modern refinement. Traditionally it was one large piece in a dish that people broke into, and that is still the better way to eat it.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Adding the sugar at the start, which stops the bananas reducing properly and gives a wet, oversweet halwa.
  • Cooking it on high heat to save time — it burns on the base long before it thickens.
  • Using bananas that are merely ripe, which gives a pale, bland result.
  • Trying to shape it while still hot. It needs to cool enough to hold.

Health Benefits

  • Bananas contribute potassium, vitamin B6 and fibre.
  • The sweetness comes largely from the fruit — half a cup of sugar across twelve bananas.
  • A use for fruit that would otherwise be wasted.

Cultural Note

Brava's kitchens dried bananas in the sun as well — that product was called mazalfoorno — and halwa ya mazu is the cooked-down version of the same instinct: nothing in a coastal household with a banana tree went to waste. It is eaten with the unsweetened Somali coffee that cuts through it.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Covered, at room temperature up to 5 days; refrigerated up to 2 weeks.

Reheating: Serve at room temperature. A few seconds of warmth loosens it if it has set very firm.

Serve with: Unsweetened Somali coffee, Shaah Cadays, Macsharo
