🍩 Gonja Plantain Donuts

Gonja donuts

Five ripe plantains mashed with allspice and vanilla, brought to a firm dough with flour, cut into rings, fried and dipped in a cooled sugar syrup with coconut on top.

These are what happens when a household harvests a bunch of gonja and cannot eat it all roasted — plantain in a donut, spiced with allspice, rung out with a bottle cap and dipped in syrup. Every bunch harvested is a different variety, gonja, bogoya, matooke or the sort that makes juice, and the sweet one ends up here. The syrup is boiled and then cooled before the donuts go in, which keeps it from soaking straight through.

Ingredients

Equipment: Bowl, Cookie cutter and a bottle cap, Frying pan, Small pan, for the syrup

  • 5 ripe plantains
  • 240 ml (1 cup) flour
  • 2.5 ml (1/2 tsp) vanilla
  • 60 ml (1/4 cup) milk
  • 2.5 ml (1/2 tsp) allspice
  • 2.5 ml (1/2 tsp) baking powder
  • Desiccated coconut, to finish
  • Oil, for frying

The syrup

  • 240 ml (1 cup) water
  • 120 ml (1/2 cup) sugar

Method

  1. Mash the plantains.
  2. Add the milk, vanilla and allspice and mix well.
  3. Mix the baking powder into the flour.
  4. Add the flour gradually until a firm dough forms.
  5. Spread the dough out with your hands to an even inch of thickness.
  6. Cut out rounds with a cookie cutter, then cut the holes with something smaller — a bottle cap does it.
  7. Fry until golden brown.

The syrup

  1. Put the cup of water and the half cup of sugar in a clean pan, bring to the boil, then take it off the heat and let the syrup cool.
  2. Dip the donuts in the cooled syrup and sprinkle with desiccated coconut.

Chef Tips

  • Cool the syrup before dipping. Hot syrup on a hot donut soaks straight through and you get a wet, heavy thing instead of a glazed one.
  • Add the flour gradually to a firm dough. Plantain varies in moisture and a fixed amount of flour will be wrong as often as right.
  • Spread the dough by hand, not with a rolling pin. It is too soft and sticky to roll.
  • An inch thick. Thinner and they fry into biscuits.
  • Allspice is the whole flavour here alongside the plantain, and half a teaspoon is enough to carry it.
  • Use properly ripe plantains. Underripe fruit gives a starchy, bland donut.
  • The coconut goes on while the syrup is still wet, or it will not stick.
  • Every harvested bunch is a different variety — gonja, bogoya, matooke or the juice kind — and it is the sweet gonja you want.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Dipping in hot syrup.
  • Adding all the flour at once.
  • Rolling the dough with a pin.
  • Cutting them too thin.
  • Adding the coconut after the syrup has dried.

Health Benefits

  • Ripe plantain supplies potassium, vitamin B6 and vitamin C.
  • Most of the sweetness comes from the fruit, with only half a cup of sugar in the syrup.
  • Desiccated coconut adds fibre and manganese.
  • Deep-fried and syrup-dipped — a dessert, not a snack to eat daily.

Cultural Note

Turning a glut into something else is a recurring habit in Ugandan cooking, and it is how a lot of Ugandan sweets exist at all — too many plantains, too many pineapples, too much fresh maize. The donut shape is borrowed, the fruit and the allspice are not.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Best the day they are made. 2 days in an airtight container.

Reheating: A warm oven for a few minutes, before the syrup goes on.

Serve with: Ugandan tea, Mandazi, Kabalagala
