🐟 Momoni

Momoni — momone, fermented salted fish

Fresh fish salted at about 22.5 per cent and fermented seven days at thirty-five degrees — the good endpoint smells of processing odour and sour milk, not sulphur.

Momoni is the fermented salted fish that gives Ghanaian stews their deepest savoury note, and it is published here as a documented process. The best endpoint comes from a 2024 study which found that the optimal sensory results used fresh fish, twenty-two and a half per cent salt and seven days of fermentation at thirty-five degrees — the same conditions as currently practised in Ghana. The cue is an odour: good momoni carries a processing odour with mild trimethylamine and a note of sour milk; bad momoni smells of high trimethylamine and rotten sulphur. The pH figures across five studies do not agree at all.

Ingredients

Equipment: Plastic bucket or fermentation vessel, A fly-free enclosure, Scales, Drying trays

  • Fresh fish — kingfish or jack mackerel are documented, gutted and cut into pieces
  • Salt, at about 22.5 per cent of the fish weight

Further salt at about three quarters of the initial weight, for the resalting route

    No household quantities are published. See the note.

      Method

      1. Gut and wash the fish and cut it into pieces.
      2. Leave overnight at room temperature in a fly-free enclosure.
      3. Salt at about 22.5 per cent of the fish weight.
      4. Place in a plastic bucket and leave undisturbed three days.

      Alternative documented route: on the third day remove and wash the fish, then resalt at about three quarters of the initial salt weight and ferment a further ten days.

      1. Ferment about seven days total at around 35 degrees for the optimised process.

      ENDPOINT, by smell: good momoni has a processing odour with mild trimethylamine and a note of sour milk. Reject it if it smells of high trimethylamine or rotten sulphur.

      1. Dry if a dried product is wanted.
      2. Use in small quantities as a savoury seasoning.

      Chef Tips

      • The endpoint is a smell and the distinction is specific: good momoni gives a processing odour with mild trimethylamine and a sour-milk note. Rotten sulphur or a strong fishy ammonia means it has gone wrong.
      • Fresh fish, not fish that has sat about. The 2024 study compared fresh fish against fish stored twenty-four hours unpreserved and found fresh gave the better result.
      • Twenty-two and a half per cent salt by fish weight is the optimised figure, and the study notes this matches what is already practised in Ghana.
      • Seven days at thirty-five degrees. That is the optimised combination; the older documented route is three days salted, then washing, resalting and ten more days.
      • A fly-free enclosure for the overnight stage. This is an open fermentation and insects are the main spoilage risk.
      • The pH literature does not agree — above 7, above 6, falling from 6.5 to 5.2, and 7.1 to 7.8 across four studies. Do not use pH as your test; use the smell.
      • Use it sparingly. A piece the size of a thumb seasons a whole pot, and more makes a stew inedible.

      Common Mistakes to Avoid

      • Using fish that is no longer fresh.
      • Under-salting, which lets spoilage organisms win.
      • Fermenting uncovered where flies can reach it.
      • Trusting a pH figure, given how widely the published values differ.
      • Using too much in a dish.

      Health Benefits

      • Very high in protein at 16.8 to 21.9 per cent in retail samples.
      • Fermentation raises free amino acids substantially, from 18 to 45 mg/g in one study.
      • A source of iodine and omega-3 fats.
      • Used in tiny quantities, so the very high salt contributes little per serving.

      Cultural Note

      Momoni is the ingredient that makes a Ghanaian stew taste Ghanaian, and it is also the one that empties a kitchen when the jar is opened. That one study concludes the fermentation is driven by the fish's own enzymes rather than by microbes is a genuinely interesting finding — it means momoni is closer to a fish sauce than to a yoghurt.

      Storage & Serving

      Storage: Months, given the salt content. Keep sealed and cool.

      Reheating: Not applicable. Used as a seasoning.

      Serve with: Abomu, Kontomire Stew, Palm Nut Soup
