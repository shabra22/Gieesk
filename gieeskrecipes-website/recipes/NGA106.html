🌿 Okoho with Bushmeat

Okoho — abahi

The extraction done properly — bark scratched off, the stem beaten with a pestle, the rope-like inner layer wrapped and soaked eight to ten minutes, then squeezed until the water goes thick and slippery.

This version has no quantities at all and the author says so, but it has the best description anywhere of how the okoho stalk is actually prepared — which is the only genuinely difficult part of the dish. The bark is scratched off gently with a knife, the stem laid on a clean surface and hit gently with a pestle to loosen it, and the rope-like inner layer removed and the woody stick discarded. That layer is wrapped, put in a bowl, covered with hot water for eight to ten minutes, and then squeezed in the bowl until the water becomes thick and slippery. The egusi balls here are called abahi.

Ingredients

Equipment: Sharp knife, Pestle, Cloth, for wrapping the layer, Bowl, Large pot

  • Okoho stick (stem)
  • Hot water, for the soak
  • Bushmeat — grasscutter, alligator, or smoked meat
  • Dried fish
  • Okpehe or locust beans
  • Dried pepper
  • Abahi — egusi balls
  • Seasoning cube
  • Water
  • Salt

The source states no quantities are provided

    Method

    1. Peel the bark off the okoho stem by scratching gently with a knife.
    2. Place the stem on a clean surface and hit it gently with a pestle to loosen it.
    3. Remove the rope-like inner layer and discard the woody stick.
    4. Wrap the layer, place it in a bowl, add hot water and let it sit 8 to 10 minutes.
    5. Squeeze it in the bowl until the water becomes thick and slippery. Reserve that liquid.
    6. Cook the bushmeat or smoked meat with the seasoning, salt and dried pepper until tender.
    7. Add the dried fish and the ground okpehe or locust beans.
    8. Form the abahi — egusi balls — and steam them in the broth until firm.
    9. Add the okoho liquid and simmer gently.
    10. Return everything, taste, and serve.

    Chef Tips

    • Scratch the bark off, do not cut it. You are after the layer underneath and a knife used hard will take that with it.
    • Beat the stem gently with a pestle. It loosens the fibres so the inner layer comes away in one rope rather than shreds.
    • Discard the woody stick once you have the inner layer. The stick itself does nothing.
    • Thick and slippery is the endpoint, and it is the best-stated cue for this dish anywhere. You will feel the water change as you squeeze.
    • Eight to ten minutes in hot water here, against fifteen in the other documented version. Both then squeeze, so judge by the liquid rather than the clock.
    • Okpehe is African mesquite seed, a different ferment from iru and from dawadawa — the other okoho recipe uses two varieties of locust bean instead.
    • Abahi is the Idoma name for the egusi balls, and it is worth knowing because it tells you they are a named component rather than an addition.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Cutting the bark rather than scratching it.
    • Skipping the pestle, so the inner layer shreds.
    • Soaking without wrapping the layer first.
    • Stopping the squeeze too early.
    • Boiling the sap rather than simmering it.

    Health Benefits

    • The okoho sap is a soluble mucilaginous fibre.
    • Melon seed in the abahi supplies protein and magnesium.
    • Very low in carbohydrate.
    • Fermented okpehe contributes B vitamins and glutamates.

    Cultural Note

    Okoho is eaten at Christmas in Idoma villages and the author frames it as family celebration food. Grasscutter — the cane rat — is the classic protein for it, which places the dish in a Middle Belt tradition of bushmeat cooking that is barely documented anywhere. The soup's thickener and its protein are both things you gather rather than buy.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: 3 days refrigerated.

    Reheating: Very low heat. The sap's thickening slackens with reheating.

    Serve with: Iyan, Eba or garri, Tuwo Shinkafa
