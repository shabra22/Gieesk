🌿 Ggobe Leaf Powder

Ggobe — dried cowpea leaf

Cowpea leaves steamed five minutes and then sun-dried for five days, so the season's greens keep as a powder to be reconstituted into sauce months later.

This is a preservation method rather than a meal: cowpea leaves are steamed briefly and then dried in the sun for five days, after which they keep indefinitely and can be crumbled into a groundnut sauce out of season. It is the same logic as smoked bamboo shoots or dried mukene — Uganda's greens are seasonal and its households are not refrigerated. The five-minute steam before drying is what stops the leaves going black and bitter.

Ingredients

Equipment: Steamer, or a pan with a lid, Large drying trays or mats, Mortar and pestle, or your hands

  • 300 g cowpea leaves
  • 300 ml salted water

To cook the finished powder into a sauce

  • 60 to 75 ml (4 to 5 tbsp) oil
  • 2 onions
  • 3 tomatoes
  • 250 g groundnut paste

Method

  1. Wash the cowpea leaves.
  2. Steam them for about 5 minutes.

The drying

  1. Spread them out and sun-dry for 5 days.
  2. Crumble or pound the dried leaves to a powder and store them airtight.

To cook

  1. Reconstitute the powder in the salted water.
  2. Add the oil, onions and tomatoes.
  3. Dissolve the groundnut paste in cold water and stir it in.
  4. Simmer until thickened, and serve.

Chef Tips

  • Steam five minutes before drying. It sets the colour and stops the leaves going black and bitter as they dry.
  • Five days of sun, spread thin. Heaped leaves rot in the middle while the top dries.
  • Bring them in overnight or when it rains. Rehydrated and re-dried leaves spoil.
  • Store the powder genuinely airtight. It will reabsorb moisture from humid air and go mouldy.
  • The source prints its leaf quantity as 300 leaves where its near-identical fresh recipe on the facing page reads 300 g. One of the two is a typo; the weight is given here because it is the one that makes sense, and the discrepancy is stated rather than hidden.
  • The powder goes into sauce with the same oil, onion, tomato and groundnut paste as the fresh version.
  • This is how a household eats greens in the dry season, and it is worth thinking of as a pantry item rather than a recipe.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Drying the leaves raw.
  • Heaping them on the drying tray.
  • Leaving them out in rain or overnight damp.
  • Storing the powder in anything that is not airtight.
  • Drying for a day or two and calling it done.

Health Benefits

  • Drying concentrates the leaves' iron, calcium and protein by weight.
  • Makes a seasonal green available year-round without refrigeration.
  • Some vitamin C is lost in drying; the minerals and fibre are not.
  • Nothing is added — the powder is leaves and nothing else.

Cultural Note

Preservation is the part of African cooking that gets least attention and does most of the work. Smoked bamboo shoots, sun-dried mukene, sun-dried nkejje pinned to sticks, muqmad, dried cowpea leaf — these are how a cuisine survives a dry season, and they are techniques rather than recipes.

Storage & Serving

Storage: The dried powder keeps indefinitely, airtight and dry.

Reheating: Cook the reconstituted sauce fresh each time.

Serve with: Posho, Matooke, Boiled Sweet Potatoes
