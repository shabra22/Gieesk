🥬 Jobyo and Dodo in Groundnut Sauce

Ejjobyo n'eddoodo

Bitter spider-plant leaves boiled thirty-five minutes before the amaranth joins them, the groundnut paste blended with their own hot cooking liquid, and half a litre of sour milk stirred in at the end — with no cooking oil at all.

This is a deliberately oil-free dish. The richness comes from three hundred grams of groundnut paste and half a litre of sour milk, and the flavour from the tomato and the two greens rather than from fat. Jobyo is the spider plant, bitter and with a medicinal reputation, and it wants a long boil — thirty-five minutes on its own before the softer dodo goes in for fifteen more. The groundnut paste is then blended using the hot vegetable liquid, which is the whole trick.

Ingredients

Equipment: Covered pan, Bowl, Wooden spoon

  • 300 g groundnut paste
  • 500 ml (1/2 l) sour milk, or plain yoghurt
  • 1 handful jobyo (spider plant leaves)
  • 3 handfuls dodo (amaranth)
  • 1 litre water
  • 10 ml (2 tsp) salt
  • 10 ml (2 tsp) ghee (optional)

No cooking oil at all

    Method

    1. Select fresh young jobyo leaves, wash them thoroughly, remove the stalks and any hard bits, and shred finely.
    2. Prepare the dodo the same way but keep the stalks unless they have hardened. Chop finely.
    3. Boil the jobyo in a covered pan with half a litre of cold salted water for 35 minutes.
    4. Add the dodo and continue boiling another 15 minutes.

    The sauce

    1. Blend the groundnut paste to smooth using the hot vegetable liquid from the pan. Gradually work in the remaining hot liquid until it is all used.
    2. Cook 10 minutes.
    3. Add the sour milk or yoghurt while stirring with a wooden spoon, until blended.
    4. Stir in the vegetables and the salt. Cover and simmer 5 minutes.
    5. Stir in the ghee before serving if you want it.
    6. Serve with kalo, posho, cassava, sweet potatoes, yams or matooke.

    Chef Tips

    • Blend the groundnut paste with the greens' own hot cooking liquid. Nothing is wasted and the sauce tastes of the vegetables rather than of water.
    • Thirty-five minutes for the jobyo before the dodo goes in. The two greens have completely different textures and cooking them together gives you one overcooked and one underdone.
    • No cooking oil, on purpose. This was written as household economy — cut the meat and the fat, lean on tomato, spice and sour milk instead — and it holds up on its own terms.
    • Young jobyo leaves. It is a bitter green to start with and older leaves are harsher.
    • Keep the dodo stalks unless they have gone hard. They are good.
    • Add the sour milk off a hard boil and stir as it goes in, or it will split.
    • The ghee at the end is optional and it is the only fat in the dish. Two teaspoons changes it noticeably.
    • Jobyo has a medicinal reputation in Uganda and is one of the indigenous greens that survives with little or no attention, which is part of why it is valued.

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • Boiling both greens together.
    • Blending the groundnut paste with fresh water.
    • Adding the sour milk to a rolling boil.
    • Using mature jobyo leaves.
    • Adding oil, which defeats the whole design of the dish.

    Health Benefits

    • Spider plant and amaranth leaves are both very high in vitamins A and C, iron and calcium.
    • Groundnut paste and sour milk supply protein without any added fat.
    • Cooked with no oil at all.
    • Contains peanuts and dairy.

    Cultural Note

    Sour milk in a savoury sauce is a habit Uganda shares with much of the cattle-keeping east of Africa, and it does work that cream does elsewhere. That a newspaper in 2011 was publishing oil-free recipes on grounds of household economy is a useful reminder that frugal cooking is not a modern wellness idea.

    Storage & Serving

    Storage: Refrigerate up to 2 days. Sour milk sauces do not keep long.

    Reheating: Warm gently without boiling, or the milk will split.

    Serve with: Posho, Boiled Sweet Potatoes, Matooke
