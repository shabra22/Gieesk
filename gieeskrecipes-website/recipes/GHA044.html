🥣 Tuo Zaafe

Tuo zaafi — TZ, tee zed

Learnt in Tamale — three cups of boiling water, three cups of millet flour and three to four cups of cold water, with the grain fermented or not.

Tuo zaafi is the northern staple, commonly called TZ and literally meaning very hot. This version was shown to the author in Tamale by Mrs Comfort Awu Akor and her daughter, alongside a sesame soup to go with it. The original was made from fonio; the published adaptation uses gluten-free millet flour. Three cups of water come to the boil; meanwhile two cups of cold water are mixed with two cups of millet flour; when the water boils the heat drops to medium and the slurry is quickly mixed in. On the fermentation question the source is admirably brief: the grain can be fermented or not.

Ingredients

Equipment: Heavy saucepan with a handle, Strong wooden spoon or paddle, Mixing bowl, Bowl of water, for shaping

  • 3 cups millet flour
  • 3 cups boiling water
  • 3-4 cups cold water

Method

  1. Heat 3 cups of water to a boil in a heavy saucepan with a handle.
  2. While the water is heating, mix 2 cups of cold water with 2 cups of millet flour.
  3. When the water boils, turn the heat to medium or medium-high and quickly mix in the millet-water mixture.
  4. Stir hard as it thickens.
  5. Work in the remaining flour and cold water as needed, alternating, until it is a smooth stiff mass.
  6. Beat it against the side of the pan until it pulls away cleanly.
  7. Wet your hands or a bowl and shape into smooth balls.
  8. Serve with ayoyo soup or a sesame soup.

Chef Tips

  • Make the cold slurry BEFORE the water boils. Flour dumped dry into boiling water lumps instantly and there is no recovering it.
  • Quickly is the operative word when the slurry goes in. Hesitate and the bottom sets before the top is mixed.
  • Turn the heat down to medium when the slurry goes in. Full boil plus thickening flour equals a scorched base.
  • Alternate the remaining flour and water rather than adding either all at once. That is how you reach a stiff smooth mass without lumps.
  • The original is made from fonio, not millet — the author used fonio in Tamale and published the millet adaptation because fonio is hard to find. Both are gluten-free.
  • On fermentation the source says only that the grain can be fermented or not, with no cue either way. Treat it as an unfermented preparation unless you have a method of your own.
  • Three cups of flour to six or seven cups of water in total is a wetter start than most swallows, because millet flour absorbs a great deal.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Adding dry flour to boiling water.
  • Leaving the heat at full boil.
  • Adding all the remaining water at once.
  • A flimsy spoon — this needs real force.
  • Assuming a fermentation step the source does not give.

Health Benefits

  • Millet is gluten-free and higher in iron and magnesium than maize.
  • Fonio, the original grain, is among the most nutritious African cereals and very low glycaemic.
  • No added fat or salt.
  • Rich in slowly digested complex carbohydrate.

Cultural Note

TZ is the food of northern Ghana the way banku is the food of the south, and the name — literally very hot — refers to how it is served rather than to chilli. That the clearest published version came from a woman in Tamale teaching a visitor, and is credited to her by name, makes it one of the better-documented northern recipes in existence.

Storage & Serving

Storage: Same day. It stiffens and dries quickly.

Reheating: Steam briefly. Do not microwave dry.

Serve with: Ayoyo Soup, Bito Soup, Sesame soup
